Language.

Language is a code too — the oldest one you run. This room is the sibling of Secret Squares: that room hides messages in little squares; this one looks at the code you already speak. Start with the surprise hiding in plain sight: English is a Germanic language. French and Spanish are its cousins, not its parents. Then spot the family resemblance in everyday words, borrow a phrasebook for six languages, visit the corner of the brain where language lives, and see exactly how a language does the same jobs as the codes in the room next door.

01 / THE FAMILY TREE

Every language has ancestors. English's are not the ones you'd guess.

Languages descend from earlier languages the way species do, splitting as their speakers spread out and their speech drifts apart. Group them by descent and you get a family tree. This is the Indo-European family — one of the world's great families, and the one English belongs to. Tap any branch to meet it. The gold-outlined path is English's own line home, and it does not pass through Latin or French:

Proto-Indo-European → Germanic → West Germanic → Anglo-Frisian → English. French and Spanish sit on the Romance branch — cousins of English, descended from Latin. The thousands of French words inside English arrived as borrowings, most of them after the Norman Conquest of 1066: a thick layer of loanwords poured over a Germanic language, the way a house can be repainted without being rebuilt.

English

The tree is a simplification, stated plainly: it shows the main branches linguists agree on and a sample of their languages, not all roughly 445 Indo-European languages. A few branches (Anatolian, Tocharian) are extinct. Where exactly the parent language was spoken, and when, is the mainstream reconstruction — the broad outline is settled science, the fine details are still argued about, and the linked articles say where.

The other great families

Indo-European is one family among many. These are some of the others — counts are approximate, because sources count languages and speakers differently, and the page says so rather than dressing guesses up as facts.

Sino-Tibetan

≈450 languages · ≈1.4 billion speakers (approximate)

Mandarin Chinese, Cantonese, Burmese, Tibetan. Mandarin has more native speakers than any other language on Earth. This family covers China and much of mainland Southeast Asia.

The biggest family by speakers

Niger-Congo

≈1,500 languages — the most of any family · ≈700 million speakers (approximate)

Swahili, Yoruba, Igbo, Zulu, Shona. One of its sub-branches, the Bantu languages, spread with farming peoples across most of sub-Saharan Africa.

The biggest family by languages

Afroasiatic

≈380 languages · roughly 400–500 million speakers (approximate)

Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa, and Ancient Egyptian. The family with the longest written record of all: Egyptian hieroglyphs in this family are more than 5,000 years old.

The oldest written record

Austronesian

≈1,200 languages · ≈300 million speakers (approximate)

Malay/Indonesian, Tagalog, Javanese, Hawaiian, Māori. Spread by boat across half the planet's surface: from Madagascar, off the coast of Africa, to Easter Island, off South America.

The widest spread by sea

Trans-New Guinea

≈480 languages · ≈3.5 million speakers (approximate)

Enga, Melpa, Dani, and hundreds of highland languages of New Guinea. New Guinea is the most language-dense place on Earth: Papua New Guinea alone has more than 800 living languages.

The densest corner of the map

02 / THE COGNATES BENCH

Same word, different costumes.

Words descended from the same ancestor word are called cognates. Pick an everyday word and look along the row: the spelling changes from language to language, but the family resemblance shows through — especially once you know that each branch changed its sounds in regular ways. Germanic, for instance, turned the parent's p into f and its t into th, which is why pater became father and trēs became three.

“father” and its family

LanguageBranchThe word

False friends — decoding with the wrong dictionary

Cognates prove that resemblance can reveal kinship. These prove the opposite lesson: a word can look exactly like an old friend and mean something else entirely. Decoding is only as good as the dictionary you decode with.

gift ≠ Gift

English vs German

In English, a gift is a present. In German, Gift means poison. The two words really are related — both come from an old word for a thing given — but German's meaning slid, over centuries, from "a dose given" to the dose you would rather not receive.

Same ancestor, opposite surprise

embarazada ≠ embarrassed

Spanish vs English

Spanish embarazada looks like "embarrassed" and means pregnant. Announcing you are muy embarazada after a mistake is a famous learner's mishap — the dictionary in your head confidently decoded the message wrong.

The lookalike that lies

actual ≠ actual

Spanish vs English

Spanish actual means current, present-day — not "real" or "in fact". A Spanish newspaper's presidente actual is the sitting president. Same Latin ancestor (actuālis), two different specialisations.

One ancestor, two meanings

04 / WHERE LANGUAGE LIVES

A network, not a spot.

Language runs on a network in the brain — for most people, mostly in the left hemisphere. Tap the glowing parts of this simplified side view (or the buttons under it) to meet the main stations. The map is schematic and not to scale; the honest headline is the one the Everyday Body room also states: functions live in networks of cooperating regions, not in single spots.

Broca's area

Two streams

Sound → meaning · sound → speaking

Modern research describes the network as two streams working in parallel: a ventral stream that maps sounds to meaning (what was said), and a dorsal stream that maps sounds to the movements of speaking (how to say it back). Comprehension and production are partners, not a single pipeline.

Two streams, one conversation

Sign languages run on the same network

It's language, not voice

Deaf signers use the same left-hemisphere network — Broca's and Wernicke's areas included — for signed languages, and strokes there cause sign-language aphasias that mirror the spoken kind. The network is built for language; speech is just one of its output devices.

The code matters, not the channel

We know the map from breakage

Aphasia is the audit

Much of this map was drawn the hard way: strokes and injuries knocked out one part at a time, and clinicians recorded what stopped working. Broca's area is named for Paul Broca, whose 1861 patient could say only one syllable — "tan". Breakage, as any operator knows, is the honest audit of a system.

What breaks shows what it did

Children can redraw the map

Plasticity, early on

When the left hemisphere is badly injured early in childhood, the right hemisphere can take language over — something an adult brain generally cannot do. The network is not fixed at birth; early on, it can be re-routed.

Young networks re-route

The brain gets a whole explorer of its own next door: Everyday Body → — its brain section maps twelve regions and states the same rule this section runs on, that functions emerge from networks.

05 / LANGUAGE IS A CODE

The same jobs, done with words.

Everything in Secret Squares — encoding, keys, compression, error correction — has a twin in the language you are reading right now. That is the claim of this room, so here it is in the open:

Encoding & decoding

Thoughts → sounds → thoughts

Speaking is encoding: a thought is turned into a stream of sounds by agreed rules. Listening is decoding: the stream is turned back into a thought. The rules — sounds, words, grammar — are the code, and a conversation works exactly when both ends run the same version of it.

Agreed rules, both ends

The shared dictionary

Decoding needs the same book

A QR reader without the QR standard sees only squares. A listener without the words hears only noise. Cognates are what a partly-shared dictionary looks like after 6,000 years of drift; a phrasebook is a pocket dictionary for the ten messages that matter most.

No dictionary, no decode

Slang is a shared key

Encryption you grew up with

In Secret Squares, a shared key lets a group read what outsiders can't. Slang and dialect do the same job socially: instant decoding inside the group, static outside it. That is not a flaw in the code — it is the code, doing access control.

Insiders decode free

Idioms are compression

A whole scene in three words

"Kick the bucket" packs a whole event into three words — compression, exactly as in Secret Squares. But compression only decompresses with the shared dictionary: decode it literally, word by word, and you get a foot, a pail, and total confusion. It is precisely the wall Steven's Spectrum Search was built against — it explains sayings literally, for the readers the compression leaves behind.

Compressed for those who share the key

Context is error correction

Redundancy on purpose

QR codes carry spare data so a damaged code still scans. Conversation carries spare data too — gesture, situation, tone, the sentence before — so a misheard word still decodes. "Sorry, the what?" is a retransmission request. Human talk is error-corrected by design.

Damaged signal, message survives

Help — how Everyday Language works.

The family tree

Tap a branch, read its card

The tree is drawn from the parent language at the top down to its living descendants. Tapping any branch names it in the panel below: its main languages, one true and interesting fact, and its line back to the root. English's line is outlined in gold all the way down, so the room's headline can be checked at a glance: the path runs Germanic, never Romance. The gold path is the argument

The cognates bench

Eleven words, one family each

Each button loads a word family: the reconstructed parent word (starred, because it was never written down), the descendants across up to ten languages grouped by branch, a note on what to look at, and — where a familiar language lost or replaced the word — a false-friend warning instead of a fake relative. Every form on the bench is a genuine descendant of the starred root; that is the bench's whole standard.

Genuine descendants only

Honest limits — reconstructions & simplifications

What this room smooths over

Proto-Indo-European is a reconstruction: its words carry stars because no one ever wrote them, and its homeland and dates are the mainstream account, not certainties. The tree shows main branches and sample languages, not all of them, and real classifications have debated corners (the page sticks to consensus). Speaker and language counts for the other families are approximations from Ethnologue/Glottolog-style counts and are labelled as such. The brain diagram simplifies a network researchers still argue about. Simplifications are stated, here and on the page, rather than hidden.

Stated, not hidden
