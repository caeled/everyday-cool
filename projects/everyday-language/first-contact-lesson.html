First Contact — the lesson

First Contact drops you among friendly neighbours whose language nobody has translated, and hands you the only tool that has ever worked in that situation: the controlled experiment. Change one thing, watch what changes. Guess, then try to prove your guess wrong. Underneath the game sits a miniature grammar small enough to check completely — and this audit did: it generated every sentence the grammar can produce, drove the field notebook's checker the way a sceptic would, and spoke every mission's answer in all three languages. The grammar is exactly as consistent as the game claims. The notebook is honest about what "consistent" means — with one blind spot, and one menu option that is quietly dead.

First Contact was built by ChatGPT at Steven Powell's direction. This lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its own functions — label(), candidate(), expected(), check() — headlessly against the claims on the game's page.

The ideas under the game

The bench is a controlled experiment

The experiment bench lets you set four features of a scene — object, colour, number, action — and read the local's sentence. The game's own advice is the whole method: hold two features steady and change one. If "stone red" and "stone blue" share a word and differ in a word, the differing word is your colour candidate; show a blue seed and the candidate either survives or dies. That is the scientific method reduced to its moving part: a scene by itself fits many explanations, so you design the next scene to make the explanations disagree. Every observation in the log is a small experiment somebody chose.

"Consistent" means not ruled out yet — and that is the honest word

The field notebook never tells you a guess is right. It says "Consistent so far" or "Ruled out by a scene", and the distinction is the game's scientific heart. A hypothesis is not a guess that has been proved; it is a guess that has survived every test thrown at it so far. The audit made this concrete: after a single scene — one red stone, named — the guess "tavi means red" is Consistent so far, because nothing yet contradicts it. Feed the notebook the full set of scenes and the same guess is Ruled out, because tavi shows up next to blue and gold stones too. Both verdicts were correct at the moment they were given. That is not the checker being wishy-washy; that is what evidence is.

Grammar is compression: word order and one suffix do all the work

Each expedition deals you twelve words. From those twelve, the grammar builds every sentence the game can say — the audit counted 72 per language, 216 across the three packs (3 objects × 3 colours × 2 numbers × 4 actions, in each pack) — using nothing but position and a single plural suffix:

naming: OBJECT(+plural) then COLOR  ·  request: ACTION first  ·  warning: NOT, then OPEN, then the namingPack 1 examples: "tavi nari" (stone red) · "dara tavi vemi" (give stone blue) · "no paku sela nari" (do-not open key red). The suffix is -im in pack 1, -et in pack 2, -an in pack 3 — and it only ever attaches to the object word.

Three expeditions, three vocabularies, one grammar. That separation is the deepest thing in the game: the words are the part you memorise, the structure is the part you understand — and structure transfers. By the third expedition you are no longer decoding a language; you are slotting new words into a machine you already own. Real field linguists work exactly this way, at much larger scale and with far less cooperative neighbours.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The game's lesson panel claims: one consistent grammar, nothing randomly translated, the notebook checks guesses against everything observed, "Consistent" means not ruled out rather than proved, wrong answers cost nothing, and the game works fully offline. The audit extracted the shipped script (7,520 bytes) and ran it under a minimal harness — the game's own functions, the game's own data, no re-implementation.

Receipt 4: twelve chips, twelve words — a perfect fit

words() returns 12 chips per pack: the six object and colour words, give, open, not, and the three plural forms. Across all 72 sentences of a pack, exactly 12 distinct tokens ever appear — the same twelve. So no mission can require a word you cannot tap, and no chip is a decoy: everything on the tray is sayable, and everything sayable is on the tray. The expeditions cycle deterministically (1 → 2 → 3 → 1), and the badge always names the pack actually in play — the audit stepped through four expeditions and the badge matched every time. (Under the hood, the script starts the pack counter at the third pack and lets the new-expedition step advance it to the first, which is why the first thing you meet is Expedition 1.)

Receipt 5: fully offline is a property of the file, not a promise

The page carries a content-security policy of default-src 'none' with connect-src 'none', and the audit's scan of the file found zero external references, zero network calls, zero storage calls, zero random calls. Everything — the three vocabularies, the grammar, the checker — is in the one file, and progress lives in the tab's memory and resets on reload, exactly as the game's footer says.

How it's built — the coding choices

One template, three dictionaries

The three language packs are three rows of a data table — ten entries each: three objects, three colours, give, open, not, and the plural suffix. label() assembles a sentence from whichever row is active: build the naming phrase (object word, suffix if "two", colour word), then either return it, prefix the action word, or prefix not + open. Because the bench, the log, and the answer key all read from that one function, the game's consistency isn't a discipline the author maintained — it's a property the structure can't lose.

The checker is three questions wearing one coat

candidate() answers by meaning type. For an object or colour guess, every scene containing the word must be a scene of that object or colour. For an action guess, every such scene must be that action — with one deliberate wrinkle: a do-not-open scene counts as evidence for "open", because the open word genuinely appears in it doing its job. For a "two" guess, every scene containing the word must be a two-scene. A guess about a word with no scenes is not graded at all. It is a small, honest piece of logic: it never inspects a scene the word isn't in, and it never calls survival a proof.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The "two" menu option is dead — a wiring defect, stated precisely: the notebook's meaning menu is built from the game's names table, and each option's value is the table's key. For the entry displayed as "two", the key is 'plural' — so choosing "two" calls candidate(word, 'plural'). But the checker tests for the display text: meaning === 'two'. 'plural' matches no branch, so every "two" guess, for every word, in every pack, returns "Need a mission clue". The audit ran all ten menu values: nine reach a real check; only this one falls through. And the branch itself is sound — fed the literal 'two' directly, it answers correctly ("Consistent so far" for a plural form over plural scenes; "Ruled out" for a base word once singular scenes exist). The one hypothesis the notebook cannot test is the plural — which is the exact hypothesis mission 2 is built around. It is a one-word mismatch between two functions, and it is named here because the game ships as built.

The plural blind spot: the checker matches whole tokens — it splits each logged sentence on spaces and looks for the word. A two-stone scene's sentence is "tavi-im nari"; its tokens are tavi-im and nari, and the bare stem tavi is not among them. So plural scenes count as evidence for the plural chip only, never for the base word. Measured: observe only plural stone scenes, then ask whether tavi means stone — the notebook answers "No scene yet", although scenes containing the stem are sitting in the log. The direction of the error is the safe one (plural scenes can neither wrongly confirm nor wrongly refute a base-word guess; they simply don't count), but the page's "checks each guess against everything you've observed" has this one seam, and "No scene yet" overstates it — the scene was seen; the checker can't see inside the word.

The not-word inherits the open pass: the deliberate rule that credits do-not-open scenes to an "open" guess applies to any word that appears in those scenes — and the not-word appears only in them. So guessing the not-word means "open" also returns "Consistent so far", and no observation the bench can make will ever rule it out: within the features the checker tracks (object, colour, number, action), the not-word and the do-not-open action are perfectly correlated. Word position would separate them — the not-word is always first — but the checker doesn't look at position. It is less a bug than a limit of the instrument, and it teaches the same lesson as the game: some distinctions need a different experiment.

The first scene is shown but not logged: a new expedition opens with a scene already on the field station — one red stone, and "tavi nari" in the speech bubble — yet the observation log starts empty and the counter reads "No observations yet". Displaying and logging are separate steps in the code, and only the bench's "Show & read" button logs. The bench is preset to exactly that opening scene, so one click records it; but a player who starts changing features straight away may never notice the first sentence was evidence too.

Small print: the key object is called "Key" on the bench, "keycase" in the mission briefs and the notebook's menu, and "keys" in scene descriptions — three English labels for one word of the neighbours' language; harmless, and it never touches a sentence the game grades. And the game's own closing caveat is accurate: this is a teaching model. The invented grammar never bends, has no irregular words, no ambiguity, no context — which is precisely what makes it decodable in an afternoon, and precisely how real languages differ.
