Boggle — the lesson

Boggle looks like the simplest game in this collection: shake sixteen dice, find words, beat the clock. Underneath it sit three real subjects — the statistics of English spelling, the graph search a computer runs when it "just finds" every word you missed, and a 172,727-word argument about what counts as a word at all. This page is the other half of the game: how the dice are loaded by the language itself, how the machine walks the same grid you do, and the audit that checked this build's dice, dictionary, and solver against its own claims — including re-deriving its entire dictionary from the public source it names.

Boggle was built by ChatGPT at Steven Powell's direction — the commission was first given to Microsoft Copilot, which could not output a file carrying the embedded dictionary, and ChatGPT completed it. This lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its engine headlessly.

The language under the game

The dice are loaded — by English

The tray is not sixteen random letters. It is sixteen physical dice, each with six faces, and the letters were dealt onto those faces the way the language deals them: common letters get more faces. Across all 96 faces of the classic set this build uses, the audit counted:

Thirty-three of the 96 faces are vowels. E alone holds eleven faces; Z holds one. That is why a shaken tray so often spells something: the dice carry English letter frequency in their pockets, the same distribution that makes E the most common letter in this paragraph. And the one Q face is printed on the die as Qu — because in English, Q without U is nearly a spelling error, so the die refuses to offer it. The game honours that literally: the audit fed it QAT, a genuine dictionary word (a shrub), on a tray whose only Q was the Qu die — and the grader correctly refused to trace it. The die says Qu, and Qu is what you get.

Finding words is a search — and you and the machine walk the same grid

Every word hunt, yours or the computer's, is a walk on a graph: sixteen dice, each connected to its (up to) eight neighbours, no die visited twice. You search it by eye, following hunches — there's an S-T-A-R-T… The machine searches it by depth-first search: step to a neighbour, extend the string, ask one question — is this the beginning of any word in the dictionary? — and either keep walking or backtrack. The dictionary is stored as a trie, a tree of prefixes, so that question costs almost nothing and whole branches of the grid die the moment they stop being plausible English. QUILT takes the machine four dice and five letters; it checks the Q branch, the QU branch, and prunes everything else. The machine's advantage over you is not cleverness. It is stamina: it walks every branch, including the ones your eyes skipped, in less time than one of your glances.

How much is hiding in a tray? The audit dealt 240 seeded trays and ran the solver on each: about 100 findable dictionary words per tray on average (fewest 8, most 371). A sample tray — S V E H / O O A N / E L C C / T T D E — held 117 words worth 167 points, including SOLACED and ENHALOS, and a small crowd of three-letter citizens like VOE, HAE, and OCA. When the reveal at the end of a round makes you feel outnumbered, that is the honest arithmetic of the grid: the words were there. Finding thirty of a hundred is a good human score.

The dictionary is a decision, not a fact

"Is that a word?" has no answer until someone picks a list. This build embeds the ENABLE word list (the Enhanced North American Benchmark Lexicon, credited on the game's own page to Alan Beale and M. Cooper, public domain) — 172,727 words of three or more letters, carried inside the file, which is most of why the file is 1.9 megabytes. A list that big cuts both ways, and the lesson should say both plainly. It will know words you have never met — ZYZZYVA (a weevil; the list's last word alphabetically), QAT, VOE, HAO — and its answer keys will be full of them. And because the lexicon's roots are in the 1990s, it does not know some words you use every day: the audit checked, and EMAIL, INTERNET, WEBSITE, BLOG, SELFIE, EMOJI, and COVID are all absent. Type EMAIL on the right tray and the game will refuse it, correctly, by its own stated rules. That is not a bug in the game; it is the nature of a frozen list. The game's page discloses the arrangement in full — the source, the count, the warning that the list is unfiltered and includes obscure and archaic terms — and the audit below verified the disclosure down to the byte.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

Boggle arrived with a commission: the exact classic dice, a real embedded dictionary honestly labelled, exact grading, classic scoring. The audit extracted the shipped engine — dice table, shake, tracer, solver, scoring — and ran it headlessly against independent implementations written from the rules, not from the game's code.

Receipt 4: scoring and flow

The scoring function is the classic table, verbatim: 3–4 letters → 1, 5 → 2, 6 → 3, 7 → 5, 8 or more → 11, with a word's letter count including both letters of Qu. Duplicates are caught and score nothing, with a message saying so. The timed round runs on a wall-clock deadline of exactly 180,000 ms — and it keeps running while the tab is hidden, which the page discloses rather than apologises for; the practice mode is genuinely untimed. The end-of-round reveal runs the solver, groups every findable word by length with yours marked, and totals both columns: "You found X of Y words and earned A of B possible points" — the same solver the audit proved complete, so the key is exactly as complete as the dictionary, neither more nor less.

How it's built — the coding choices

One file, no network, and a list carried in the open

The whole game — board, engine, and the 172,727-word list as a plain-text block — lives in a single HTML file with a content-security policy of default-src 'none': no libraries, no assets, no dictionary lookups, nothing fetched. The audit confirmed there is not one external reference in the file. At load, the list is read once into a set for membership tests and a trie for the solver; after that, every question the game asks is answered from memory.

The neighbourhood is precomputed, the prefixes are a tree

Adjacency for the 4×4 grid is computed once into a table (corners have 3 neighbours, edges 5, the four middle dice 8 — the audit verified the table symmetric and complete), and both the tracer and the solver walk it. The tracer that lights your typed word and the solver that builds the answer key share the same movement rules and the same bitmask trick for "die already spent" — one bit per die in a 16-bit mask — which is why the two can be proved to agree: they are the same walk, one guided by your word, the other by the dictionary tree.

Honest randomness

The shake draws from the browser's cryptographic generator with rejection sampling — the standard fix for the small bias that plain modulo arithmetic would leave in the faces — and the page says so. Nothing about a tray is curated: no reroll, no seeding toward friendly letters. The kindness in a Boggle tray comes from the dice themselves, not from the dealer.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own disclosure is accurate — the audit checked it line by line: the dice are the exact classic set, printed in full on the page; the dictionary is a subset of English and real words missing from it do not score; the reveal means every traceable word in this list, not every word in English; the list is unfiltered, with obscure, archaic, and potentially offensive entries, and no definitions; the timed round runs while the tab is hidden. All of that holds, and the dictionary disclosure holds to the byte — the embedded list is provably the named source, filtered exactly as described.

The flip side of a frozen list: the disclosure speaks of "real English words missing from it" in general. The audit can name the ones that will actually surprise a player: EMAIL, INTERNET, WEBSITE, BLOG, SELFIE, EMOJI, and COVID are not in ENABLE. A player who finds EMAIL on a tray has found a real word and will be refused — the refusal is the list's age showing, and it is the price of an answer key that can be complete. The same size that admits ZYZZYVA also guarantees your reveal will contain words nobody at the table has ever seen; on the audit's trays the key averaged about 100 words, most of them strangers.

No score outlives the round: there is no stored best score — close or reload the page and the round is gone. The page says this plainly ("Rounds stay in memory; closing or reloading resets the game"), and the file indeed contains no local storage at all. Whether that is a lack or a virtue is a kitchen-table question; it is not a hidden one.

Small print: the file's lines end in CRLF (Windows-style) throughout, and the dictionary load quietly depends on the browser normalising those line endings, which the HTML standard guarantees — in any real browser the list parses cleanly, as this audit verified under browser rules. It is the kind of detail that only bites someone extracting the word list with a naive tool (it bit the audit's first harness pass, which briefly "proved" THE was missing). Also: the word box accepts up to 32 typed letters while the longest findable word on any tray is bounded by the 16 dice — harmless headroom. And the 28-letter dictionary champion above can never be played; a word list answers "is it English?", not "can it fit on the tray?" — the solver, not the list, is in charge of the second question.
