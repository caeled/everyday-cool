Balance It — the lesson

Balance It is a counting game with one rule underneath it: in a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. Build a molecule and the count has to be exact. Balance an equation and the tally has to match on both sides of the arrow — in the smallest whole numbers, or it doesn't count. This page is the other half of the game: the law it runs on, how to read a formula, the working method for balancing, the game's own levels worked in full, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The one rule: conservation of mass

In the 1780s Antoine Lavoisier weighed reactions carefully — sealed vessels, nothing allowed to sneak in or out — and found the books always balanced: the mass of everything you start with equals the mass of everything you end with. The reason, which the atomic theory later supplied, is that a reaction does not make or destroy atoms. It takes molecules apart and reassembles the same atoms into new molecules. Bonds break and form; the cast does not change.

That is why the Chemistry room's own summary of every kitchen experiment is "count the atoms before and after, and the tally matches." A balanced equation is that tally, written down. The arrow in an equation does not mean "equals" the way it does in arithmetic — it means "rearranges into" — but the atom count on each side of it must be equal, element by element, or the equation is claiming atoms appeared from nowhere.

Reading a formula: subscripts, coefficients, brackets

Three kinds of number appear around a formula, and mixing them up is the one beginner error that matters:

  • A subscript (the small number inside a formula) counts atoms within one unit of the substance. H2O is two hydrogens and one oxygen, every time, in every drop. Changing a subscript changes the substance itself — H2O is water, H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, and no amount of balancing may ever edit a subscript.
  • A coefficient (the number in front) counts how many units you are talking about. 2H2O is two water molecules: four hydrogens, two oxygens. Coefficients are the only numbers balancing is allowed to touch.
  • Brackets multiply. A number after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside it. Ca(OH)2 is one calcium, and the bracket doubled: two oxygens, two hydrogens. The game's parser counts it exactly that way — and counts Al2(SO4)3 as Al 2, S 3, O 12, two brackets deep.
Ca(OH)₂ = Ca ×1, O ×2, H ×2 One formula unit of calcium hydroxide. Read the bracket as "the whole group, twice" — that reading is the entire skill the game's bracket finale is testing.

The balancing method

Balancing is bookkeeping with a working order. The method below is honestly a set of heuristics — habits that reliably untangle school equations — not a law. The law is only the tally; these are how experienced counters get the tally to agree.

  1. Count first. Write the ledger: every element, how many on the left, how many on the right, before changing anything. (The game keeps this ledger live under the equation; on paper, you keep it yourself.)
  2. Never touch a subscript. If the counts disagree, the answer is always a coefficient. Editing H2O into H2O2 doesn't balance the equation; it swaps water for a different chemical.
  3. Balance metals first. Iron, aluminium, calcium — elements that usually appear in exactly one substance on each side are the easiest anchors.
  4. Then the other non-metals — carbon, chlorine, sulfur — one element at a time, easiest first.
  5. Leave hydrogen and oxygen late. They tend to appear in several substances at once, so their counts keep shifting as you adjust everything else. Settle them last, when nothing else is left to move them.
  6. When a group survives intact, count the group. If the same bracketed group — a sulfate, a nitrate — appears whole on both sides, treat it as one block instead of unpacking it into atoms. (In the game's finale the OH group does not survive — it becomes water — so there you count atoms, not blocks. Knowing which case you are in is the judgement the heuristic needs.)
  7. Pairs meeting odd numbers: think in common multiples. Oxygen arrives as O2 — in twos. When the other side wants it in threes, the smallest meeting point is six: three O2 against two groups of three. Worked in full below.
  8. Recount everything after every change. One coefficient moves every element in its substance at once. The ledger is the game.
  9. Divide through at the end. If every coefficient shares a factor, the equation is balanced but not finished — see the next section.

And why the game's steppers offer only whole numbers from 1 to 9: a coefficient of zero would erase its substance from the reaction entirely, and a fraction would claim half a molecule takes part. Chemists do sometimes write a fraction halfway through their working — then multiply every coefficient through to clear it. The finished equation always lands on whole numbers.

Worked examples — the game's own levels

Before the game shipped, its stored solutions were checked by running them through the game's own parser: all seven equations balance, every element matching, and every solution's coefficients share no common factor. The all-ones starting position wins none of the seven — every level genuinely needs balancing. Two of them, worked the way the method above works them:

Example 1: methane combustion — CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O

Start with every coefficient at 1 and read the ledger:

ElementLeftRightState
Carbon11✓ already right
Hydrogen42right side short
Oxygen23right side heavy

Carbon is anchored. Fix hydrogen next (oxygen stays late): the left has 4 hydrogens, each water carries 2, so set H2O's coefficient to 2. Hydrogen now reads 4 = 4 — but recount everything: the right side's oxygen just moved, to 2 (in CO2) + 2 (in the two waters) = 4, against 2 on the left. Oxygen last, as promised: set O2 to 2 and the left reads 4. Ledger all green:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O C: 1 = 1 · H: 4 = 4 · O: 4 = 4. Coefficients 1, 2, 1, 2 — the equation every gas stove quietly obeys.

Example 2: rust — Fe + O2 → Fe2O3

All ones: iron reads 1 against 2, oxygen 2 against 3. The metal-first habit says fix the iron: set Fe to 2 and iron matches — but oxygen is stuck, and it is the instructive kind of stuck. Oxygen reaches the left side only in pairs (O2) and the right side only in threes (each Fe2O3 holds 3). No coefficient on a pair makes 3, and none on a three makes 2. The smallest count both can reach is 6: set O2 to 3 (six oxygens in) and Fe2O3 to 2 (six oxygens out). Now recount the iron: two rust units hold 4 irons, so Fe goes to 4.

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ Fe: 4 = 4 · O: 6 = 6. Coefficients 4, 3, 2. The pair-and-three knot is the whole level — once you see the 6, the rest is arithmetic.

Example 3, briefly: photosynthesis — 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2

Glucose's coefficient is pinned at 1 — one glucose molecule is the product the leaf is making. Its 6 carbons force 6 CO2; its 12 hydrogens force 6 H2O. Then count oxygen: the left now holds 12 + 6 = 18; glucose keeps 6 of them, so 12 leave as O2 — six molecules of it. The proof's ledger for this level reads C 6 = 6, H 12 = 12, O 18 = 18.

Why smallest whole numbers: an equation is a ratio

Double every coefficient in the methane equation — 2CH4 + 4O2 → 2CO2 + 4H2O — and every atom still matches. The game's checker confirms it: balanced, with a common factor (gcd) of 2. So why doesn't it win? Because an equation doesn't report how much gas anyone actually burned; it states the proportion the reaction runs in. A ratio is written in its smallest whole numbers, the way 2/4 is written 1/2 — the doubled version is the same truth wearing a bigger coat, and it hides the proportion it exists to state. The game's rule is the chemist's convention: balanced and minimal, or not finished. When you land on a reducible set, the game tells you the factor and shows you the reduced coefficients — dividing through is the last step of the method, not a trick.

How it's built — the coding choices

Balance It is one HTML file with no libraries, and its arrangement is the same discipline the Chemistry room's formula bench uses: the rules live in one pure core, fenced between two marker comments (/* === CORE === */ and /* === END CORE === */), with no knowledge of the page around it.

One parser, three customers

The core parses formulas itself — elements, subscripts, brackets with multipliers — into atom-count maps. Nothing about any level is hard-coded: Mode A's judge compares two count maps, Mode B's ledger sums count maps times coefficients, and the balance check reads the same sums. The ledger you watch while playing is computed by the very code that decides whether you won, so the display and the judgement can never drift apart. And because the core is pure, the proof program lifts it out of the shipped file and runs it in Node: parsing receipts (Ca(OH)2 → Ca 1, O 2, H 2; Al2(SO4)3 → Al 2, S 3, O 12), all seven stored solutions re-verified, the all-ones position shown to win nothing, and every Mode A target poked with an extra atom, a missing atom, and a stray element — each must lose, and each does.

The win is two checks, not one

"Balanced" and "finished" are separate questions, so the code asks them separately: per-element equality across the arrow, and the greatest common divisor of the whole coefficient set. A set can pass the first and fail the second — that is exactly the doubled methane above — and the feedback for the two failures is different, because the fixes are different.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there are no ions and no charges here. Real equations must balance electric charge as well as atoms; nothing in this game carries a charge, so atom counts are the whole story — equations with ions (much of real solution chemistry) are a level beyond this one. There are no state symbols — no (s), (l), (g), (aq) — though real equations wear them; they decorate the counting without changing it. The whole-number rule is the school convention, honestly a convention: professional thermochemistry does write H2 + ½O2 → H2O when the point is "per mole of water formed," and the game will still ask you to multiply it through. The seven equations are the standard school set, chosen because each teaches a distinct move; the parser underneath them is general and counts formulas no level uses. Mode A speaks of "building a molecule" — for an ionic compound like Ca(OH)2, strictly, you have assembled one formula unit of a crystal lattice, not a lone molecule; the counting is identical either way. Element tiles cover the elements the targets need, not the whole periodic table.
