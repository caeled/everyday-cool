Voltaic — the lesson

Voltaic is an electrochemistry bench: two beakers, a salt bridge, a wire, and a voltmeter. Pick two half-cells and it tells you which one gives up electrons, which one takes them, how many volts the pair makes, how that voltage moves when you change the concentrations or the temperature, and — if you hook up a power supply and push hard enough — how to run the whole reaction backwards and plate metal with the current. This page is the other half of the bench: the laws it runs on, the numbers the shipped code actually produced when it was driven headlessly through every preset, every electrode pair, and every slider, and the places where the bench bends the truth, named plainly.

The one idea: electrons with somewhere to go

Every reaction on this bench is a custody transfer. One substance is oxidised — it loses electrons. Another is reduced — it gains them. The electrons cannot appear or vanish, so the only question a cell answers is: how badly does each side want the transfer to happen? That wanting is measured in volts. Each half-cell carries a standard reduction potential, E°, measured against the hydrogen electrode (defined as exactly 0.00 V). A very negative E° (magnesium, −2.37 V) marks a metal that would much rather be an ion: it gives electrons up easily. A very positive E° (gold, +1.50 V) marks an ion that grabs electrons greedily. Put any two together and the lower one becomes the anode (oxidation, electron source) and the higher one the cathode (reduction, electron sink). The cell voltage is simply the gap between them:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode Daniell cell: +0.34 V (Cu²⁺/Cu) − (−0.76 V) (Zn²⁺/Zn) = +1.10 V. The bench measured exactly +1.100 V — see the audit table below.

Two sign rules follow, and the bench obeys both in both modes. In a galvanic cell (a battery, running on its own) the anode is the negative terminal and the cathode is positive. In an electrolytic cell (driven by an outside supply) the reactions reverse, so the signs reverse with them: anode positive, cathode negative. What never changes, in either mode, is the definition: anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction, and electrons in the external wire always travel anode → cathode. Inside the salt bridge, the current is carried by ions instead: cations (here K⁺) migrate toward the cathode, anions (NO₃⁻) toward the anode, keeping both beakers electrically neutral as charge moves.

The standard table — and the audit of every pair

The bench ships eleven half-cells. Before this lesson was written, the shipped code was run headlessly — its own solver, driven through its own controls — for all 110 ordered pairs at 1.0 M. Every pair's E° came out as exactly the difference of the two standard reduction potentials, the anode was always the lower-potential side, and every overall equation the bench wrote was balanced (it balances by least common multiple of the two electron counts, so Al|Cu correctly comes out as 2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu with n = 6). Concentration moves voltage: the Nernst equation

Standard potentials are the 1.0 M story. Real cells drift as reactants are used up and products pile up, and the correction is the Nernst equation:

E = E° − (RT / nF) · ln Q At 25 °C this collapses to the rule of thumb E = E° − (0.0592/n)·log₁₀Q: each factor of ten in Q costs or gains 0.0592/n volts. R and F are the gas constant and Faraday's constant; the bench uses the full RT/nF form, so its temperature slider genuinely works — measured below.

The bench applies Nernst per half-cell first — each electrode's reduction potential shifts by 0.0592/nhalf volts per decade of its own ion — and the cell voltage is the difference. That is why the same decade costs different amounts on different benches. Measured from the running code at 298.15 K:

Two lessons hide in that table. First, the sign: diluting the product side ([Zn²⁺], what the reaction makes) raises the voltage, diluting the reactant side ([Cu²⁺], what it consumes) lowers it — Le Chatelier wearing a voltmeter. Second, the concentration cell: two identical copper electrodes, E° = 0.000 V, and still a real voltage, +0.068 V in the preset (0.01 M against 2.0 M), because the dilute side dissolves (it is the anode) and the concentrated side plates out, both sides walking toward equal. Leave the bench running with Dynamic Concentration Discharge on and you can watch a Daniell cell do the same walk in miniature: after 10 simulated seconds its concentrations had drifted to 1.040 M and 0.960 M, Q had moved to 1.08, and E had sagged from +1.100 V to +1.099 V. Batteries die by Nernst, not by running out of metal.

Running it backwards: electrolysis

Push an outside voltage larger than the cell's own, in the opposing direction, and the transfer reverses: the former cathode becomes the anode, the signs flip, ΔG changes sign, and the bench relabels everything to match. Measured on the Daniell pair: switching to electrolytic mode auto-raised the supply to 2.10 V, the badges read CATHODE (−) on the left (zinc now plates out) and ANODE (+) on the right (copper now dissolves), the equation rewrote itself as Cu + Zn²⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Zn, E° for the reaction as now written read −1.100 V with ΔG +212.3 kJ, and the current was (2.10 − 1.10)/(4.0 + 1.2) = 192.3 mA, as displayed. Drag the supply down to 0.50 V and the bench refuses honestly: status "Below Threshold", current 0.0 mA — a supply weaker than the cell cannot reverse it. The canvas labels confirm the plumbing in both modes: galvanic Daniell draws "e⁻ flow →", K⁺ → (toward the cathode), NO₃⁻ ← (toward the anode); electrolytic Daniell draws all three arrows reversed. Silver plating is the threshold-free case: Ag|Ag has E° = 0.000 V, so any applied voltage drives silver off the left bar and onto the right one — and if you switch that pair to galvanic mode the bench correctly reports "Equilibrium" and no current at all.

Counting electrons: Faraday's law

Voltage says how hard the electrons are pushed; Faraday's law counts what they carry. The mass of metal dissolved or deposited is fixed by the charge passed:

m = Q·M / (n·F) Q in coulombs, M the molar mass, n the electrons per ion, F = 96,485 C/mol. One mole of electrons — 96,485 C — deposits one mole of Ag, half a mole of Cu or Zn, a third of a mole of Al. Electroplating is bookkeeping with a power supply.

The bench's Faraday panel was driven for 10 simulated seconds in three presets, and its masses and gas volumes were checked against its own displayed charge:

How it's built — the coding choices

Voltaic is one HTML file. Its electrochemistry lives in one solver function that the audit could drive directly: it computes each half-cell's Nernst potential independently (E = E° + (RT·coeff/nF)·ln[ion], with the coefficient handling H⁺'s twos and fours), assigns anode and cathode by comparing them, balances the overall equation by least common multiple of the electron counts, forms Q in the direction of the reaction actually running, and derives ΔG = −nFE from the result — which is why ΔG, the equation, the badges, and the electron arrows can never disagree with each other: they are all read off the same snapshot. In electrolytic mode the same solver runs with the direction forced backwards and the threshold test Vext > E applied before any current flows. The display layer — corrosion pits eating the anode, dendrite crystals growing on the cathode, ions drifting to the electrode that will consume them — is illustration hung on that snapshot, not a second physics engine.

Bends and simplifications, named

The charge clock runs 12× fast — the one real bend. The Faraday panel is internally perfect (mass per displayed coulomb is exact, above), but the displayed charge accumulates at twelve times I × t on the bench's own clock: 10 seconds at 297.9 mA should pass 2.98 C, and the panel shows 35.67 C. The code's own comment admits the scaling ("scaled Coulombs for readable mg/mL display"). So: trust every ratio on that panel, but do not quote its coulombs against its seconds — a real silver-plating run at 0.30 A needs about two minutes, not ten seconds, to move 40 mg.

Concentration drift runs on its own invented rate. With Dynamic Discharge on, the anode beaker gains and the cathode beaker loses concentration at a fixed visual coefficient (Daniell moved 0.040 M in 10 s). That number is not derived from the charge passed — it cannot be, because the bench never states a beaker volume. (In a 1-litre beaker, the charge shown would have moved it 0.132 M.) The direction is always right and the Nernst sag it causes is computed honestly from the new concentrations; the speed is theatre.

"Power P" is total chemical power, not load power. The telemetry's 232.7 mW for Daniell is I × E for the whole circuit. The 4.0 Ω load resistor actually dissipates I²R = 178.9 mW; the other 53.7 mW is lost in the model's fixed 1.2 Ω internal resistance — which is also why the lamp can be labelled with a wattage no real load would see.

No overpotential. The bench drives electrolysis the instant Vext exceeds E — 1.231 V would split its water. Real electrodes demand noticeably more (surface kinetics, bubble formation, resistance); practical water electrolysis runs well above 1.23 V. The thermodynamic threshold is exact; the engineering threshold is higher.

Concentrations stand in for activities. The Nernst solver uses molarity directly, which is the ideal-solution approximation. It is good at 0.01–0.1 M and increasingly generous at the top of the sliders — the concentration-cell preset runs one side at 2.0 M, where a real Cu²⁺ activity is well below 2.0 and a real cell would read less than the bench's +0.068 V.

The rust preset is a corrosion cell, not rust. Its +1.23 V oxygen potential is the standard acid value ([H⁺] = 1 M). Rain and soil water sit near neutral, where the O₂/H₂O potential is about +0.82 V, and real rust is a mixed Fe(III) oxide-hydroxide formed in local cells on one piece of metal — not Fe²⁺ accumulating in a beaker opposite a platinum electrode. Also, the preset card's "+1.67 V" quotes E°; the running bench reads +1.691 V because the preset's own 0.20 M Fe²⁺ shifts the iron half-cell. Both numbers are shown; now you know why they differ.

"Silver Plate" plates silver onto silver. The preset is really a silver transfer / refining cell — a sacrificial silver anode feeding a silver cathode, mass conserved to the milligram. Real plating deposits onto a different object (the cathode would be your key, your spoon, your circuit board). The physics taught is identical; the jewellery is imaginary.

Smaller print. The temperature slider runs 250–400 K, but the beakers hold water: outside roughly 273–373 K at atmospheric pressure there would be ice or steam, so treat the extremes as a Nernst-slope explorer (the T-scaling itself measured exact). Internal resistance is a flat 1.2 Ω for every cell, concentration, and temperature. The gas electrodes respond only to [H⁺]; their gas pressures are silently fixed at 1 atm, with no pressure control. And in electrolytic mode the "Standard E°" readout flips sign (Daniell shows −1.100 V, ΔG +212.3 kJ) — that is correct bookkeeping for the driven reaction as written, not a claim that the Daniell cell's own E° changed.
