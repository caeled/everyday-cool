Spectrum — the lesson

Spectrum is two labs in one file. The Atomic Lab lights eight real elements — hydrogen, helium, lithium, sodium, neon, mercury, calcium and iron — and shows the bright lines each one actually emits. The Molecular IR lab tunes an infrared laser across six real molecules and shows which vibrations absorb it and which are forbidden by symmetry. This page is the other half: the physics underneath, and the audit — every line and vibration in the game was extracted by running the shipped code headlessly, clicking every element, every line chip and every vibrational-mode card, and comparing what the running code displays against real spectroscopy. The headline result is unusually good, and the one systematic flaw is named in full below.

Why atoms emit lines, not rainbows

An electron in an atom can only sit at certain energies — the rungs of a ladder, with nothing in between. Drop from a higher rung to a lower one and the energy difference leaves as a single photon whose colour is fixed by the size of the drop. Heat a gas, run a discharge through it, and billions of atoms make billions of drops — but only at the rung differences that element owns. That is why each element's spectrum is a fingerprint: a short list of exact wavelengths, not a smear. Shine white light through a cool gas instead and the same rungs take photons out at exactly those wavelengths — the game's Absorption (Fraunhofer) mode is the same fingerprint, printed dark on bright.

E = hc/λ  ·  ν = c/λ  ·  hc = 1239.84 eV·nm One photon, three descriptions. The game's telemetry card converts between all three live, and the audit re-computed every conversion it displayed — see the receipts below.

Hydrogen's ladder has a formula — the one case in all of spectroscopy where school algebra gets the exact answer. With the electron's reduced-mass Rydberg energy, 13.5984 eV:

En = −13.5984 / n² eV  →  λ from ΔE between rungs Drops to n = 2 are the visible Balmer series; drops to n = 1 are the ultraviolet Lyman series; drops to n = 3 are the infrared Paschen series. The game computes hydrogen's lines from this formula at run time — it does not store them — which is why they land where they do below.

One naming subtlety, stated plainly: the wavelengths most textbooks quote for the Balmer lines — Hα 656.3, Hβ 486.1, Hγ 434.0, Hδ 410.2 nm — are measured in air. The game computes vacuum wavelengths, which run about 0.03% longer, and its own tube note quotes the air values (656.3) while its telemetry shows the vacuum value (656.5). Both are real; the page mixes the two conventions without saying so. The difference is a fifth of a nanometre — invisible on its spectrometer — but it is a genuine mix, and now it is a named one.

The sodium doublet deserves its sentence: the game not only places D₂ at 589.0 and D₁ at 589.6 nm, it assigns them to the correct upper levels — D₂ from 3p ²P3/2, D₁ from 3p ²P1/2 — and at the default 1.6 nm slit width the pair blurs into one golden line, exactly as it does in a real school spectrometer. The one systematic flaw: the level diagram disagrees with the line list

For hydrogen and lithium the game is fully self-consistent: recompute any line from the two level energies it connects (λ = hc/ΔE) and you get the listed wavelength back to within 0.05%. For the other six elements, the level ladder is schematic — a handful of term energies, far fewer levels than the real atom has — while the line list is real. The audit recomputed all 99 lines from their own stored level pairs: 45 of the 99 disagree with the game's own level energies by more than 1% (helium 3 of 9, sodium 1 of 7, neon 15 of 20, mercury 2 of 9, calcium 4 of 11, iron 20 of 24). Why it matters, and where it doesn't: the spectrometer, the line chips and the telemetry all read the line list, so everything you measure there is right. But clicking levels on the energy diagram, firing the tunable photon, and the automatic cascade all work from the level energies — so in iron, a click that the diagram prices at one colour can be labelled with a line of another. A few line names also point at levels that aren't the ones named: helium's 402.6 nm line is labelled "5d" but wired to the 4d level (the same level correctly carries 447.1 nm); its 471.3 nm "4s" line is wired to that 4d level too. Sodium's 515.3 nm line is labelled "6s → 3p" — there is no 6s level in the game; it is wired to the 5d level. Mercury's 407.8 nm line is labelled 7s ¹S₀ but wired to the 7s ³S₁ level, its 491.6 nm "8s" line is wired to a 6d level, and its 623.4 nm "9p → 7s" line is wired to 6d → 6p. Calcium's H and K lines are a real doublet from two split Ca II levels; the game keeps only one shared level for both, priced exactly at the K line. None of this moves a line on the spectrometer — it is the diagram's bookkeeping, not the data, that is bent.

The IR lab: molecules vibrate, and symmetry decides who absorbs

A molecule's bonds are springs. Each pattern of stretching and bending — a normal mode — vibrates at one characteristic frequency, quoted as a wavenumber in cm⁻¹. Infrared light is absorbed only when the vibration makes the molecule's dipole moment wobble (Δμ ≠ 0): the light's electric field needs a moving electrical handle to grab. Symmetric vibrations of symmetric molecules have no handle — carbon dioxide's symmetric stretch and methane's breathing mode are IR-inactive, visible only to Raman spectroscopy. That single rule is why CO₂ and CH₄ are greenhouse gases through their other modes, and the game states it correctly on every card.

λ (μm) = 10 000 / ν̃  ·  E (meV) = 0.123984 × ν̃  ·  ν (THz) = 0.029979 × ν̃ Measured live from the running code: tuning to CO₂'s 2349 cm⁻¹ stretch displayed 4.26 μm, 291.2 meV and 70.42 THz — all three conversions exact.

Every wavenumber is a real gas-phase fundamental, and every active/inactive call is right — including the subtle ones (methane ν₂ inactive, CO₂'s bend active). Two honest notes on the two rows marked "see note": CO₂'s symmetric stretch is quoted in many textbooks at 1333 cm⁻¹, the value the game uses, but Fermi resonance with the bend's overtone splits the real Raman bands into a doublet at 1285 and 1388 cm⁻¹ — 1333 is the unperturbed position, a standard textbook simplification rather than an observed band. Ammonia's umbrella mode is likewise a real doublet (about 932 and 968 cm⁻¹) because the nitrogen tunnels through the hydrogen plane; 950 cm⁻¹ is its centre, fairly quoted as one number at this level. And a context note the game leaves implicit: these are isolated-molecule values. The O–H stretch of liquid water is a broad band near 3400 cm⁻¹, dragged down and smeared by hydrogen bonding — the game's sharp 3657/3756 cm⁻¹ pair is the honest spectrum of a lone water molecule, not of a glass of water. There is no C=O double-bond stretch (~1700 cm⁻¹) in the game at all: its carbon–oxygen entries are CO₂'s asymmetric stretch (2349) and CO's triple bond (2143), both correctly placed.

Conversions, measured

The audit drove the photon slider and the IR laser across their ranges and read the running telemetry. Spot receipts: 656.3 nm → 1.89 eV (hc/λ = 1.889); 121.6 nm → 10.20 eV; 400 cm⁻¹ → 25.00 μm · 49.6 meV · 11.99 THz; 3657 cm⁻¹ → 2.73 μm · 453.4 meV · 109.63 THz. The constants are the real ones (hc = 1239.842 eV·nm in code, c exact), the colour of each line is computed with the standard Bruton wavelength-to-RGB approximation, and the transmittance curves are Lorentzian dips on a 98.5% baseline — a display model, with widths and depths chosen for legibility, not measured cross-sections.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: (1) The level↔line inconsistency above is the big one: for six of the eight elements the energy-level diagram is schematic, and 45 of 99 lines cannot be recomputed from the two levels they are drawn between — worst, iron's 561.6 nm line against its levels' 926.6 nm. The spectrometer data itself is accurate everywhere. (2) Air vs vacuum wavelengths are mixed silently (prose 656.3 nm, computed 656.5 nm for Hα). (3) CO₂'s 1333 cm⁻¹ symmetric stretch is the unperturbed textbook value; the observed Raman Fermi doublet is 1285/1388 cm⁻¹. (4) NH₃'s 950 cm⁻¹ umbrella is the centre of a tunnelling doublet. (5) IR values are gas-phase isolated-molecule fundamentals — no hydrogen-bond shifting, so no liquid-water 3400 cm⁻¹ band, and no C=O ~1700 cm⁻¹ mode is offered. (6) Line intensities, IR band widths and depths, and the partial charges drawn on the molecules (e.g. CO's ±0.22) are schematic display values, not measured oscillator strengths or dipole data — though CO's dipole is drawn the right way round, carbon negative, which is the famous real surprise. (7) One display quirk measured in the audit: tuning to methane's inactive breathing mode at 2917 cm⁻¹ shows 53.2% transmittance — that dip is the tail of the neighbouring active 3019 cm⁻¹ band overlapping it in the Lorentzian model, not the breathing mode absorbing; the telemetry correctly still reads "IR Inactive". (8) Absorption mode reuses the emission line positions as Fraunhofer-style dark lines — exactly right in position (Kirchhoff's law), schematic in depth.
