Geometry — the lesson

Geometry is a working 3D VSEPR bench: fourteen real molecules plus a build-your-own steric builder, all of them held in shape by a live repulsion solver you can flick with a finger. The idea underneath is one sentence — electron domains around a central atom repel, and the shape is whatever arrangement keeps them farthest apart — and this page is the other half of the exhibit: the counting rules, the ideal angles, the real angles (which are not always the ideal ones), how dipoles add up or cancel, and an audit of the exhibit itself. Every number quoted below was measured by extracting the shipped code, running its solver headlessly until it settled, and reading the angles and dipoles the running code actually produced — not read off its labels.

The one idea: domains repel

VSEPR — Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion — counts the electron domains around a central atom. A domain is one bonding pair (a single, double, or triple bond each counts as one domain, because all its electrons occupy the same region) or one lone pair. The count is the steric number:

SN = bonding pairs (BP) + lone pairs (LP) Written AXmEn: A is the central atom, X a bonded atom, E a lone pair. Water is AX₂E₂ — SN 4. A double bond is still one X: CO₂ is AX₂, not AX₄, which is why carbon dioxide is linear and not tetrahedral. The exhibit counts this correctly — SO₂ loads as 2 bonding domains of order 2, not 4 domains.

The domains arrange themselves to maximise separation, and that arrangement — the electron geometry — is fixed by SN alone. The molecular geometry is the shape of the atoms only: lone pairs occupy space and push, but they are invisible in the molecular shape's name. That one distinction explains the whole menagerie: water's electron geometry is tetrahedral, its molecular geometry is bent.

Lone pairs push harder — ideal vs real angles

The ideal angles above assume all domains repel equally. They don't. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, spreads out more, and repels more strongly than a bonding pair:

LP–LP > LP–BP > BP–BP So each lone pair squeezes the bond angles next to it. Methane, with no lone pair, keeps the ideal 109.5°. Ammonia's one lone pair compresses H–N–H to about 107°. Water's two lone pairs compress H–O–H further, to 104.5°. Those are the real angles, and they — not 109.5° — are what the exhibit shows at rest, because its solver weights lone-pair repulsion above bond-pair repulsion (weights 1.66 / 1.36 / 1.00 in the code). Turn "Realistic LP Compression" off in the Builder tab and the same solver relaxes to exactly 109.47° for both NH₃ and H₂O — measured — which is the cleanest possible demonstration of what the lone pairs were doing.

The headline is genuinely good: all 14 geometry labels correct, all 14 polarity calls correct, and ten of fourteen molecules land within about half a degree and a few hundredths of a debye of the real values — CH₄ at 109.47°, H₂O at 104.47° and 1.85 D, BF₃ at exactly 120.0° with μ = 0 despite the most polar bonds on the bench. The electronegativity differences are also honest Pauling arithmetic throughout (O–H 1.24, B–F 1.94, C–H 0.35 — all check). The bends are specific, and they are named in the next section rather than averaged away.

Dipoles: polar bonds are not a polar molecule

A bond between atoms of different electronegativity is a small arrow — a bond dipole — pointing from δ⁺ to δ⁻. The molecule's net dipole is the vector sum of those arrows, and vectors can cancel. That is the whole logic the exhibit runs on, and run headlessly it behaves exactly as the theory says:

  • Symmetric ⇒ zero, however polar the bonds. CO₂'s two C=O dipoles oppose at 180°: measured μ = 0.00 D. BF₃'s three B–F dipoles (Δχ = 1.94, the largest on the bench) sit at 120° and sum to 0.00 D. SF₆: six polar bonds, 0.00 D.
  • Asymmetric ⇒ the arrows add. Bend CO₂'s skeleton by replacing its shape with water's — two bonds at 104.47° — and the same arithmetic gives 1.85 D. NH₃'s pyramid points all three bond dipoles partly the same way: 1.48 D measured.
  • The dipole is linear in Δχ. Sweeping water's Δχ slider in the running code: 0 → 0.00 D, 0.50 → 0.75 D, 1.24 → 1.85 D, 2.50 → 3.74 D. Same geometry, proportional dipole — the geometry supplies the direction sum, Δχ supplies the size of each arrow.

Flicking a CO₂ oxygen sideways in the exhibit briefly breaks the symmetry and a transient dipole appears, then dies as the solver relaxes — which is physically the right story, too: bent CO₂ in its bending vibration really does carry an instantaneous dipole, and that is exactly how CO₂ absorbs infrared.

Bends and errors, named plainly

SF₄'s equatorial angle is wrong — the exhibit's one large geometric error. Measured from the running solver: Feq–S–Feq = 124.2°, where the real molecule measures 101.6° — a 22.6° miss, in the wrong direction: the lone pair should squeeze the equatorial bonds together, and the solver instead lets them spread wider than the ideal 120°. (Its axial angle, 170.6° against a real 173.1°, and its dipole, 0.62 D against 0.63 D, are fine.) The exhibit's own angle-arc code even goes looking for an equatorial pair "≈102°" and settles for displaying the 124.2° it actually has. Trust the seesaw's shape and axial angle; do not quote its equatorial angle.

SO₂ disagrees with itself. Its note on the page says the angle is compressed to "~118–119°" — and the real value is 119.3°, so the note is right — but the solver itself settles at 116.5°, about 2.8° too tight. The words and the model on the same screen give you two different molecules. The dipole (1.63 D) matches reality exactly.

Two smaller over-bends. ClF₃ settles at 85.9° / 171.7° against real 87.5° / 175.0° — the lone pairs push about 3° too hard. BrF₅'s axial-to-basal angle measures 87.8° against a real 84.8°, and its own note claims "≈85°": right note, under-bent model, same pattern as SO₂ in reverse. NH₃ at 106.8° against the textbook 107° is within rounding and is not counted against it.

The dipole numbers are calibrated, not predicted. The code's formula is honest vector addition — μ = Δχ × |sum of bond unit vectors| — but multiplied by a per-molecule fudge factor (dipoleK) that ranges from 0.40 (SF₄) to 1.80 (SO₂), with 1.22 for water and 1.57 for ammonia. That is why the dipoles land so close to experiment: they were tuned to. Two consequences to keep straight. First, the zeroes are real physics — symmetry makes the vector sum vanish whatever the factor — but the non-zero values are fitted, and the generic builder (which uses a flat 1.15) will not predict a new molecule's dipole reliably. Second, the sum runs over bond vectors only: a lone pair's own contribution to the dipole is never added as a vector — it is folded silently into that same factor, even where a note (for NH₃) speaks of "bond and lone-pair dipoles" aligning. The story is right; the mechanism in the code is a calibration, not that story.

sp³d and sp³d² are labels, not the modern explanation. The exhibit tags SN 5 and SN 6 molecules as sp³d / sp³d² hybridised, the standard school presentation. Modern computational chemistry finds very little d-orbital participation in molecules like SF₆ and PCl₅; the bonding is better described with three-centre four-electron bonds and ionic character. The geometries and angles are unaffected — VSEPR predicts shapes without needing the hybridisation story — but read "sp³d²" as a bookkeeping label for "six domains," not as a claim about which orbitals are occupied. A related school-level simplification: the solver is a classical repulsion model (inverse-fourth-power pair repulsion on a sphere, with lone-pair weights fitted to reproduce NH₃ and H₂O). It is a model of VSEPR, not a quantum calculation — which is precisely why its failures, like SF₄ above, show up as wrong equilibria rather than wrong labels.

How it's built — the coding choices

One HTML file, no libraries — the 3D is hand-rolled: unit vectors on a sphere, a quaternion trackball camera, painter's-algorithm depth sorting, and spheres, bonds, lone-pair lobes, and dipole arrows all drawn on a 2D canvas. The physics is a damped repulsion solver stepped eight sub-steps per frame; flicking an atom adds velocity and the molecule visibly vibrates and relaxes back to its VSEPR minimum, which is the single best teaching moment in the exhibit — the shape is shown to be an equilibrium, not a drawing. Seeding matters and is done thoughtfully: domains start from the correct topology (equatorial sites offered to lone pairs first at SN 5, trans sites first at SN 6), so the solver lands in the true global minimum — square-planar XeF₄ rather than a tetrahedral tangle. The audit above was possible because the model is deterministic code: load a preset, step the solver, read the numbers. Where those numbers disagreed with the page's own notes, this lesson has said so.
