02 / THE NUMBERS BENCH

Count it. Weigh it.

The neutron calculator

Every nucleus is protons plus neutrons. If you know the mass number (protons + neutrons together) and the atomic number (protons), the neutrons are one subtraction away.

The formula bench

Type a chemical formula with ordinary digits — H2O, C6H12O6, Ca(OH)2 — and the bench counts the atoms and weighs one mole of it, showing every row of the arithmetic. Round brackets work the way they do in chemistry: the number after a bracket multiplies everything inside it.

03 / THE KITCHEN SHELF

Eight experiments, all kitchen.

Nothing here needs a laboratory, and nothing here uses anything stronger than vinegar, lemon juice, and dish soap. Each card says what you need, what to do, what it shows about atoms and molecules, and the honest safety line. Heat and hot water always belong to a grown-up — the cards say so where it matters.

Red-cabbage pH indicator

Purple juice that reads acidity

You need: red cabbage, hot water, a strainer and jug, clear cups, and testers: lemon juice, vinegar, baking soda stirred into water, a drop of dish soap in water.

  1. Chop a few cabbage leaves and put them in the jug.
  2. A grown-up pours hot water over them. Leave 15–20 minutes, then strain. The water is now deep purple.
  3. Pour some purple juice into each clear cup.
  4. Add a spoonful of one tester per cup and watch: acids turn it pink or red, plain water leaves it purple, and gentle bases turn it blue or green.

What it shows: the purple pigment molecule changes its shape — and so its colour — depending on how acidic its surroundings are. Chemists call that an indicator: a molecule that reports on other molecules.

Safety: hot water is a grown-up's job. Don't taste the cups once testers are in, and wash hands after.

Milk-and-soap colour burst

Colours that run away from soap

You need: whole milk, a shallow plate, food colouring, dish soap, a cotton swab.

  1. Pour milk into the plate until it covers the bottom.
  2. Add a few drops of different food colourings near the middle. They mostly sit still.
  3. Dip the swab in dish soap and touch it to the milk's surface.
  4. The colours burst outward and keep swirling for a minute or more.

What it shows: a soap molecule has two ends — one that loves water, one that loves fat. The fat-loving ends rush to grab the fat globules in the milk, and the stampede drags the colour along. You are watching molecules hunt.

Safety: don't drink the milk afterwards. Food colouring stains clothes and fingers — aprons are wise.

Oobleck

A liquid that punches back

You need: cornstarch, water, a bowl, a spoon. About 2 cups of starch to 1 cup of water.

  1. Put the cornstarch in the bowl and stir the water in slowly.
  2. Squeeze a handful hard: it feels solid. Open your hand: it melts and drips.
  3. Try to stir fast (hard!) and then slowly (easy).
  4. Rest a spoon on top and watch it slowly sink.

What it shows: the starch grains are tiny solid particles floating in water. Push slowly and they slide past each other; push fast and they jam together like a crowd at a door. Materials whose flow changes with pressure are called non-Newtonian — no atoms change, only the traffic does.

Safety: completely food-safe, but throw it in the bin, not the sink — it can clog drains. It is gloriously messy; work on a tray.

Lemon-juice invisible ink

Writing that appears with warmth

You need: a lemon (or bottled lemon juice), a cotton swab or small brush, white paper, and a warm lamp.

  1. Dip the swab in lemon juice and write or draw on the paper.
  2. Let it dry completely. The writing vanishes.
  3. A grown-up holds the paper near a warm lamp (or gently warms it) for a minute or two.
  4. The writing reappears, brown, as if it were always there.

What it shows: lemon juice contains carbon compounds that brown when warmed — heat speeds up their reaction with oxygen in the air, and the juice-soaked parts of the paper react sooner than the dry paper around them.

Safety: the warming step is grown-ups only — paper and heat need respect, and a lamp, never a flame.

The vinegar egg

An egg with no shell

You need: a raw egg in its shell, a jar, white vinegar, patience (one to two days).

  1. Put the egg in the jar and cover it with vinegar.
  2. Watch the shell: tiny bubbles form all over it.
  3. After 24 hours, a grown-up can pour off the vinegar and add fresh. By 48 hours the shell is gone.
  4. Rinse the egg gently. It is rubbery and slightly see-through. Hold it a few centimetres over the sink and let it drop — it bounces, a little.

What it shows: the bubbles are carbon dioxide gas. Vinegar (an acid) reacts with the shell's calcium carbonate and rearranges its atoms: solid shell in, gas and water out. The tough membrane under the shell is not attacked, so the egg keeps its shape.

Safety: it is still a raw egg — wash hands after handling it, keep it over the sink, and it is for looking, not eating.

Ice-cream in a bag

Salt makes ice colder than ice

You need: a small zip bag; milk or cream, a spoon of sugar, a drop of vanilla; a big zip bag; ice cubes; coarse or rock salt; a towel or gloves.

  1. Put the milk, sugar, and vanilla in the small bag and seal it well.
  2. Half-fill the big bag with ice, add a generous handful of salt, and put the small bag inside. Seal the big bag.
  3. Wrap it in the towel and shake and roll it for 5–10 minutes.
  4. Open up: the milk has frozen into ice cream. Wipe the small bag clean before opening it, so no salty ice gets in.

What it shows: salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes, so salty ice melts — and melting ice pulls heat out of everything around it, dropping the mixture well below 0 °C. That extra cold is what freezes the cream. Molecules of salt, interfering with ice crystals, change what temperature "freezing" happens at.

Safety: the bag gets painfully cold — always hold it through the towel or gloves. Check both bags are sealed before shaking.

Sugar crystals

Watch molecules stack themselves

You need: sugar, water, a saucepan, a clean jar, a wooden skewer or string, a clothespin, and about a week.

  1. A grown-up heats 1 cup of water and stirs in about 3 cups of sugar, a little at a time, until it dissolves. Let it cool a little.
  2. Pour the syrup into the jar.
  3. Hang the skewer in the syrup, held by the clothespin across the jar's mouth, not touching the bottom.
  4. Cover loosely and leave it somewhere quiet. Check each day: crystals grow on the skewer.

What it shows: hot water can hold more dissolved sugar than cold. As the syrup cools and water slowly evaporates, the sugar molecules have to leave — and they leave in an orderly way, stacking into a crystal's neat repeating pattern. A crystal is molecules keeping formation.

Safety: hot syrup is a grown-up's job from start to finish — it burns worse than hot water. Whether the finished crystals get tasted is also a grown-up's call.

The density rainbow

Liquids that refuse to mix, in layers

You need: a tall clear glass, honey, dish soap, water with a drop of food colouring, vegetable oil, and a spoon.

  1. Pour honey into the glass until it covers the bottom.
  2. Tilt the glass and pour dish soap very slowly down the inside wall. It lands on the honey and stays there.
  3. Do the same with the coloured water, pouring it over the back of the spoon so it lands gently.
  4. Finish with the oil, poured just as slowly. Four layers, stacked like a rainbow in a glass.

What it shows: each layer has a different density — the same cupful weighs a different amount — because their molecules pack and weigh differently. Heavy sinks, light floats, and poured gently they stay in their lanes. (The oil also refuses to mix with water at all: oil molecules and water molecules simply don't attract, which is its own second lesson.)

Safety: for looking, not drinking. Oil on a floor is a slipping hazard — wipe any spill straight away.

05 / HOW ATOMS WORK HERE

Five ideas that run the whole room.

Three pieces

Protons, neutrons, electrons

An atom is a nucleus — protons and neutrons packed tight — with electrons around it. Protons carry a positive charge, electrons a negative one, neutrons none. A neutral atom has equal protons and electrons, which is why the bench can say "electrons: same number as protons."

The proton count is the element

Isotopes

Same element, different weight

Change the protons and you have a different element. Change only the neutrons and you have an isotope: same chemistry, different weight. Carbon-12 has 6 neutrons; carbon-14 has 8. The bench's neutron counts use each element's most common isotope.

Neutrons vary; protons define

Formulas are recipes

Fixed counts, every time

H2O is not a suggestion. Every water molecule ever made has exactly 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen, and that fixed recipe is why the formula bench can count and weigh any formula you type: the counts never drift.

Chemistry counts exactly

The mole

A chemist's dozen

Atoms are too small to count one by one, so chemists count them in moles: 602,214,076,000,000,000,000,000 at a time (Avogadro's number). One mole of water molecules weighs about 18 grams — a couple of mouthfuls. The formula bench's "grams per mole" is the bridge between one molecule and a kitchen scale.

Counting by the mole bridges atom and kitchen

Reactions rearrange

Nothing appears, nothing vanishes

In every kitchen experiment here, atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. The eggshell's atoms leave as gas and water; the cabbage pigment's molecules only change shape. Count the atoms before and after any reaction and the tally matches — that bookkeeping is the deepest rule in this room.

Atoms are conserved

The atom bench

The picker and the picture

Press any element button. The drawing shows that element's shells with the right electron counts, the nucleus labelled with protons and neutrons for its most common isotope, and the facts beside it state all three counts in plain words. The drawing is the Bohr model — a teaching map. Real electrons live in clouds, not circles, and for the heaviest elements here the shell picture is the standard textbook simplification.

Counts exact; orbits simplified

The numbers bench

Two calculators

The neutron calculator takes an atomic number and a mass number and shows the subtraction in three steps. It refuses impossible pairs — a mass number smaller than the atomic number — and says why, in plain words. Nothing you type is kept.

Impossible numbers get a plain refusal

Reading a formula

Symbols, numbers, brackets

Each element symbol is a capital letter, sometimes followed by one small letter (Na, Fe, Cl). A number after a symbol counts that element; no number means one. A number after a closing bracket multiplies everything inside the brackets — Ca(OH)2 is one calcium, two oxygens, two hydrogens. The bench counts the atoms, then weighs them row by row using standard atomic masses, so you can check its arithmetic.

The bench shows its working

The kitchen experiments

Eight cards, all ordinary ingredients

Each card lists what you need, numbered steps, what the experiment shows about atoms and molecules, and a safety line. Two of them ask for a few days (the egg, the crystals) and several involve heat or hot water — those steps are marked as a grown-up's job on the card itself.

Heat belongs to grown-ups

Safety, plainly

The whole room's rule

Nothing in this room uses bleach, ammonia, or any cleaner stronger than dish soap, and nothing should ever be mixed with anything else beyond what a card says. The standing rules: grown-ups handle heat and hot water; experiments are for looking and learning, not eating, unless a card and a grown-up both say so; hands get washed after raw egg and before food. When in doubt, stop and ask — that is a chemistry rule too.

If in doubt, stop and ask

Works offline?

Honest answer: mostly yes

The benches are arithmetic, and arithmetic travels: the atom bench, both calculators, the combinations, and every experiment card work fully offline, including inside the desktop app. Only the Links Out shelf needs the internet, because those benches live on other people's sites.
