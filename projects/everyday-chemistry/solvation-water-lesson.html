Solvation — the lesson

Almost everything chemistry does in your kitchen, your body, and the sea happens in water — and water can do it because one small bent molecule is lopsided. This page is the other half of the Solvation exhibit: why the bend makes water polar, what a hydrogen bond really is, how water takes salt apart and wraps the pieces, why oil refuses to join in, and how water walks through a membrane toward salt — osmosis. Every number below was measured by running the game's own code headlessly and driving its labs, not by reading its labels.

One bent molecule: 104.5° and a lopsided charge

Water is three atoms in a bent row: oxygen in the middle, a hydrogen on each side, with an H–O–H angle of 104.5° — not the 109.5° of a perfect tetrahedron, because oxygen's two lone pairs squeeze the bonds together. Oxygen also pulls electrons harder than hydrogen does, so the molecule ends up lopsided: a partial negative charge (δ⁻) sits on the oxygen, a partial positive charge (δ⁺) on each hydrogen. Bent shape plus lopsided charge is a dipole — a molecule with a positive end and a negative end — and nearly everything on this page follows from that one fact.

The audit measured the game's molecule directly from its running coordinates: the H–O–H angle computed from the shipped code's atom positions is exactly 104.500°, and its site charges are −0.84 on oxygen and +0.42 on each hydrogen — essentially the SPC/E charges (−0.8476 / +0.4238) that research water models actually use. This part of the exhibit is not a cartoon; it is the real geometry with real charges.

Hydrogen bonds: a direction, not just a stickiness

A hydrogen bond is what happens when one water's δ⁺ hydrogen leans toward a neighbour's δ⁻ oxygen, roughly in a straight O–H···O line, aiming at one of the oxygen's lone pairs. It is much weaker than the bond inside a molecule — about 20 kJ/mol, as the game's legend says — so liquid water's bonds flicker: forming, breaking, reforming billions of times a second, about 3.5 bonds per molecule at any instant in real liquid water. In ice the same bonds stop flickering and lock into open six-membered rings, which is why ice takes up more room — and floats. The game's phase lab quotes the right densities: ice 0.92 g/cm³, liquid 1.00 g/cm³.

The game builds its hydrogen bonds the honest way — a donor hydrogen must point at an acceptor oxygen, roughly collinear, aimed at a lone-pair lobe — and the direction in the running code is correct. Two measured bends, though:

  • The network is undercounted. Measured in the running sim, liquid water at 25 °C averages 0.45 hydrogen bonds per H₂O (0.46 in the phase lab), rising only to 1.03 at −20 °C and falling to 0.34 at 125 °C. The ordering with temperature is right; the level is far below the real ~3.5, because the game's water is spread too thin (see the density bend below) and its bond test is strict.
  • The bond is drawn too long. The chamber's own scale bar says 92 world units = 1.0 nm. The measured mean O···O distance across the game's recorded hydrogen bonds is 34.3 units — 3.73 Å against the real 2.8 Å, about a third too long. The game's bond spring is built around that longer distance, so the looseness is in the model, not just the counting.
  • The promised ice rings did not form. In 100 seconds of simulated time at −20 °C the ring detector found zero hexagonal rings, even though the lab promises them and the "ice order" percentage (a formula, not a ring count) climbed to 30%. Cold water in the game gets stiffer and more bonded — it does not visibly crystallise.

Dissolving salt: pry, then wrap

Table salt is a crystal lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ held together by charge. Water attacks it with the dipole: oxygens (δ⁻) crowd around a sodium ion, hydrogens (δ⁺) crowd around a chloride, and corner ions — held by the fewest lattice neighbours — get pried off first. Each freed ion travels wrapped in a hydration shell of oriented waters. Real coordination numbers are about 5–6 waters around Na⁺ and about 6 around Cl⁻.

Driving the game's salt lab: the crystal does dissolve — 81.3% of ions free after 15 s of simulated time, 100% by 40 s — and the orientation rule in the code is the right one (a water counts as hydrating Na⁺ only with its oxygen side in, Cl⁻ only with a hydrogen side in; in the audit's sample, both Cl⁻ shell waters had a hydrogen nearest the ion). But the shells themselves are the exhibit's biggest bend:

Stated plainly: time-averaged over a full minute of the running sim, each Na⁺ carried 0.28 hydrating waters and each Cl⁻ 0.30 — the best single frame ever seen was 2 around a sodium and 3 around a chloride, against the real 5–6 and ~6. A direct count of all waters (any orientation) within shell range of an ion found only 0–2 per ion. The cause is density: 112 waters in the game's chamber sit so far apart that a full shell cannot physically gather. The "Hydration Cages" counter on the game's dashboard therefore reads 0 most of the time even while its own "dissolved" counter reads 100% — the prying is real, the cage is mostly a glow. Trust the dissolution; picture the shells fuller than the game can show them.

Oil, soap, and which way a micelle faces

Oil molecules carry no charge and cannot hydrogen-bond, so water's network squeezes them out — the hydrophobic effect — and droplets merge to minimise the boundary. Soap molecules are two-faced: a polar head that loves water and a hydrocarbon tail that hates it. At an oil droplet they stand radially: tails buried in the oil, heads out in the water. That wrapped droplet is a micelle, and it is how dish soap lifts grease.

Measured in the running oil lab: soap does gather at the oil — 81.8% of oil droplets gained surfactant contact after soap was added, up from 0% — and on average a surfactant's head sits slightly farther from the oil centre than its tail (17.3 vs 15.4 world units). But molecule by molecule the stance is unreliable: only 5 of 16 surfactants near oil actually stood tail-in / head-out; the rest lay sideways or backwards while still counting toward "emulsified." The gathering is real; the neat radial micelle in the description is the tendency, not what most molecules in the sim are doing at any given moment.

Osmosis: water walks toward the salt — verified

A semipermeable membrane passes water but not ions. The side with more solute has less free water — some is tied up in hydration shells — so water crosses both ways but crosses net from the pure side toward the concentrated side, diluting it, until the pressure of the built-up column pushes back as hard. That balancing pressure is the osmotic pressure, and van 't Hoff's law sizes it:

Π = iMRT Π in atm when M is mol/L, R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), T in kelvin, and i counts the particles each formula unit splits into (i = 2 for NaCl). Check the law: 0.1 M NaCl at 298 K gives Π = 2 × 0.1 × 0.08206 × 298 ≈ 4.9 atm — nearly five atmospheres from a pinch of salt in a litre, which is why osmosis moves real water in kidneys, roots, and desalination plants.

This was the claim the audit checked hardest, because a reversed osmosis is a false lesson. The verdict: the direction in the running sim is correct. Starting from 66 waters left (pure) and 48 right (18 ions), 2,400 steps of the game's own physics moved the counts to 58 left / 56 right — net flow left → right, toward the solute — and the ions never leaked: the closest any ion came to the membrane was x = +26.5, always on its own side. Adding 6 more ions on the right pulled 10 more waters across in the next 1,200 steps (58/56 → 48/66). Water walks toward the salt, in the game as in the world.

The numbers hung on that correct flow, however, are stage dressing:

  • Π is not computed from iMRT. There is no molarity anywhere in the sim. The displayed pressure is a tuned formula — (ion count ÷ 18) × 2.4 atm × (T ÷ 298 K), scaled by how much water has accumulated — which settled at 1.99 atm for the starting 18-ion setup and 4.36 atm after 6 more ions. Those are plausible-looking values in a plausible range, but they are dialled, not derived; you cannot check them against van 't Hoff because the sim never defines the concentration they would come from.
  • The flux readout is synthetic too. It is computed as (16 − excess water) × 0.06, not by counting pore crossings. Measured against the actual counts, it read +0.48/s while the compartments sat perfectly flat at equilibrium (58/56 unchanged for 1,200 steps), and −0.07/s during the strongest rightward flow after extra solute was added. Watch the water counts, not the flux number.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the whole exhibit is a 2D world. Real hydration shells and ice rings are three-dimensional — a 2D water can hold at most a ring of neighbours in the plane, so some of the shell shortfall is the price of flatland; the rest is the chamber's low density, which is a choice, not a dimension. The water is a rigid 3-site model: real molecules flex, vibrate, and swap protons (that swapping is how acids and bases move through water, and it is absent here). The "polarity" slider is a teaching dial, not a physical quantity — real water's polarity does not come in percentages, and at 0% the game correctly shows dissolution failing, which is the honest lesson inside the invented dial. The membrane's osmotic drive is an applied bias force near the pores, not something that emerges from counting free water; it produces the right direction and a saturating level, which is what a first encounter needs — but the pressure and flux numbers hung on it are display formulas, as the table says. Temperature is a thermostat target the velocities are nudged toward, so "125 °C" is a speed setting wearing a unit; the phase behaviour it drives (bonds loosening, then breaking up) is qualitatively right.
