Mechanisms — the lesson

Mechanisms is an electron-pushing lab: five organic reaction pathways — SN2, SN1, E2, Diels–Alder, and nucleophilic acyl substitution — each driven along a reaction coordinate ξ from reactants (ξ = 0) through its transition states and intermediates to products (ξ = 1), with curved arrows, orbital lobes, partial charges, and a live free-energy profile. This page is the other half: what each mechanism actually is, what the shipped animation does at every step when its own code is run, and — named plainly — where it bends or breaks the chemistry. Three of the five mechanisms are sound. Two carry claims that are false as written, and they are flagged in red below, not buried.

How this audit was done

The game's JavaScript was extracted from the shipped file and run headlessly in Node with DOM and canvas stubs — the house method — and every mechanism was driven, not just read: buildScene(ξ) and computeKinetics() were called at ξ = 0, 0.14, 0.28, 0.40, 0.50, 0.62, 0.74, 0.88 and 1.00 for all five mechanisms, plus substrate variants (methyl / 1° / 2° / 3°), leaving-group variants (I, Br, Cl, F), and the SN1 attack-face variants. The atoms, bond orders, curved arrows, annotations, equations, barriers and stereochemistry labels quoted below are what the running code actually presented at each step. Reading code alone is not an audit; two of the faults below only appear when the animation is driven to its final frame.

FALSE CLAIM 1 — SN1 "racemic" tert-butanol. At ξ > 0.88 the running code annotates its default SN1 product: "Racemic Mixture: 50% (R) + 50% (S)", and the HUD stereochemistry readout says "Racemic (50:50 R/S)". But the equation on the same screen is (CH3)3C–Br + HO⁻ → (CH3)3C–OH + Br⁻. Tert-butanol's central carbon carries three identical methyl groups; it is achiral. There is no (R)- or (S)-tert-butanol, and no racemic mixture of it can exist. Racemisation is a real SN1 signature — but only when the starting carbon is a stereocentre, which the tert-butyl case is not.

FALSE CLAIM 2 — the E2 "methyl" setting contradicts itself on screen. Selecting the methyl substrate sets the kinetics flag blocked = true, raises the barrier to 179 kJ/mol in the audit run, and displays "No β-Hydrogen!" and "Methyl halide has no β-carbon / β-hydrogen — E2 impossible!" — and the molecule on stage is still 2-halobutane, which has β-hydrogens, and driving ξ to 1.00 still forms the C=C bond to order 2.0 and lands on "(E)-2-Butene (trans) · Zaitsev Major Product." A reaction the page itself declares impossible completes anyway. (The SN2 tertiary case, by contrast, really is blocked in playback — the nucleophile rebounds at ξ = 0.34.)

1. SN2 — backside attack

The chemistry. A nucleophile attacks the carbon bearing the leaving group from the side opposite the C–X bond, donating into the σ* antibonding orbital. Bond-making and bond-breaking happen in one concerted step — there is no carbocation, ever — through a single transition state in which carbon is roughly planar with both Nu and X partly bonded. The three other groups flip past the plane like an umbrella turning inside out: the product's configuration is inverted (Walden inversion). Anything that crowds the back side slows it brutally, so the rate order is methyl > 1° > 2° ≫ 3°, and the rate law is second order: rate = k[RX][Nu⁻].

What the running code does. Verified step by step. At ξ = 0 the nucleophile sits on the axis opposite Br, with the note naming the σ* axis. Through ξ = 0.28–0.62 the Nu–C bond order climbs (0.12 → 0.53 → 0.94) as the C–Br order falls in mirror image (0.92 → 0.53 → 0.09) — genuinely concerted, no intermediate anywhere in the profile (single barrier, ΔG‡ = 68 kJ/mol for the default 1°/Br/DMSO case in the audit run). At ξ = 0.50 the substituents pass through the plane and the label reads "Pentacoordinate Trigonal Bipyramidal TS ‡" with a "3-center-4-electron axial bond" note — advanced wording, but correct. Sterics behave: methyl is fastest, 2° raises ΔG‡ by 19 kJ/mol, and 3° (ΔG‡ = 166 kJ/mol, blocked) physically rebounds the nucleophile at ξ = 0.34 in playback and never forms product. Leaving-group order I > Br > Cl ≫ F and the polar-aprotic advantage are both applied in the right direction.

The bend. The (S) → (R) labels are bought with a hidden deuterium. Plain ethyl bromide, CH3CH2Br — the substrate in the on-screen equation — is achiral and has no (S) or (R) form. The scene quietly makes the reacting carbon's third substituent a deuterium atom (atom S3, element D), which does make the carbon a stereocentre — of the isotopologue CH3CHDBr, a different (and much more interesting) substrate than the equation admits. The inversion the animation shows is real; the label is honest only if you spot the D. A second, smaller bend: the methyl setting's stereochemistry readout says "Achiral (Inversion)" — inversion at a methyl carbon is meaningless, since there is no configuration to invert. And a determined learner can scrub the ξ slider past the 3° rebound and park the "blocked" reaction at ξ = 1; the block lives in the playback loop, not in the scene builder.

HO⁻ + CH₃CH₂Br → CH₃CH₂OH + Br⁻ · rate = k[RX][Nu⁻] As shipped. With the scene's hidden D, the honest version is HO⁻ + CH₃CHDBr → CH₃CHDOH + Br⁻, (S) → (R).

2. SN1 — the carbocation

The chemistry. The C–X bond ionises first, alone, in the slow rate-determining step: rate = k[RX], with no nucleophile in the rate law. The carbocation left behind is planar sp² with an empty 2p orbital, so a nucleophile can capture it from either face — at a stereocentre that gives (near-)racemisation, half retention and half inversion. Carbocation stability sets the order, 3° > 2° ≫ 1° > methyl, polar protic solvents help by stabilising the ions, and because a carbocation is formed, rearrangements (hydride and alkyl shifts to a more stable cation) are always on the table.

What the running code does. The two-step shape is right: TS₁ at ξ = 0.28 (ΔG‡ = 64 kJ/mol for the default 3°/protic case), a genuine intermediate well at ξ = 0.50 (25.6 kJ/mol) labelled "Trigonal Planar sp² Carbocation Intermediate (C⁺)" with an empty-2p orbital graphic and both lobes open, then a low TS₂ (41.6 kJ/mol) for capture. The nucleophile correctly plays no part in the rate law ("Nu strength: zero-order in RDS"). Solvent logic flips correctly versus SN2 — protic now lowers the barrier. The face selector honestly shows top-face capture as retention and bottom-face as inversion.

The errors. First, False Claim 1 above: the default product is achiral tert-butanol, yet the final frame announces a racemic (R)/(S) mixture. The confusion is visible in the scene data itself — the three substituents the code actually draws on the "tert-butyl" carbon are labelled Me, Et and Pr (methyl, ethyl, propyl), not three methyls. A carbon bearing Me/Et/Pr/OH really would be a stereocentre and really would racemise — but it is not (CH3)3C–OH, and it is not the substrate in the equation. The 2° setting has the same mismatch in miniature: the scene draws Me/Et/H while the equation claims (CH3)2CH–. Second, the racemic final frame bonds both ghost nucleophiles to the carbon at once (C–NuTop and C–NuBottom both reach bond order 1.0 at ξ = 1) — read literally, a five-bond carbon holding two –OH groups. It is meant as a superposition of the two products, but nothing on screen says "ghost." Third, the 1° setting is labelled "extremely slow" yet is not blocked: it animates smoothly to product over a 120 kJ/mol barrier at a computed relative rate of ~10⁻¹⁰ — a reaction that, for a simple primary halide, effectively does not happen by this pathway at all. Rearrangement, the classic SN1 trap, is not mentioned anywhere in the exhibit.

3. E2 — anti-periplanar elimination

The chemistry. A strong base removes a β-hydrogen while the C–H electrons form the C=C π bond and the leaving group departs with the C–X electrons — all three events in one concerted step, rate = k[RX][base]. The geometry is strict: the breaking C–H and C–X bonds must lie in the same plane, anti-periplanar (180° dihedral), so the σ orbitals can overlap into the new π bond. Where two β-carbons compete, the more substituted alkene (Zaitsev) usually dominates with a small base; a bulky base flips the preference to the less substituted (Hofmann) product.

What the running code does. The core is verified. The substrate is 2-bromobutane held in the anti-periplanar conformation, annotated as such. Driving ξ: the base–H bond order rises 0 → 1 as Cβ–H falls 1 → 0, the central C–C order grows 1.0 → 2.0, and Cα–Br falls 1 → 0, all on the same ξ schedule — concerted, with a single TS at ξ = 0.50 and no intermediate. The product is (E)-2-butene, correctly the Zaitsev alkene for this substrate (the alternative, 1-butene, is never shown — see the bends). The three arrows (below) are textbook.

The bends. False Claim 2 above is the headline: the methyl setting declares E2 impossible and then performs it anyway, because the scene builder never swaps the substrate — only one substituent label changes, and the molecule on stage remains a 2-halobutane with β-hydrogens throughout. The same applies in reverse to the other settings: 1° and 3° change the barrier numbers and the notes, but the drawn molecule and the (E)-2-butene product never change, so the "alkene substitution" control is adjusting a molecule that isn't there. Two smaller overstatements: the closing note says anti-periplanar geometry "dictates stereospecific formation of (E)-2-butene" — in an acyclic substrate the other anti-periplanar conformer gives some (Z)-2-butene, so E is the major product, not the only one; and the arrows are numbered 1, 2, 3, which reads as a sequence even though the step note (correctly) says the three pairs move simultaneously. Hofmann elimination is not mentioned; with the small ethoxide base shipped, Zaitsev is the right call, but the bulky-base exception is part of the rule.

4. Diels–Alder — the [4+2]

The chemistry. A conjugated diene (4π electrons, in its s-cis conformation) and a dienophile (2π) react in a single concerted step through a cyclic, six-electron transition state: two new C–C σ bonds form at the diene termini while the π bonds reshuffle, leaving one double bond in the new six-membered ring. There is no intermediate. The reaction is stereospecific — substituents that are cis on the dienophile stay cis in the product (the "cis principle") — and with a substituted dienophile the endo approach is usually preferred.

What the running code does. Verified, and the cleanest of the five. The diene starts labelled s-cis, ethene approaches, and at ξ = 0.50 every ring bond order reads exactly 1.5 with both new σ bonds at 0.5 — the symmetric aromatic-like TS the annotation claims ("Cyclic 6π-Electron Aromatic Pericyclic TS ‡"; "aromatic" for a Hückel 6-electron cyclic TS is standard textbook language, not an error). At ξ = 1 the bookkeeping is perfect: C1–C2, C3–C4 and C5–C6 have fallen 2 → 1, C2–C3 has risen 1 → 2 (the cyclohexene double bond, in the right place), and the two new σ bonds stand at 1.0. The energy profile is a single barrier (92 kJ/mol) dropping to −68 kJ/mol, and the note gives the right reason: two π bonds traded for two stronger σ bonds. The Woodward– Hoffmann note (suprafacial [4+2], diene HOMO / dienophile LUMO) is correct.

The limits, honestly. With ethene as the dienophile there are no substituents, so the cis principle has nothing to bite on and the endo rule does not arise — the page claims neither, which is the honest choice, but it also means the exhibit demonstrates the shape of stereospecificity (the termini hydrogens fold syn, wedge/dash pairs preserved through ξ) without ever testing it. One cosmetic oddity: the equation decorates the product with "(Δ, Concerted)" — Δ normally denotes heat, not a property of cyclohexene.

1,3-Butadiene (4π) + Ethene (2π) → Cyclohexene 3 π-bonds in, 1 π-bond + 2 new σ-bonds out. One step, no intermediate — the audit found no bend in the mechanism itself.

5. Acyl substitution — addition, then elimination

The chemistry. Nucleophilic acyl substitution is two steps, not one. The nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon at the Bürgi–Dunitz angle (~107° to the C=O axis, into the π* orbital); the C=O π electrons move onto oxygen, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate (carbon goes sp² → sp³). Then an oxygen lone pair collapses back down, the C=O reforms, and the leaving group is expelled (sp³ → sp²). Which group leaves is decided by basicity: the weaker base leaves, giving the reactivity order acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide, and among halides I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ ≫ F⁻ as the departing group.

What the running code does. Verified. Default case: HO⁻ + CH3COCl → CH3COOH + Cl⁻. TS₁ at ξ = 0.28 (56.5 kJ/mol), the C=O bond order dips 2 → 1 through a real intermediate well at ξ = 0.50 (23.3 kJ/mol) labelled "Tetrahedral Alkoxide Intermediate (sp³)" with the oxygen carrying the formal − charge, then TS₂ at ξ = 0.74 (47.8 kJ/mol) restores the C=O to order 2.0 as Cl⁻ leaves. The 107° Bürgi–Dunitz trajectory is drawn and labelled. The leaving-group physics is right in both steps, and fluoride proves it: switching the acyl halide to F raises TS₂ to 65.8 kJ/mol — above TS₁ — so the collapse step becomes rate-limiting, exactly the behaviour the "best leaving group leaves" rule predicts; iodide drops TS₂ to 28.9 kJ/mol.

The bends. Small ones only. The intermediate's step note calls it "high-energy" — it sits above the reactants on this profile, but a tetrahedral intermediate is a genuine (if shallow) energy minimum, not a transition state, and the wording blurs that distinction the rest of the page works hard to teach. With the "weak" nucleophile selected the nucleophile is water, and the product is written CH3CO–OH2⁺ — the protonated acid; the final deprotonation step that would give the neutral carboxylic acid is not shown. And the exhibit only ever varies the halide, so the broader leaving-group ladder (chloride vs ester vs amide) is asserted in the kinetics notes rather than demonstrated.

The arrows, audited

A curved arrow must start at the electron source — a lone pair, a nucleophile, or a bond — and point at the sink. Every arrow the running code drew was checked at the ξ where it appears:

No arrow in the exhibit runs backwards. Whatever else bends, the electron-pushing grammar — the thing the page exists to teach — is sound throughout.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: every barrier, intermediate energy and relative rate in the exhibit comes from an additive scoring model inside the code (a base barrier plus fixed kJ/mol adjustments for bulk, leaving group, nucleophile and solvent, fed through the Eyring equation). The directions of those adjustments agree with real chemistry everywhere the audit checked; the numbers are teaching values, not measured activation energies, and no label on the page says so. SN1 rearrangements are absent. Hofmann elimination is absent. The Diels–Alder cis principle and endo rule are untestable with an unsubstituted dienophile. The SN2 (S)/(R) story depends on an unlabelled deuterium. And the two red flags above — racemic tert-butanol, and the self-contradicting E2 methyl setting — are not simplifications; they are errors a learner would carry away as facts, and they are the two things a revision should fix first: give the SN1 default a genuinely chiral tertiary substrate (or drop the racemic label for tert-butyl), and make the E2 substrate control actually change the molecule — or disable the run, the way SN2's 3° case honestly does.
