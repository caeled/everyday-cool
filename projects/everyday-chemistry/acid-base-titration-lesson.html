Titration — the lesson

A titration is a controlled argument between an acid and a base, measured one drop at a time. You add a solution you know exactly (the titrant, in the burette) to a solution you are interrogating (the analyte, in the flask) and you watch the pH. Most of the time almost nothing happens — then, within a fraction of a millilitre of the equivalence point, the pH leaps by six units or more. That leap is the measurement. This page is the other half of the lab: the charge balance the curve is solved from, the landmarks every curve must hit, the audit numbers measured by running the shipped lab headlessly and driving it the way a user would, and — named plainly — the places where the lab's own words bend away from its own (excellent) maths.

The one equation underneath: charge balance

A solution is always electrically neutral. Every positive charge in the flask is matched by a negative one, at every moment, at every volume. Write that bookkeeping down — hydronium and sodium on one side, hydroxide, chloride and however much of the acid sits in each of its protonation states on the other — and there is exactly one pH at each titrant volume that makes the books balance. That is the whole simulation. There is no Henderson–Hasselbalch shortcut and no piecewise "before / at / after equivalence" patchwork in the solver: it bisects the charge-balance residual over pH until the residual changes sign inside a window too small to see.

[H₃O⁺] + [Na⁺] = [OH⁻] + [Cl⁻] + (charge-weighted acid species) For acetic acid the last term is [CH₃COO⁻]; for phosphoric acid it is [H₂PO₄⁻] + 2[HPO₄²⁻] + 3[PO₄³⁻]. The fraction of the acid in each state at a given pH comes from its pKₐ values. Solve for the pH that satisfies the equation and you have the curve — all of it, buffer region, cliff and all.

Everything else on the screen is derived from that one solve: pOH is 14 − pH, the species bar reads the same protonation fractions the solver used, and buffer capacity β is the slope of the same charge-balance function (the Van Slyke definition, β = −dQ/dpH). Display and judgement share one core, so they cannot drift apart.

The landmarks, measured

The audit extracted the lab's shipped JavaScript, ran it in Node behind DOM and canvas stubs, clicked its real preset and dosing buttons, and compared its pH against an independently written charge-balance solver (bisection over [H⁺] rather than over pH) at hundreds of volumes per system. Default conditions throughout: 25.0 mL of 0.10 M analyte, 0.10 M titrant, so the first equivalence volume is 25.00 mL by stoichiometry.

Read the strong–strong row first, because it is the calibration check for the whole instrument: the initial pH is 1.0000, exactly −log(0.10), and the equivalence point lands at pH 7.0000 at exactly 25.00 mL — neither value is asserted by the code, both fall out of the charge balance. Either side of the cliff the curve is just as steep as the real one: pH 3.6981 at 24.90 mL and 10.3002 at 25.10 mL, a 6.6-unit leap for 0.2 mL of titrant.

The weak-acid row is the Henderson–Hasselbalch check. At half-equivalence, half the acid has been converted, [HA] = [A⁻], and the equation says pH must equal pKₐ:

pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA])  →  at [A⁻] = [HA], pH = pKₐ Measured: pH 4.7605 at 12.50 mL against pKₐ 4.76 — agreement to 0.0005, the small residue being the water and dilution terms the exact solver keeps and the Henderson–Hasselbalch approximation drops. The equivalence point is basic, 8.7295, because the acetate left behind hydrolyses — exactly as a weak acid / strong base titration must behave.

And the slope tells the buffer story in numbers. In the buffer region the weak-acid curve crawls — about 0.07 pH units per millilitre between 10 and 20 mL — while at the equivalence point it moves about 15.7 pH units per millilitre (7.156 at 24.90 mL to 10.3005 at 25.10 mL). Same burette, same titrant; the buffer is the acid–conjugate pair absorbing the added base, and when the pair runs out, the cliff arrives.

The audit receipt

Driving the interface, not just the solver: clicking +5 mL, +1 mL and +1 Drop on the strong-acid preset left the flask at 6.10 mL added, HUD reading pH 1.22 and 6.10 mL — the buttons, the state and the display agree. The Jump-to-Eq button landed on 25.00 mL, pH 7.0000. Across a dense sweep (every 0.37 mL over each system's full range, six systems, both blood-shock directions), the largest disagreement between the shipped solver and the independent solver was:

max |ΔpH| = 1.1 × 10⁻¹³ That is floating-point noise, not chemistry: the two solvers implement the same charge-balance model independently and agree to thirteen decimal places. What this proves is that the shipped code faithfully computes its model. What it cannot prove is the model itself — for that, see the simplifications below: the model assumes concentrations stand in for activities and K_w = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ throughout.

That table is the real lesson about indicators: an indicator does not find the equivalence point, it changes colour across its own range, and the choice works when the curve's cliff passes through that range. Phenolphthalein suits the weak-acid run because the equivalence pH (8.73) sits inside 8.2–10.0. Methyl orange suits the weak-base run not because 3.1–4.4 contains 5.28 — it doesn't — but because the falling cliff crosses the band within 0.4 mL of equivalence. The lab's default indicator for each preset is the textbook choice, and the numbers above show why.

Bends and errors, named

The solver has no bends worth the name. The words around it have several, and they matter because a learner will quote the words, not the bisection.

  • "½ Eq (pH = pKₐ)" is exact only for the monoprotic systems. The jump button wears that label for every preset. For acetic acid it holds to 0.0005 and for ammonia's conjugate to 0.0005 — but at the diprotic first half-point the lab's own solver reads pH 2.176 against pKₐ₁ 2.00 (+0.18), and for phosphoric acid the three half-points read 2.286 against 2.15 (+0.14), 7.2000 against 7.20 (exact), and 11.862 against 12.35 (−0.49). Neighbouring dissociations, dilution and water itself all pull on the later and earlier steps; pH = pKₐ at half-equivalence is a monoprotic truth and a polyprotic approximation.
  • The preset prose quotes rounded textbook equivalence pHs, not the lab's own numbers. Diprotic Eq 1 is described as pH ≈ 4.60 — the ½(pKₐ₁ + pKₐ₂) shortcut — while the solver measures 4.6398. The triprotic text promises cliffs at 4.68 and 9.72 and a third near 12.1; the solver measures 4.7039, 9.6630 and 12.1755. The weak-acid text says equivalence is "∼8.72"; the solver says 8.7295, which rounds to 8.73. None of these is far off — all are the standard approximations — but the page presents them as the lab's results, and they are not quite.
  • The weak-base explanation mislabels its own centre. It says the buffer plateau "centers at pH = 9.25 (pK_b = 4.75)". The measured centre is indeed 9.2495 — but 9.25 is the pKₐ of the conjugate acid NH₄⁺ (14 − 4.75), not the pK_b itself. The number is right; the name attached to it invites exactly the pKₐ/pK_b mix-up the preset exists to teach past.
  • Buffer capacity does not quite peak at half-equivalence. The text says half-equivalence produces "peak buffer resistance". Measured β in the buffer region peaks at 10.35 mL (39.6 mM/pH), before the 12.50 mL half-point (38.4 mM/pH) — dilution shrinks the total buffer concentration as titrant is added, dragging the peak early. The claim is within about 3% and fine as a rule of thumb; stated as an identity, it bends. (β also grows large again in excess strong base — that rise is the titrant itself, not a buffer.)
  • The colour starts before the band. The stated indicator bands are the real ones, but the colour functions blend over slightly wider windows — phenolphthalein fades in from pH 8.0 to 10.1, bromothymol blue from 5.8 to 7.8, methyl orange from 2.8 to 4.7. On screen the flask will blush a little before the printed range says it should. A visual softening, honestly a reasonable way to draw a gradual transition — but the drawing and the label disagree by about 0.2 pH at each edge.
  • Two pKₐ values are conventions, not constants of nature. Sulfurous acid's pKₐ₁ appears as 2.00; published tables put it nearer 1.8–1.9 depending on conditions. The blood preset's carbonic pKₐ₁ of 6.10 is the clinical apparent constant used in the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for plasma — the right choice for that preset, and a different number from the true first dissociation constant of H₂CO₃. The lab uses both silently.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the model works in concentrations, not activities, so at 0.10 M its pH values are the ideal-solution values a textbook computes, not the last-hundredth values a calibrated electrode would report. K_w is fixed at 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ and pH + pOH at exactly 14 — true at 25 °C, and the lab never names a temperature. The blood preset is a closed system: the flask's total carbonate is fixed and nothing breathes. Real blood is an open system — the lungs blow off CO₂ — which is a large part of why the real buffer holds pH 7.40 as well as it does; the lab's 20:1 ratio at the start is real (measured here: 19.95:1, pH exactly 7.4000 by construction), but its crash once bicarbonate is exhausted is the closed-system crash, faster than the body's. Volumes are additive and drops are a tidy 0.1 mL. None of this is hidden in the code — the charge balance is all there to read — but none of it is announced on the page either, so it is announced here.
