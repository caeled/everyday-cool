Oscillator — the lesson

A chemical reaction that ticks. Mix bromate, malonic acid and a metal catalyst in acid and the solution does not settle to one colour — it flips, red to blue to red, on a rhythm of its own, and in a shallow dish the flips travel as rings and spirals. That is the Belousov–Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction, and this game claims to run it, not draw it. This page is the audit of that claim: the model underneath, what was measured by running the shipped code headlessly, and every place the simulation bends the chemistry — named plainly.

The verdict: emergent, not scripted

The oscillation is emergent. The shipped file integrates a real reaction model — the two-variable Oregonator, the Field–Noyes reduction of the BZ mechanism — on a 240 × 170 grid, every frame, and the colour is only a lookup table read off the resulting numbers. There is no sine wave driving the chemistry: the only Math.sin calls in the file are the audio chime's oscillator and a pulsing dot drawn over pacemaker sites. Three headless tests settle it:

  • Leave it alone and nothing happens. A grid filled with the resting state and given no perturbation stayed at rest for 39 τ of simulated time — zero excited cells at every sample. A scripted animation cannot sit still; this system can, because rest is one of its genuine states.
  • Poke it once and one wave travels, then dies. A single droplet of activator (49 excited cells) expanded into a travelling front — 776 excited cells at 9.8 τ, 806 at 19.5 τ — and by 29.2 τ it had left the dish entirely: back to rest, not looping. That is an excitable medium spending a single pulse, not a film on repeat.
  • Turn the kinetic knobs and the period moves the way kinetics says it should — including switching the oscillation off altogether at parameter values where the Oregonator has only a steady state. A display-speed control cannot kill an oscillation; a parameter inside the equations can. The measurements are in the next section.

The spirals are emergent in the same sense. The game seeds a broken wavefront — a strip of excited cells with a refractory wake behind it — and nothing else. The free ends curl as the front advances into recovered medium and lags near the wake, and the curl closes into the counter-rotating pair. Slice a wave with the slicer tool and the same thing happens to your cut. Nobody draws an Archimedean spiral anywhere in the code.

The model: the Oregonator, in two variables

The full BZ mechanism (Field, Körös and Noyes, 1972) runs to dozens of steps. Field and Noyes reduced it to two variables that carry the whole behaviour:

∂u/∂t = Du∇²u + (1/ε)[ u(1−u) − f·v·(u−q)/(u+q) ]
∂v/∂t = Dv∇²v + (u − v) The equations the game prints on its own equation card, and — the audit checked — the equations its solver actually steps. u is the activator (scaled HBrO₂, bromous acid, the autocatalytic species); v is the oxidised form of the catalyst (ferriin to ferroin's ferrous form, or Ce(IV) to Ce(III)); ε is the timescale ratio between them, f the stoichiometric factor governing bromide yield, q a small rate-constant ratio.

The story those two lines tell: HBrO₂ makes more of itself (autocatalysis — the u(1−u) term), but only while bromide, Br⁻, is scarce; the oxidised catalyst slowly recovers and, through f, restores the bromide that shuts autocatalysis down; then bromide is consumed again and the cycle restarts. Excite, recover, repeat — a limit cycle in the stirred case, and in a still dish the same cycle at every point, coupled to its neighbours by diffusion (the ∇² terms), which is what turns a clock into waves. Bromide is not a third integrated variable here: the game computes it on the fly as a quasi-steady readout, w ≈ f·v/(u+q), which is the standard Oregonator shortcut — see the bends, below, for what its display scaling does to the number.

Measured: the period answers to the parameters

The audit extracted the solver from the shipped file and ran it in Node, stepping it at the game's own step size and timing upward crossings of the activator at the probe — the same crest test the game's own period readout uses. Periods are in τ, the model's dimensionless time unit; the game honestly labels them τ and never claims seconds. (At the default display speed, 1 τ passes in about 0.19 s of wall-clock time, so the stirred clock's 20.5 τ is roughly a four-second heartbeat on screen.)

And the one knob that is not kinetics: Simulation Speed multiplies the time step fed to the solver each frame (simDt = dtReal × 5.2 × speed). It changes how fast τ flows past your eye and changes no period measured in τ. That is the honest kind of speed control — it is labelled as display speed in effect, and the audit confirms it never reaches inside the equations.

The real chemistry, checked against the page

A classic BZ mixture is potassium bromate (the oxidiser), malonic acid (the fuel), sulfuric acid, and a redox catalyst — ferroin or cerium. The colour is the catalyst reporting its own oxidation state: ferroin is red when its iron is Fe(II) and pale blue when oxidised to Fe(III), so each blue wavefront is a region where the catalyst has been oxidised, sweeping through red, reduced solution behind the activator pulse. The game's default Ferroin palette gets the direction right — resting crimson at low v, blue at high v — and its telemetry names the right cast: HBrO₂ the activator, the oxidised catalyst, Br⁻ the inhibitor. That triangle — activator, inhibitor, catalyst — is the actual logic of the reaction, and the game teaches it correctly.

Stated plainly — what the page does not tell you: the recipe itself never appears. Nowhere does the game name bromate, malonic acid, or the acid medium, so a player can learn the dynamics without ever learning what is in the beaker. Real BZ also runs down: in a closed dish the reagents are consumed and the oscillation fades and stops, typically over tens of minutes. The Oregonator treats bromate and malonic acid as an inexhaustible pool — the standard reduction, and the reason this dish can tick forever.

Simplifications and bends, named

Timed pacemakers in three presets. Target Pacemakers, Double-Slit and Labyrinth do not wait for waves to start themselves: code on a timer (periods 5.4, 6.1 and 7.0 τ in Targets) stamps activator into fixed spots whenever the local catalyst is recovered. Real BZ target patterns do arise from dust specks and heterogeneities acting as pacemakers, so the device is chemically motivated — but in this file the rhythm is imposed by a clock in the code, and the probe measurement above (5.40 τ, matching the fastest timer) shows it. The waves between the stamps are genuine reaction–diffusion; the metronome is not.

“CSTR” without the flow. The stirred mode is labelled a CSTR (continuous stirred-tank reactor), but no inflow or outflow terms exist in its equations — nothing flows in or out. It is a well-mixed batch Oregonator: the solver averages the dish, steps the 0D equations on the mean, and mixes the field toward it. The pulsing is real kinetics; the reactor label overclaims the plumbing.

Dimensionless numbers wearing concentration brackets. The telemetry labels read [HBrO₂], [Fe³⁺] and [Br⁻], which in chemistry means molar concentrations. The values are the Oregonator's dimensionless scaled variables, bounded in [0, 1] — no molarity is computed anywhere. The bromide readout is bent further: it is the quasi-steady estimate f·v/(u+q) multiplied by an ad-hoc 0.32 “softly scaled for readout” (the code's own words) and shifted by +0.015 in the denominator. Directionally truthful, numerically decorative.

A positivity-preserving integrator, not the textbook step. The solver advances the reaction with a semi-implicit Patankar-style update and clamps both variables to [0, 1] every substep. That is why it never produces a NaN or a negative concentration across the whole slider range — a genuine virtue — but it means the periods measured above are the periods of this discretisation of the Oregonator, close kin to the ODE's, not a high-precision integration of it. Diffusion is an explicit 9-point Laplacian with zero-flux walls.

Two variables for a many-step reaction. Everything except HBrO₂ and the catalyst's oxidation state — bromide included — is folded into ε, f and q. That is the Oregonator itself, a respected reduction, but it is a reduction: no bromine colour, no CO₂ bubbles from the malonic acid, no reagent consumption.

No temperature, no concentrations. The page exposes no temperature slider and no reagent-concentration sliders at all. The Chemistry tab's controls are the abstract ε, f, q and the diffusion ratio Dv/Du — powerful, and one step removed from anything a bench chemist would pour. A real BZ period is famously temperature-sensitive (Arrhenius kinetics); here that whole axis is absent, not faked.

Palettes are paint, two of them borrowed. Colour is a 128 × 128 lookup table over (u, v) — it reports the state, it does not feed back into it, and switching palettes changes nothing chemical. The Cerium palette's indigo-to-champagne is artistic (real cerium flips between near-colourless Ce(III) and yellow Ce(IV)), and the Briggs–Rauscher palette is a different oscillating reaction entirely — iodine and starch — offered here purely as a colourway for a bromate system that contains neither.

Presets retune the kinetics. Each curated preset silently sets its own ε, f, q and diffusion ratio, so “Spiral Breakup” is partly a parameter change wearing a scene's name. The sliders do show the new values after a preset loads — the retune is visible if you look, silent if you don't.

How it's built — the coding choices

One HTML file. The state lives in two pairs of Float32Array grids (240 × 170, ping-ponged each step so neighbours always read the same instant), plus a wall mask for the glass obstacles and a dish mask for the optional circular boundary. Each frame the solver substeps adaptively — the substep is capped by both the diffusion stability limit and a fraction of ε — so dragging ε across its range cannot destabilise it. The period readout in the corner is not decoration either: it times real upward crossings of u = 0.36 at the probe and smooths them, the same measurement this audit repeated from outside. The audit's method, for the record: the solver was lifted out of the shipped file unchanged, run in Node with the page's UI stubbed away, and driven through the runs tabulated above — the game file itself was never edited.
