Catalysis — the lesson

The game — full title Catalysis: Collision Theory, Activation Energy & Le Chatelier Lab — puts a gas of molecules in a piston chamber and lets you heat it, squeeze it, inject reactants or products, and lay down a platinum or iron catalyst bed. Underneath the animation sit three real ideas: reactions happen when collisions carry enough energy and the right orientation (collision theory), the fraction of collisions that qualify grows exponentially with temperature (the Arrhenius equation), and a disturbed equilibrium shifts to partly undo the disturbance (Le Chatelier's principle). This page is the other half of the game: those laws stated plainly, and then the audit — what the shipped simulation actually does when you run it, measured, including the places where it bends the chemistry.

How this audit was done

The game's JavaScript was lifted out of the shipped file and run headlessly in Node with the browser parts stubbed out, with two hooks added to the extracted copy only (the shipped file was never edited): a counter on every forward and reverse reaction event, and a handle on the simulation state so the audit could set temperatures, trim molecule counts, toggle the catalyst, and fire the game's own Inject and Compress buttons. Every number below was measured from the running code over simulated seconds, usually across several random seeds — not read off the code and not taken from the game's own readouts, except where the readout itself is what is being checked.

Collision theory: rate is collisions × energy × orientation

A reaction event needs three things at once: two molecules must meet, the meeting must carry at least the activation energy Ea along the line of centres, and the molecules must be oriented so the right atoms touch — the steric factor p. The game implements all three literally: molecules fly, collide elastically, and a collision reacts only if ½μvrel² along the collision axis clears the barrier and an orientation check passes. That machinery gets the concentration law right, measured:

Setup (460 K, Ea 42, first 4 s)Forward rateWhat the law predicts
38 A + 38 B0.67 /s—
76 A + 76 B2.92 /srate = k[A][B]: doubling both → ×4. Measured ×4.4 ✓

Steric p = 0.903.08 /sTripling p should roughly triple the rate. Measured ×1.37 ✗ bent

The steric bend has a visible cause in the code: the orientation check passes a collision anyway whenever a random roll beats p × 0.25, and the geometric threshold is mapped so the pass rate saturates. Turning the p slider from 0.30 to 0.90 is real, but it is a volume knob turned down — you get about a third of the effect the label promises.

Temperature and the Arrhenius equation

k = A · e−Ea/RT The rate constant grows exponentially as temperature rises, because the fraction of molecules with energy above Ea sits on the exponential tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. R = 0.008314 kJ/(mol·K) in the real world.

Measured forward rates for A + B ⇌ AB at the stated Ea of 42 kJ/mol, three seeds each:

TemperatureMeasured forward rate
300 K0.73 /s
460 K2.80 /s
640 K5.67 /s

The direction and the accelerating shape are right. But extract the activation energy the way an experimentalist would — from the slope of ln(rate) against 1/T, using the real gas constant — and the model's effective Ea comes out at ≈ 9.6 kJ/mol, not the 42 kJ/mol on the slider. The reason is declared in the game's own code, in a comment: it does not use the real gas constant. Its R_EFF = 0.0285 is 3.43 times the real R, "calibrated so Boltzmann tails are visually active" across the 160–960 K slider. Hotter-feeling molecules at every setting is what makes the chamber lively at 460 K — with the real R and a 42 kJ/mol barrier, almost nothing would ever react on screen. In the model's own scaled units the extracted Ea is ≈ 33 kJ/mol; the remaining gap to 42 is honest physics, not a fudge — only the component of collision velocity along the line of centres counts toward the barrier, so the effective slope sits below the headline threshold. Read the temperature slider as a relative dial — hotter is faster, exponentially — not as a thermometer you could check an Arrhenius plot against.

The Boltzmann panel disagrees with itself

The Inspect tab plots the energy distribution f(E) ∝ (E/kT²)·e−E/kT and shades the tail above Ea, but the number it prints for that tail is computed as plain e−Ea/kT — a different formula from the curve it shades. At 460 K the label reads 4.1%; the true area of the shaded tail of the plotted curve is (1 + Ea/kT)·e−Ea/kT = 17.1%. At 300 K it is 0.74% printed against 4.35% shaded. The picture is the correct one; the caption undercounts it by roughly a factor of four.

The catalyst: right on the diagram, shy in the chamber

A catalyst offers a lower-barrier path. It must lower the forward and reverse barriers by the same amount, leave ΔH untouched, and therefore leave the equilibrium constant — and the equilibrium position — exactly where it was. It only gets you there faster. On paper the game is exactly right, measured from its own barrier function at the default settings:

Ea,fwd 42 → 23.1 kJ/mol · Ea,rev 64 → 45.1 kJ/mol Both barriers drop by the same 18.9 kJ/mol (the stated 45% cut), ΔH stays at −22, and Ea,rev = Ea,fwd − ΔH holds before and after. Keq is therefore unchanged ✓ — the one catalyst error a teaching sim must never make, avoided.

In the running chamber, though, the catalyst is much weaker than the diagram promises. The lowered barrier applies only to collisions involving an adsorbed molecule or taking place within about 26 pixels of the catalyst bed at the floor; the great majority of collisions happen in the bulk gas and pay full price. Measured time for AB to first reach 40 molecules from the standard start, three seeds each: catalyst off 16.0, 12.5, 19.5 s; catalyst on 15.5, 16.5, 12.5 s — no measurable speedup. And the equilibrium position moves a little, against the rule: in paired seeds the settled AB count was 64.3 with the bed off against 66.7 on, and 64.4 against 67.7 — a small but consistent forward nudge. The cause is an asymmetry the diagram hides: on the surface, adsorbed molecules are pre-oriented, boosting the forward steric factor by ×1.25, while the reverse reaction (an AB molecule torn apart by a collision) has no steric gate to boost — its gate is a flat 42% coin flip. A catalyst that helps one direction's aim and not the other's shifts the balance it is supposed to leave alone. Small here — a few molecules in ~65 — but it is the game's one genuine equilibrium error, and it is in the catalyst, the exhibit's headline act.

Equilibrium and Le Chatelier: mostly, the shifts are real

Left alone at 460 K, the A + B ⇌ AB chamber settles at about 64–68 AB molecules. The game's own van 't Hoff formula, Keq = 1.85·e−ΔH/ReffT = 9.91 at 460 K, predicts ≈ 68 — the particle equilibrium and the stated Keq agree. Then the stresses, each applied to an equilibrated chamber and followed in the running code:

StressMeasured responseVerdict
Inject reactants (+20 A, +20 B)AB climbs 68 → 86 over ~90 s; the formula predicts ≈ 85✓ shifts right, right amount
Inject products (+22 AB)AB spikes to ~90, then falls back through 83 and keeps falling — net reverse reaction eats the injection✓ shifts left
Heat the exothermic reaction, 460 → 750 KAB falls 68 → ~58; Keq falls 9.91 → 5.18, predicting ≈ 60✓ heat shifts an exothermic equilibrium left
Heat the endothermic preset, 640 → 900 KAB falls 86 → 83 in 25 s and keeps drifting down as decomposition (Keq 0.72 → 1.02) takes over✓ direction right, slow
Compress, Δn = −1 (V 1.00 → 0.50 L)AB first plunges 68 → 49, then recovers over ~120 s to ~69; the equilibrium prediction at the new volume is ≈ 73✗ wrong-way plunge first
Compress the pressure-neutral HI preset (Δn = 0)HI dips 99 → 81 instead of holding its composition✗ should be unmoved

The compression failure has a mechanical cause worth understanding, because the physics it gestures at is real: a moving piston does work on a gas and heats it. The game's piston imparts its velocity to molecules it catches — but the Compress button animates the volume change so fast that the measured gas temperature spiked to 5,544 K against a 460 K setpoint in the first two seconds. That thermal explosion dissociates AB wholesale (heat shifts this exothermic equilibrium left), and the chamber then spends two minutes re-forming what the squeeze knocked apart. Real compression heating is gentle and the thermostat here is not the problem; the piston simply moves at a speed no hydraulic press would survive. Drag the piston slowly by hand and the shift behaves far better than the button does.

One more honest timescale note: equilibria here are slow. The hydrogen iodide preset was still approaching its equilibrium after 240 simulated seconds — its Q had climbed from 0.08 to 2.45 against a stated Keq of 6.28, direction right, arrival not yet. Patience is genuinely part of the chemistry; just know the "Dynamic Equilibrium" pill can light up on the strength of balanced rates long before the composition has finished travelling.

The Haber preset's quiet shortcut

N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 is a four-molecule reaction; a true four-body collision essentially never happens, in this chamber or in nature (the real reaction proceeds step by step on the iron surface). The game fakes it openly in its mechanics: an N2–H2 collision that clears the barrier captures any two further H2 molecules within a 94-pixel radius and consumes all four at once. It works — measured 32 forward events in 30 s, NH3 rising 16 → 66 — and it makes the rate grow with H2 density in roughly the mass-action way. But the radius is a net, not a collision: at high density it can harvest H2 molecules that were nowhere near the event, which is part of why Haber kinetics in this room feel generous. The preset's ΔH also deserves a footnote: the slider's −24 kJ/mol is a scaled model value; the real Haber ΔH is about −92 kJ per 2 mol of NH3 formed. All four presets' energies live on the same compressed scale as the temperature dial — internally consistent, externally approximate.

Simplifications and bends, named in one place

Stated plainly: (1) The gas constant is 3.43× the real value by design, so the temperature dial is relative: the stated 42 kJ/mol barrier behaves, against real R, like ≈ 9.6 kJ/mol. (2) The Maxwell–Boltzmann panel's printed "E ≥ Ea" percentage uses a simpler formula than the curve it shades and undercounts the shaded tail about fourfold (4.1% printed vs 17.1% shaded at 460 K). (3) The steric slider delivers about a third of its labelled effect — tripling p raised the measured rate only ×1.37, because the orientation check has a random bypass. (4) The catalyst's lowered barrier applies only at the bed, so the bulk rate — and the time to equilibrium — barely changes when it is switched on. (5) The catalyst slightly shifts equilibrium forward (+2 to +3 AB molecules in paired seeds), because surface pre-orientation boosts the forward steric factor while the reverse reaction has no steric gate; a real catalyst shifts equilibrium not at all. (6) The reverse reaction is not the forward reaction run backwards: AB is split by collision with any molecule, gated by a flat 42% roll and no orientation check — an asymmetry that is also why several equilibria settle a few molecules short of the stated Keq. (7) The Compress button slams the piston hard enough to spike the gas to a measured 5,544 K, knocking the equilibrium the wrong way before it recovers; compression is the one Le Chatelier stress the game gets transiently wrong. (8) Preset ΔH and Ea values are on a compressed model scale (Haber ΔH −24 here, ≈ −92 kJ/mol in reality). What survives all of that: the concentration law is quantitatively right (×4.4 measured for ×4 predicted), heating and injection shift equilibria in the right direction by about the right amount, the catalyst lowers both barriers equally and leaves ΔH alone, and the particle equilibrium lands where the game's own Keq formula says it should.
