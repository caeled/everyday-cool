Folding — the lesson

A protein is a chain that finds a shape. The sequence of amino acids along the chain decides which shape is comfortable, water pushes the oily parts inside, and the folded shape is the one that does the work — heat it enough and the shape comes undone, which is denaturation. The exhibit behind this page simulates all of that with beads and springs, and this lesson is its audit: what the model in the code actually is, what it measurably does when you run it for a long time, and — plainly — where its claims bend. The folding is real dynamics. The denaturation, measured, is not. Both halves of that sentence are receipts, below.

What the model honestly is

It is not the classic HP lattice model, though it is a cousin. In the HP model a chain sits on a lattice, every residue is just H (hydrophobic) or P (polar), and the energy is a clean count: −1 for every H–H pair that ends up touching. This exhibit is an off-lattice, two-dimensional bead-and-spring simulation. Each residue is a bead moving freely in the plane, joined to its neighbours by harmonic springs (rest length 21 units, stiffness 155) with a bond-angle bending stiffness on top. There are four bead types, not two — the 38-residue Globular sequence is PHHHPP+HHP-HHPPPHHH+PP-HHHPPHH+PP-HHPP: 17 H, 15 P, 3 positively charged (+), 3 negatively charged (−). Motion is Langevin dynamics: velocity-Verlet steps plus a thermostat that kicks each bead with random noise whose size grows with temperature and damps its velocity — a fair picture of being jostled by solvent.

The energy is a sum of invented wells, not a contact count:

E ≈ springs + angles − H–H wells − native-pair wells − salt bridges + steric bumps An H–H pair within range feels a Gaussian well centred at 28 units plus a long-range "funnel" pull; its energy contribution is −(2.6 × well + 0.5 × funnel) × hydrophobic strength × solvent polarity — a smooth amount that depends on distance, not a fixed −1 per contact. There is no stated amount per contact anywhere on the page, and the audit confirms none exists in the code: the terms carry ad-hoc scale factors (bond energy ×0.015, angle energy ×0.08) chosen so the display looks reasonable.

Two further ingredients matter for honesty. First, the code pre-seeds native pairs — 44 of them for the globular chain: helix-style i→i+4 pairs plus selected long-range H–H pairs — and pays an energy bonus when those specific pairs come together. That is a Gō-like bias: the target fold is partly written into the force field, so folding here is funnelled by construction, not purely emergent from the sequence. Second, "hydrogen bonds" in the readout are a proximity count: any two residues at least 3 apart in sequence that come within a distance cutoff, capped at 2 per residue. There are no donors, acceptors, or bond angles — closeness is being called a hydrogen bond.

Measured: the folding works

The audit extracted the shipped JavaScript and ran it headlessly (Node, with the page's DOM and canvas stubbed), driving the real presets for thousands of frames at the game's own step size. Five fresh Globular Fold runs at 295 K:

Every run collapses from an extended coil to a compact globule and the energy falls by roughly a factor of ten: final energy ≤ initial energy in every run, by a wide margin. But the five runs do not land on the same answer — final energies spread from 277 to 400 and contact counts from 71 to 77. That spread is the honest signature of a real search getting stuck in different near-native traps: local minima exist here, and the dynamics falls into whichever one it meets first. Nothing in the code guarantees the global minimum, and nothing should — real folding has the same character, which is why the funnel matters.

Two controls confirm the collapse is caused by the stated forces, not by the stage scenery. With hydrophobic, H-bond and electrostatic strengths all set to zero, the same coil barely moves: Rg 36.6 → 31.8 after 2,000 frames, with 0 H-bonds and 2 H–H contacts — no collapse. And flipping the solvent polarity to −1 (nonpolar "oil") genuinely turns the fold inside-out: after 1,500 frames the chain stays compact (Rg 9.1 → 8.8) but its H–H contacts crash from 72 to 10, because in oil the polar residues are the ones driven into the core. The β-hairpin preset also does what it says, more slowly: Rg 35.0 → 10.4 and 19 counted H-bonds after 2,500 frames at 285 K.

Measured: the denaturation does not happen

This is the exhibit's headline claim — a Heat Denature button, a Thermal Sweep preset that promises to "melt" the protein at 590 K and refold it on cooling — and it is the claim the audit could not make true. The thermostat itself is honest: with all forces switched off, the beads' root-mean-square speed rises with temperature exactly as it should, 12.9 at 150 K, 19.1 at 295 K, 33.6 at 650 K (roughly √T scaling). The problem is the other side of the tug-of-war: the attractive wells are so strong relative to that noise that the folded state never lets go.

There is no melting curve and no transition temperature in the shipped code: Rg, contact count and H-bond count are flat from 285 K to 650 K. Worse, the page's own status pill can only say "Denatured" when the temperature is ≥455 K and the H-bond count or Rg has actually collapsed — conditions the physics never produces — so during the Thermal Sweep the banner announces melting while the telemetry keeps reporting a native fold. Real proteins mostly denature between about 40 °C and 80 °C (313–353 K); this model's slider runs to 650 K, far past any real protein's survival, and its protein survives all of it. What heat honestly does here is make the folded globule jitter faster in place.

Measured: membranes assemble — with a stagehand

The lipid mode is a genuine rule-driven self-assembly, not an animation. Each lipid is a 3-bead amphiphile — one polar head, two hydrophobic tail beads — and the forces segregate by type: tail–tail pairs attract (a Gaussian well), head–tail pairs repel, heads in water attract weakly at a preferred spacing, and in oil the rules invert so heads cluster instead. Starting from the dispersed "soup," 2,500 frames at 295 K in water took the system from 6 small clusters covering 37% of lipids to 2 clusters with mean size 38 and 100% of lipids shielded — the tails really do hide from the water by the interaction rules.

But two stagehands help. The soup spawns inside a compact arena, so it starts out already 37% clustered; and the code applies a soft containment dish plus a constant inward "osmotic" pressure that herds every lipid toward the centre whether or not any force calls it there. A control run with hydrophobic strength set to zero still ended 95% shielded — the head–tail repulsion has a strength-independent base term and the herding does the rest — so the assembly is real but assisted, and easier than the page implies. Note also what assembles: one or two big blobs containing essentially all 76 lipids, not a population of micelles; and a neatly seeded bilayer, left alone for 1,000 frames, breaks up into 4 clusters. The inverted case works as advertised: in oil the same soup forms 6 smaller clusters (mean size 12) organised around head cores — inverted micelles, by the rules.

Measured: the elastomer recoils, crosslinks barely matter

The polymer mode is six 18-bead chains pinned between two clamps, with up to 30 sulfur cross-link springs between neighbouring chains. Stretched to a span of 630 and released, the clamps snap back to a span of about 243 — real recoil, driven by the springs. The surprise is what does not drive it: with the S–S crosslinks switched off, the same release lands at 242, indistinguishable. Held stretched at fixed clamps, the crosslinked network's energy and tension beat the uncrosslinked one by only about 2% (27,272 vs 26,747; 694 vs 681 in the page's tension units) — the backbone springs do nearly all the work, so the prominent Vulcanized ON/OFF toggle is close to cosmetic in the mechanics. One claim does check out, weakly: at a fixed span, tension rises with temperature — 236 at 200 K, 248 at 315 K, 256 at 550 K — the right direction for the entropic (Gough–Joule) effect the page names, at about a 9% size over a range where a real entropic spring would grow far more.

Simplifications and bends, named

Denaturation is claimed but not simulated: measured above — at every temperature up to 650 K the folded protein stays folded (Rg ≈ 9 Å, H-bonds and H–H contacts unchanged), and heating a half-zipped hairpin finishes folding it. The Heat Denature button, the Thermal Sweep's "MELTED" phase and the banner text "hydrogen bonds snapping!" describe an event the physics never delivers. There is also no chemical denaturant (no urea slider) — the only perturbations are temperature and solvent polarity.

The energy is not a free energy and not in kJ: the readout says "Free Energy E_pot … kJ," but the number is a potential-energy sum only — no entropy term, so it is not a free energy — built from terms with display-chosen scale factors. Moving one residue 500 units away (stretching its two backbone springs) swung the readout by over a million "kJ," which is the springs talking, not chemistry. Treat the number as a score that goes down as the fold improves, nothing more.

The H-bond counter can exceed its own target: folded globular runs report "28 / 15" — the numerator counts every close pair (native or not), while the denominator for the globular preset is a heuristic (42% of chain length), not the 44 native pairs the code actually seeded. A percentage over 100% is the tell that the two numbers come from different definitions.

The Salt-Bridge Lock preset ends with no salt bridges: in the audit's 2,500-frame run it folded fine (Rg 35.6 → 10.0) but finished with 0 active salt bridges; the globular preset, which makes no such promise, fluctuated between 2 and 5. Salt bridges here are transient decorations, not the lock the preset name claims.

Ångströms are a costume: Rg is the true radius of gyration of the bead positions multiplied by 0.22 to land in a plausible Å range; the lipid mode then displays the same quantity ×0.1 as "nm." The changes are real; the units are set dressing.

Two dimensions, four letters, implicit water: real proteins fold in 3D from 20 amino acids, and the hydrophobic effect is water's entropy, not a pairwise attraction — here water is never simulated (the drifting chevrons are decoration), "hydrophobic attraction" is a direct bead-to-bead well whose sign flips with the polarity slider, and a weak centring tether quietly keeps the protein from wandering off-screen. None of that makes the collapse fake — the controls above show the rules doing the work — but it is a cartoon of the mechanism, and the native-pair bias means the cartoon was shown a picture of the answer first.

What is plainly true on the page: folding is driven by burying hydrophobic residues away from water; the folded state is compact, lower in energy, and reached by a stochastic search that can trap; membranes form because amphiphiles segregate heads from tails, and invert when the solvent inverts; a cross-linked network recoils when stretched. Those are the real ideas, and the simulation genuinely enacts them — it is the melting, the units, and a few labels that outrun the code.
