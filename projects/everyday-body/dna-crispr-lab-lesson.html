DNA & CRISPR Lab — the lesson

The exhibit — it calls itself Helix on its own canvas — is a three-room molecular lab: a DNA replication fork with proofreading, a Central Dogma room that transcribes a gene and folds the protein on a live ribosome, and a CRISPR–Cas9 room that scans for PAM sites, checks a 20-nucleotide guide, cuts, and repairs. Underneath all of it is one idea: life runs on a four-letter code read in three-letter words, and every rule in the code can be written down and checked. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the codon table, the worked answers, and the audit: the exhibit's own shipped code, driven headlessly and diffed base-by-base against the standard genetic code and the established facts of Cas9. The headline is a split verdict: the genetic code in this lab is perfect — 64 of 64 codons, zero mismatches — and its flagship CRISPR precision-edit preset can never fire.

⚠ False claims found — read these first

1. The “CRISPR HDR Edit” preset can never cut. Its guide RNA is shifted by one base. The preset promises to target the sickle-cell GTG and repair it to wild-type GAG via a donor template. The audit drove the exhibit's own PAM-check state machine with the preset's own DNA and guide: the DNA carries two PAM sites (TGG at index 20, AGG at index 23), and the preset guide scores 3/20 at both — far under the lab's own 17/20 acceptance line — so Cas9 rejects each PAM and scans forever. No R-loop, no cut, no repair. The guide is the right sequence in the wrong window: the guide that scores 20/20 at the AGG PAM is AGUGCACCUGACUCCUGUGG (DNA bases 3–22); the preset ships GUGCACCUGACUCCUGUGGA (bases 4–23), one base too far downstream — it even swallows the PAM's first base as its last letter. The repair machinery underneath is innocent: force the cut at that PAM (index 20) and the donor lands exactly right, rewriting CCTGTGGAG → CCTGAGGAG — GTG (Val) corrected to GAG (Glu). The preset's aim is off by one; its scissors and its donor are fine.

2. The NHEJ “indel” is not an indel. After a knockout cut, the banner announces “Error-prone indel introduced → Gene Knockout achieved!” and the repair summary reads “NHEJ Knockout (3 bp Indel).” Driven on the knockout preset's own DNA at its real cut (index 21), the repair changed the sequence's length by zero — 36 nt in, 36 nt out — and simply substituted three bases at the cut (AGG → TAC at indices 20–22). An indel inserts or deletes bases and shifts the reading frame; a 3-base substitution does neither. Real NHEJ repair is famous precisely for random insertions and deletions. What the lab does is a tidy 3-letter rewrite wearing an indel's name — the gene is scrambled at the cut, but no frame is shifted and nothing was inserted or deleted.

Everything else that was measured — the full codon table, base pairing, transcription, both worked mutation presets, the knockout preset's PAM scan and cut position — checked out against driven output, as the proofs below record. One thing the lab simply does not offer, so there was nothing to test: there is no reverse-complement tool anywhere in it (the word “reverse” never appears in the file). It shows you the complementary strand base-by-base along the helix, but it never writes a strand backwards — which is the half of “reverse complement” that makes it reverse.

What this is

DNA is a text in four letters — A, T, C, G — where A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds) and C pairs with G (3). Because the pairing is strict, either strand specifies the other, which is how a cell copies the text (replication), and how it reads it: RNA polymerase builds a messenger RNA copy of a gene — same text as the gene's coding strand, with U in place of T — and the ribosome reads that message in three-letter words called codons. There are exactly 4³ = 64 codons, and the table that maps them to amino acids is one of the most conserved facts in biology. That completeness is what makes this lab auditable the way a truth table is: 64 codons is a small enough universe to test all of it. This audit did.

  • Base pairing is the copying rule: A↔T, C↔G. Get one letter of it wrong and every copy, transcript, and guide in the building is wrong.
  • Transcription is DNA → mRNA. The convention that matters: the sequence you type into this lab is the coding strand, 5′→3′, so the mRNA is that same sequence with T→U. (The polymerase physically reads the other, template strand — and because that strand is the coding strand's complement, the message comes out as a copy of the coding strand. The lab labels its input “Gene Coding Sequence (5′→3′)”, so its convention is stated, not hidden.)
  • Translation starts at the first AUG (Met, the start codon) and stops at the first stop codon — UAA, UAG, or UGA. The traps in every hand-drawn codon table are the same few codons: AGA and AGG are Arg (not Ser, not stop), and UGA is a stop (not Trp — that confusion comes from mitochondrial codes, where UGA really does mean Trp).
  • CRISPR–Cas9 is a programmable pair of scissors. The Cas9 protein only sits down where it finds a PAM — for the common SpCas9, the motif 5′-NGG-3′ on the strand downstream (3′ side) of the 20-base target — then it unzips the target, checks it against its guide RNA, and cuts both strands about 3 bp upstream of the PAM. The cell's repair crews then either glue the break sloppily (NHEJ — usually knocking the gene out) or copy a supplied donor template (HDR — a precise edit).

CRISPR, measured — where the cut actually lands

The page's central CRISPR claim is printed in its own reference panel: Cas9 requires a 5′-NGG-3′ PAM “before R-loop unzipping and HNH/RuvC cleavage 3 bp upstream of PAM.” The audit measured each clause against the shipped code:

PAM at index 24 (5′-TGG-3′) → cutSiteIdx = 24 − 3 = 21 → break between bases 20 | 21 Measured by driving updateCrispr() itself: with the knockout preset loaded, Cas9 first checked the decoy AGG PAM at index 20, scored the guide 3/20, released it, and kept scanning — then locked onto the TGG at 24 with a 20/20 guide match and set cutSiteIdx = targetPamIdx − 3. Three bases (indices 21, 22, 23) sit between the break and the PAM, which is the canonical blunt SpCas9 cut. The PAM is looked for in the right place, too: the finder only accepts a PAM when a full 20-base target fits immediately upstream of it, so the PAM is always on the 3′ side of its protospacer, as it should be — and the guide is compared against the strand that carries the PAM, which is the strand a real guide RNA matches.
  • The acceptance line is a flat 17/20. At 17 matches or better, this Cas9 unwinds and cuts; at 16 it walks away (measured: a 4-mismatch guide scores 16/20 and is rejected). That number is a chosen threshold in the code, not a computed efficiency or a measured off-target probability — the page never claims a percentage for it, and now you know not to read one into it.
  • Position does not matter to it — and in a real cell, position is nearly everything. Three mismatches in the guide's last three letters — the PAM-proximal “seed” region, where a real Cas9 is least forgiving — scored the same 17/20 and were accepted and cut, identically to three mismatches at the guide's far end, where real Cas9 often tolerates them. The lab's off-target model is a counting model: honest as a first picture (“more mismatches, less cutting”), but the seed rule is the very next lesson, and it isn't in this room yet.
  • “Auto-Match PAM” matches the first PAM, not the best or nearest in any scored sense. Driven on the knockout DNA, it built a perfect 20/20 guide — for the decoy AGG site at index 20, the first site in the list. The button does what it says only if “the PAM” means “the first PAM.”

Bends named honestly

With no AUG, the ribosome starts anyway. The Sequence tab says codons translate “from the first AUG start codon.” Driven on GGGGGG — no AUG anywhere — the translator quietly started at base 0 and returned Gly–Gly. Starting at the first AUG when one exists is real; the fallback is a classroom convenience the note doesn't mention.

Invalid letters are deleted, not rejected — and deletion moves the frame. Feed the translator ATGXXXGCTAAATAA and the X's vanish silently, giving a clean Met–Ala–Lys–STOP. In a real sequence file a stray character is an error to stop on; here it is absorbed, and in a longer sequence quietly absorbing a letter shifts every codon after it.

The anticodon is written without its direction. The tRNA that serves codon AUG is labelled with anticodon UAC — the per-base complement, in the codon's own order. That string is correct read 3′→5′, the way an anticodon actually sits against the codon; read 5′→3′, the way sequences are conventionally written, the same anticodon is CAU (the reverse complement). The lab never says which direction it's writing in — a small silence in exactly the place where direction is the whole point.

The frameshift preset's bookkeeping is +1 and −1. Its banner says a +1 insertion after codon 3 — and there is indeed an inserted T there, and the promised premature UAA stop really does arrive at codon 4. But the mutant sequence is 36 nt, exactly the wild type's length: a base was also dropped at the far end to keep the string the same size. The biology on screen is honest; the ledger underneath quietly nets to zero.

The “Fidelity” readout is a display formula, not a measurement. In the replication room it starts at 99.9% and subtracts a flat 2.8 points per persisted mismatch, floored at 82% — whether the fork has copied ten bases or ten thousand. In the Dogma room the same label shows the percentage of translated amino acids that aren't mutated. Neither number is computed from an error rate; both are dials that move when something happens, drawn to look like instruments. (Real Pol III with proofreading is far better than 99.9% — on the order of one error in a hundred thousand bases or more.)

A PAM with fewer than 20 bases upstream is invisible. The finder starts looking at index 20, because a guide needs its full 20-base target. A genuine NGG at index 19 of a short custom sequence will never be reported. That's a defensible design choice for a Cas9 room — such a site couldn't be targeted by this guide length anyway — but the count in the corner (“PAM sites found”) means “targetable PAM sites found.”

Sickle-cell numbering counts the starter Met. The changed codon is the 7th codon of the sequence as typed, ATG included — the banner's “codon 7” is right on that counting. You will also meet this same mutation in textbooks as Glu6Val, counted after the initiator Met is trimmed from the finished protein. Both numbers name the same base; the audit verified the base itself (index 19, A→T) rather than the numbering.

One tooltip's wording runs ahead of its mechanism. RNA polymerase is described as synthesising “a complementary 5′→3′ mRNA strand.” The mRNA the lab actually builds is a copy of the coding strand (T→U) — complementary to the template strand the polymerase reads, identical to the strand you're shown. The output is exactly right; the sentence is true only once you supply which strand it means.
