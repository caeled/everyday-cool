Hodgkin–Huxley Neuron — the lesson

The exhibit runs the real 1952 Hodgkin–Huxley equations — not a cartoon of them — in a 48-compartment axon cable, with a synapse on the end and a spiking-network mode beside it. The single-axon core is genuinely good: resting potential, threshold, all-or-none spikes, gating variables, ionic currents and the refractory period all measured the way the textbooks say they should. But three of its six presets do not do what their names promise, and its myelinated mode is numerically broken at the frame rate the page actually runs at. Verdict up top: trust the unmyelinated axon completely; do not trust the myelin, the burst train, or the top half of the temperature slider.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE — Myelinated “Saltatory Conduction” does not conduct. The preset promises “Myelinated node leaps” and the science card claims myelin makes conduction “~30× faster.” Driven at the page's own frame stepping (0.242 bio-ms per frame at 60 fps), the myelinated cable blows up numerically: compartments pin at the code's own voltage clamps in a frozen checkerboard of +70 mV and −105 mV, the terminal compartment never fires, and no clean spike ever travels node to node — while the telemetry calmly displays a conduction velocity of 120.0 m/s, which is the display formula's own clamp maximum, not a measurement. The cause is in the code: internodes get capacitance 0.075 µF/cm² with a large axial coupling, and the fixed sub-stepping is far too coarse for that stiffness. (Driven 50× finer than the page ever steps, the same code propagates beautifully, node peaks ≈ +24 mV — the physics is right, the integration step is not.) As shipped and as actually run: no saltation, and the “~30×” figure is a ratio of two display formulas, not of two measured propagations.

FALSE — “Tonic Bursting Train” fires once. The preset applies a sustained Iext of 14.5 µA/cm², described as a “Sustained I_ext oscillation.” Driven for a full 2,000 ms of simulated time it produced exactly 1 spike, then settled at about −59 mV and stayed there. A sweep confirms it is not a tuning miss: sustained currents of 12, 13, 16 and 20 µA/cm² each produced exactly one spike in 1,000 ms, and 8–11 µA/cm² produced none. This cable, driven only at its first three compartments against its own axial load, does not fire repetitively at any sustained current tested — so there is no train, tonic or bursting. (The name is also a misnomer: tonic firing and bursting are different patterns, and this preset delivers neither.)

FALSE at the top of the range — the temperature slider kills the neuron it claims to warm. The slider runs 6.3–37 °C under a “Q₁₀ Kinetics” label, and its φ factor is computed correctly (see below). But driven with the standard stimulus pulse, the spike degrades by 25 °C (probe peak only ≈ +14 mV, no full spike) and at 37 °C — body temperature, the top of the slider — there is no spike at all: peak −65.0 mV, zero spikes, at both fine and frame-rate stepping. A student who slides to body temperature learns that neurons stop working there. They don't; this integration does.

OVERSTATED — the “+40 mV” peak. The biophysics card says m-gates open Na⁺ channels “for rapid depolarization (+40 mV).” Measured peak at the probe node under the default preset (18.5 °C) is +26 mV; the highest peak measured at any temperature was +37.5 mV at 6.3 °C. The spike overshoots zero and heads toward ENa exactly as it should — it just never reaches +40 in this cable, whose axial load trims the peak.

What this is

  • The Hodgkin–Huxley model (1952). Hodgkin and Huxley, working on the squid giant axon, wrote the membrane as a capacitor in parallel with three currents — a fast sodium current gated by m³h, a delayed potassium current gated by n⁴, and a leak — and won the Nobel Prize for it. This exhibit implements that model with the original maximal conductances (gNa 120, gK 36, gL 0.3 mS/cm², Cm 1 µF/cm²).
  • A cable, not a point. The axon is 48 coupled compartments, so spikes genuinely propagate: measured travel from compartment 11 to compartment 44 took 5.25 ms in the unmyelinated cable. A probe compartment (22) feeds the oscilloscope and the live current readouts.
  • A synapse and a network. When the terminal compartment fires, Ca²⁺ enters, vesicles dock, and transmitter raises a postsynaptic conductance — excitatory (glutamate, E = 0 mV) or inhibitory (GABA, E = −80 mV). Mode B reuses the same HH neuron in a small network with STDP plasticity; this audit drove Mode A (the HH engine) and did not drive the network mode — see the bends for what is and isn't claimed about it.

How the audit measured it

The shipped script's own state, hhRates(), hhSteadyState(), resetAxon(), injectPulse() and stepCable() — plus its real synaptic-release and particle code — were extracted verbatim and run in Node. Nothing was re-implemented. Each test was run twice: fine-grained (small steps, to read the model's true spike shape) and at the page's real operating regime (0.242 bio-ms per stepCable() call, which is what its animation loop passes at 60 fps). Where the two regimes disagree, the lesson says so — that disagreement is itself one of the findings.

The convention question — which Hodgkin–Huxley is this?

Cm dV/dt = Iext − gNa m³h (V − ENa) − gK n⁴ (V − EK) − gL (V − EL) The equation as the page prints it, and as the code computes it. Hodgkin & Huxley's 1952 paper wrote voltages as displacement from rest (rest = 0, ENa = +115 mV in that shifted frame). This file uses the modern absolute convention instead — rest −65 mV, ENa +50, EK −77 — and, crucially, it re-bases the original rate constants correctly: its hhRates() works in v = V + 65, exactly the shift the 1952 formulas expect. Judged on its own terms, the convention is used consistently everywhere: steady states, telemetry defaults and the leak reversal (−54.387 mV, the classic HH value carried into the modern frame) all agree.

That consistency is worth stating plainly, because mixing the two conventions is the most common way HH demos go quietly wrong. This one doesn't. Its resting steady-state gates (m 0.053, h 0.596, n 0.318) are the values the real equations give at −65 mV, and its spike — threshold near 13 µA/cm² for a brief pulse, +26 mV peak, −74.5 mV undershoot, ≈2 ms absolute refractory period — is the squid axon's behaviour at a cool temperature, which is what 18.5 °C on its slider says it is simulating. At HH's own native 6.3 °C the same code gives a peak of +37.5 mV and a broader spike (half-width 1.41 ms vs 0.37 ms at 18.5 °C) — the temperature dependence of the shape is real physiology, right up until the integration gives out at the warm end.

What's true — the credit side

  • The equations are the real ones, integrated honestly. Gating variables use Rush–Larsen exponential integration (unconditionally stable), the rate constants are the classic HH forms including the removable-singularity guards at v = 25 and v = 10, and nothing in the unmyelinated path is faked: threshold, all-or-none amplitude, refractory failure and recovery all emerged from driving the code, not from reading it.
  • The refractory period is enforced by the biology, not a timer. The second pulse fails because h is still low and n still high — the same mechanism the science card describes (“the h-ball plugs the pore… n-gates… enforce the refractory period”).
  • TTX, glutamate and GABA behave. Sodium block silences the axon completely; the excitatory synapse pulls the postsynaptic cell toward 0 mV and the inhibitory one toward −80 mV, both measured.
  • The synapse is triggered by arrival, not by the soma. Release requires the terminal compartment itself to cross 0 mV — which is why the TTX run, whose soma bump never propagates, produces no postsynaptic response at all.

Bends named honestly

The conduction-velocity readout is a display formula, not a measurement. The cable's 48 compartments have no length in µm anywhere in the code, so metres per second cannot be computed from the model. The code instead blends a “physiological scale” constant into the travel time when a spike happens, drifts toward a formula target (66 or 2.3 × φ0.35 × √(gNa/120)) when none does, and hard-codes 70.0 / 2.8 m/s in network mode. Treat every m/s number on the page as an illustration of the right ballpark (unmyelinated ~1–3 m/s, myelinated tens of m/s), never as a result.

Space clamp vs cable. The page calls its engine a “Hodgkin–Huxley ODE,” and Mode A is really a cable of 48 HH patches — richer than the space-clamped original, and the reason propagation can be tested at all. No page text claims space-clamp conditions, so nothing is false here; just know the “ODE” badge undersells (and mislabels) what's running.

The GABA preset turns myelin on. Buried in its settings (myelinated = true) is a switch its name never mentions. The IPSP itself measured correctly, but the preset routes its spike through the broken myelinated cable to get there.

Peak amplitude is cable-trimmed. A space-clamped HH patch with these parameters peaks higher; here each compartment spends current charging its neighbours, so +26 mV at 18.5 °C is this cable's honest answer. The bend is only in the card's “+40 mV” wording.

Frame-rate aliasing. At 60 fps the page samples its own oscilloscope every 0.12 ms inside the step, but telemetry reads frame-end state; a ~1 ms spike can look smaller on the numeric readouts than on the trace. Both are the same spike.

Mode B (spiking network & STDP) was not driven in this audit. Its neurons reuse the same HH engine audited above, and its science card states the standard STDP rule (pre just before post, Δt > 0, strengthens). Its conduction-velocity readout is hard-coded (70.0 / 2.8 m/s), and its learning claims are unverified here — not flagged false, flagged untested.

One neuron, one synapse, no pumps. There is no Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, no ion concentration dynamics (reversal potentials are constants, so spikes can fire forever without running anything down), and the postsynaptic cell is a single passive compartment that can be pulled to −7.6 mV by one EPSP — a very strong synapse. Standard simplifications for a teaching model; named here so nobody mistakes them for the whole cell.
