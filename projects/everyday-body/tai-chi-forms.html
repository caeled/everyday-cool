What this is. A small exhibit about one move — redirection — shown four ways in Tai Chi and four more ways across this collection. The forms animation runs itself. The bench below it is yours: slide, fire, nudge.

  • The forms strip cycles through the four gates of Grasp the Bird's Tail — Ward Off, Roll Back, Press, Push — while a ribbon of light arrives, bends around the figure, and leaves at a new angle. Nothing is blocked. That is the whole lesson in one picture.
  • The Redirect Bench: a ball meets a round gate. Slide the aim up or down to choose where on the curve it lands; the readout compares the turn you got with the turn geometry promises. Or swap the gate for the padded block and meet the ball head-on — watch what opposition costs.
  • The Resonance Bench: one Nudge button pushes a swing, always along its motion, always the same strength. When you press it decides what it buys — the same push, timed with the swing, is worth many times the same push timed against it.
  • On a touch screen: everything here is buttons and a slider — the whole exhibit plays by tap. The forms strip animates on its own.
  • Honest limits: the figure is a teaching diagram, not instruction in Tai Chi — learn the forms from a teacher, in a body, over time. The physics is drawn true but simple: the bench's ball reflects elastically and loses nothing; real balls, cushions, and bodies all spend a little. What survives every simplification is the shape of the idea — direction is cheaper to change than speed, and timing beats force.

Tai Chi Forms — the same move

Steven's observation, made over his own games: steering a ship by its recoil, banking a shot off a wall, drifting a shadow-plane through a gap, sending a radio signal the long way around the Earth — and a ball off a pool cushion — are essentially the same move. Tai Chi has been teaching that move, in the body, for centuries. This bend shows the forms, then shows the physics wearing four other costumes.

“Its all digital taichi” — Steven's name for the family, coined over Shardstorm's recoil steering and extended the same night to the reflection and shadow benches and to Skip. This exhibit is that sentence, built.

The four gates — Grasp the Bird's Tail, the sequence at Tai Chi's heart

Bench 1 — Redirect, don't oppose — a ball, a curve, and a block

The gate is round, radius 64. Aim dead centre and the ball comes straight back at you. Aim off centre and the curve sends it away turned — at the same speed it arrived with.

Bench 2 — Resonance — the same push, timed

Both buttons push equally hard. One agrees with the swing; the other argues with it. Press each a few times and read what the same strength buys.

Reflection — the cushion

A pool ball leaves a cushion at the angle it arrived, speed nearly intact: the cushion never grabbed it, only turned it. The Physics room's pool table draws both angles live — and Bug Hunt's whole arsenal is this move, shots banked off walls into bugs hiding around corners.

A radio aerial sings when the wave arrives at its natural rhythm, and a swing grows when the push agrees with its timing. DipoleLab is full of this: small signals, exactly timed, adding up to a voice from across the planet. Skip — the long way around

A shortwave signal does not punch through the Earth; it bends off the ionosphere and comes down a continent away — the long way around is the only way there. Skip, the game that started this whole chain of exhibits, is redirection at planetary scale: meet the sky at the right angle and the world turns your signal for you.

Pool — the bank shot

The bank shot is Ward Off with a cue: you do not fight the table's geometry, you borrow it. Aim at the mirror point, let the cushion do the turning, and the ball arrives from a direction the pocket never guarded. The angles still always end up in some form of a polygon — Steven's words, and the reason this exhibit exists.

Why the body belongs in this list: a cushion, a sky, and a swing are honest machines, but the forms are the same geometry practised by the one machine you carry everywhere. Ward Off receives on a curve. Roll Back lets the push spend itself into empty space. Press gathers the line to a point. Push sends — timed with the settle, not against it. Four names for receiving, turning, and returning what arrived, at something like its own speed.
