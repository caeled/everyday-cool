The back-door lesson

Tai Chi Forms — what was built, what was measured, what bends

Where it came from

On the night of 25–26 November 2026, playing the flight-recorder build of Shardstorm, Steven described steering the ship by its own motion — drive, rotation, and brief thrust bursts — as Tai Chi: thrust used as an aim rather than a control. Minutes later he generalised it himself: the recoil works “even the recoils from a bounce… redirection using thrust as an aim even in the reflection and shadow bench. Skip. Its all digitial taichi.” The next message proposed this exhibit: a simple Tai Chi Forms bend showing the forms and relating them to reflection, resonance, Skip, and pool — “why they are essentially the same move” — with its card here in Everyday Body. This page is the receipt for that build.

What was built

One page, three working parts. (1) The forms strip: a drawn figure cycling through the four gates of Grasp the Bird's Tail — Ward Off (掤 Péng), Roll Back (捋 Lǚ), Press (擠 Jǐ), Push (按 Àn) — while a ribbon of light arrives, bends around the lead hand, and leaves at a new angle, over pond rings. (2) The Redirect Bench: a ball fired at a round gate with a chosen aim offset; specular reflection off the circle; a padded-block swap that meets the ball head-on and stops it dead. (3) The Resonance Bench: a pendulum and two equal-strength Nudge buttons, one along the motion and one against it. Four panels then carry the same move to its other addresses — the pool cushion and Bug Hunt's banked shots, DipoleLab's resonance and Shardstorm's recoil steering, Skip's ionospheric bend, and the bank shot — each with its link.

The audit — 17 of 17 checks, on the shipped page

The audit drove the shipped page headlessly through its real controls and its test hooks (window.__tai), and compared the benches against independent geometry:

  • Redirect angles match theory. Fired at offsets 0 / 20 / 38 / 55 px, the ball turned 180.00° / 148.12° / 116.37° / 81.64° against the geometric promise of 180.00° / 148.20° / 117.26° / 82.22° — inside a degree at every offset (the residue is the sim's contact sampling, named below).
  • Speed is conserved exactly through the reflection at every offset: out at 300.00 of 300 px/s. The curve negotiates direction only.
  • The miss case is honest: at offset 100 the ball clears the gate, the bench records a miss, and the readout says so in words.
  • The block case: swapped in, the padded block stops the ball dead — in at 300, out at 0 — the printed contrast with the gate.
  • Resonance behaves as physics says: from a set swing, one along-motion nudge grew the amplitude 22.1° → 27.2°; two against-motion nudges shrank it back to 21.9°; and the displayed amplitude matches the energy formula (½ω² + ω₀²(1 − cos θ)) to a twentieth of a degree.
  • Zero page exceptions across the whole drive.

Two defects the audit caught, fixed before shipping

The theory readout used the wrong circle. The first audit run showed the sim “overturning” at wide offsets (82° measured against a promised 61.5° at offset 55). The sim was right and the promise was wrong: a ball turns off the circle its centre travels — gate radius plus ball radius (64 + 9 = 73) — not the drawn 64. The theory function now uses 73, the page's printed promises match its physics, and this paragraph is the receipt that they didn't, at first.

A first-frame guard. The forms animation threw once, on its very first frame, when the animation clock read earlier than the script's start stamp (a headless-clock quirk a real browser is unlikely to meet). The elapsed time is now clamped at zero; the audit's exception count went from one to none.

What bends — printed on the exhibit, and here

  • The figure is a diagram, not instruction. Four postures interpolated by a drawing are a teaching cartoon of Grasp the Bird's Tail, not the form itself — the real sequence is longer, weighted, and learned from a teacher in a body over time. The exhibit says so in its Help.
  • The reflection is perfectly elastic. Real balls, cushions, and bodies all spend a little speed in the turn. The bench spends none, so the conservation receipt reads exactly 300.00 — the shape of the claim (direction is the cheap part) survives the simplification; the digit is the idealisation.
  • The nudge is an ideal impulse — instant, exactly along or against the motion. A hand on a real swing is neither. The bench's point is the comparison, and the comparison is true: identical strength, opposite timing, opposite results.
  • Contact is sampled, not solved. The sim steps at 240 Hz and reflects at the first sampled contact, which is why measured turns sit up to ~0.9° under the closed-form promise at wide offsets. Printed here rather than tuned away.

Verdict

The exhibit does the one thing it was built to do: it puts the forms and the physics in one frame and lets a visitor feel the identity — turn a thing on a curve and keep its speed; oppose it and spend everything; push in rhythm and small becomes large. Steven named the family “digital taichi” over his own games. This is the family's portrait, hung in the Body room, where the original machine for the move lives.
