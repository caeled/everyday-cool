Heart Hemodynamics — the lesson

A beating-heart simulator with valves, an ECG, a Wiggers diagram and a live pressure–volume loop, driven by sliders for heart rate, contractility, arterial resistance (afterload) and blood volume (preload), plus rhythm presets from Normal Sinus to ventricular fibrillation and a 200 J defibrillator. This page is the other half: the physiology, the numbers measured by driving the shipped code itself, and an honest list of where it bends. Verdict up front: as shipped, this one did not hold up. Its pressure model is genuinely good — but its volume pump was broken at rest: the mitral valve never opened in the shipped code, the ventricle never refilled, and the headline telemetry (70 mL stroke volume, 62% ejection fraction, 5.04 L/min cardiac output) decayed within seconds to the values of a severely failing heart. On 23 November 2026 Steven Powell sanctioned a one-line fix — the valve now opens and the ventricle fills again — and when that repair unmasked a second defect capping ejection, he sanctioned that repair too (two lines). Driven after both fixes, the resting heart now measures exactly the stroke volume and cardiac output printed on its tin — 70 mL and 5.04 L/min — while its ejection fraction reads 53% against the printed ≈ 62%, for an arithmetic reason the second fix section states plainly. The whole story, with receipts, is in “The valve that never opened” and “The ejection that never happened” below; everything else in this lesson describes the exhibit exactly as shipped. This room is an educational simplification, not medical advice.

⚠ False and misleading claims — flagged first

FALSE — the resting telemetry on the tin. The page loads showing 120/80 mmHg, stroke volume 70 mL, ejection fraction 62% and cardiac output 5.04 L/min. Those numbers are the model's initial state, not anything it computes. Driven headlessly, they decay from the moment the simulation starts: by the end of the page's own 3.1-second pre-warm, stroke volume is already 20.8 mL and EF 20%; settled at rest it reads SV ≈ 9 mL, EF 12%, CO ≈ 0.65 L/min at 111/81 mmHg — severe heart-failure numbers on a preset labelled “Normal Sinus.” Open the exhibit, wait ten seconds, and watch the telemetry panel: it does this live, in the browser, exactly as measured here.

FALSE — the mitral valve never opens. The code opens the mitral valve only when left-atrial pressure exceeds left-ventricular pressure by 0.8 mmHg. Measured over 20 simulated seconds at defaults, the largest LA−LV difference reached was −1.54 mmHg — it never even reaches zero, let alone +0.8. The valve's open fraction was 0.000. With no filling phase, ventricular volume can only ratchet down toward its end-systolic floor, which is why every volume-derived number above collapses. The phase label still cheerfully reports “DIASTOLIC FILLING” for a quarter of each cycle while the valve it describes is shut.

FALSE — “Athletic Brady … stroke volume (~105 mL)”. That figure is printed in the preset's own description. Driven and settled, the Athletic Bradycardia preset measures SV 20.2 mL, EF 13.2%, CO 0.97 L/min. The direction is right (its SV is the highest of any preset), the number is out by a factor of five.

MISLEADING FLOORS — EF and SV readouts are clamped, and the clamps are what you see. The code clamps EF to a minimum of 12% and raw stroke volume to a minimum of 8 mL. At rest the unclamped EF is 9.0 ⁄ 118 ≈ 7.6%; the panel displays 12% because that is the floor, in almost every scenario tested — rest, exercise, preload, afterload and contractility sweeps all sit pinned at exactly 12%. A readout sitting on its own clamp is not a measurement, and here it hides how broken the underlying volume model is.

What this is

The heart is a pump whose output is set by how often it beats, how much it ejects per beat, how full it starts (preload), how hard it squeezes (contractility) and what it pushes against (afterload). The exhibit models the left heart beat-by-beat — chamber pressures, valve states decided by pressure gradients, LV volume, a Windkessel aorta — and derives its telemetry once per beat from the simulated extremes. The identities it is built on, and the ones this audit checked, are:

Cardiac output: CO = HR × SV  ·  Ejection fraction: EF = SV ⁄ EDV = (EDV − ESV) ⁄ EDV At rest in a real adult (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology ranges): HR ≈ 70 bpm, SV ≈ 70 mL, CO ≈ 5 L/min, EDV ≈ 120 mL, ESV ≈ 50 mL, EF ≈ 55–70%.
Mean arterial pressure: MAP ≈ DBP + (SBP − DBP) ⁄ 3  ·  and MAP ≈ CO × TPR + CVP The exhibit's HUD uses the first formula — checked below against its own displayed pressures. It has no explicit TPR or CVP readout, so the second identity can only be checked as an implied resistance, (MAP − 5) ⁄ CO.
Frank–Starling: ↑ preload (EDV) → ↑ SV, with ESV roughly unchanged  ·  ↑ afterload → ↓ SV More filling stretches the ventricle and it ejects more; more resistance means less of the beat escapes. Both directions were driven on the exhibit's own sliders.

How this was audited

The exhibit's own shipped stepPhysiology() engine — the exact code in heart-hemodynamics.html (sha256 a3eff107…, guest file untouched; hash re-verified after the audit) — was extracted and driven headlessly in Node at the same 8 ms step the page's own animation loop uses, with presets applied through its own preset buttons and sliders moved through their own input handlers. A test hook in the in-memory copy only exposed the internal state so EDV, ESV and valve fractions could be read; no shipped logic was changed. “Measured” means the model's own settled output after 30–60 simulated seconds unless a decay timeline is stated — early readings are start-up transients, and in this exhibit the transient is the story, so it gets its own table. Reference values are the standard OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology resting ranges. The exhibit file has since been changed under sanction twice (the two fix sections below); it is now sha256 a75bb305…. Every audit table in this lesson describes the as-shipped file, a3eff107…, and all post-fix measurements were taken by driving the fixed files the same way.

The valve that never opened — and the one-line fix

The audit above left this exhibit standing exactly as delivered, its failure documented, because repairing a guest's file is the curator's call, not the auditor's. On 23 November 2026 Steven Powell made that call — “Fix the valve (one line, records updated)” — making this the third sanctioned guest-file edit in the collection, after the Zenith Chess pacing fix and the Orbital Pinball gate fix. The fix was made by Muse, and this section is its record.

The defect, in one line of arithmetic

The code opens the mitral valve only when left-atrial pressure exceeds left-ventricular pressure by 0.8 mmHg. But the ventricle's diastolic pressure is floored at its end-diastolic-pressure term, EDP = 6 + 5 × preload — 11 mmHg at the default 100% preload — while atrial pressure idles at 5.5 × preload, 5.5 mmHg, and the atrial kick that raises atrial pressure also raises ventricular pressure by 0.72× as much. The gradient the valve demands was unreachable by construction: measured over 20 simulated seconds, the largest LA−LV difference was −1.54 mmHg (audit drive; −1.76 in the fix-day re-drive) and the valve's open fraction was 0.000. One offset — a diastolic floor set permanently above the atrium it was supposed to fill from — kept the heart from ever filling.

The change — exactly one line

Before: const edpLV = clamp(6 + 5 * preload + (state.mitralRegurg ? 4 : 0), 4, 22); The end-diastolic LV pressure floor, in the exhibit's pressure section.
After: const edpLV = clamp(5 * preload - 1 + (state.mitralRegurg ? 4 : 0), 4, 22); EDP at 100% preload: 11 → 4 mmHg — the formula's own clamp floor. Nothing else in the file changed.

That restores the physical ordering the whole model assumes: through diastole, atrial pressure now sits above ventricular pressure, which is what a filling gradient is. The valve's own rule — open at +0.8 mmHg — is untouched; it simply became reachable.

What the fix did not do — said plainly

The fixed heart still does not meet the numbers on its tin. SV 19.2 mL against a claimed 70; EF 14.4% against ~62%; CO 1.38 L/min against 5.04. Fixing the valve unmasked a second defect the audit could not see while the ventricle never filled: ejection is capped. The volume code only ejects while the aortic valve is open — about 10% of the cycle (phase ≈ 0.29–0.38, set by the pressure crossover) — but its ejection target is scheduled to sweep from EDV to ESV over phase ≈ 0.27–0.49, so by the time the valve closes the target has only swept about halfway. Measured ESV after the fix is 114 mL against a target ESV of 44 mL: the ventricle fills fully and then can only empty partway before its valve shuts. No setting of the filling side can cure that; it is a separate fault, in a different part of the model, and repairing it is a second change beyond the sanctioned line. It awaits Steven's word, and this lesson does not pretend the exhibit is whole without it.

The ejection that never happened — and the two-line fix

The valve fix above left a heart that filled and could not empty. The same day, 23 November 2026, Steven Powell sanctioned the second repair as well — “Sanction the ejection fix too” — making this the fourth sanctioned guest-file edit in the collection, after the Zenith Chess pacing fix, the Orbital Pinball gate fix and the Cardio valve fix. The fix was made by Muse, and this section is its record.

The defect, in one timing mismatch

The volume code ejects only while the aortic valve is open, and in this model that valve is open about 10% of the cycle — measured open window phase ≈ 0.31–0.39 at rest, set by the pressure crossover (a real aortic valve is open ≈ 30–35% of the cycle; the audit named that bend above). But the ejection target was scheduled to sweep from EDV to ESV across phase ≈ 0.27–0.49 — the whole systolic span. By the time the valve shut, the target had swept only about halfway (≈ 95 mL at close against an ESV target of 44), and the volume's chase toward that moving target (an exponential lerp at 18/s, a ≈ 55 ms time constant, inside a ≈ 67 ms window) lagged further behind it. Measured after the valve fix: the ventricle filled to 133 mL and emptied only to ESV 114 mL — a 19.2 mL stroke volume from a heart holding 133.

The change — exactly two lines, both in the ejection branch

Before: const ejFrac = clamp((phi - (sysStart + 0.04)) / Math.max(0.12, sysDur - 0.05), 0, 1); The sweep's span: ≈ 0.22 of the cycle at rest — more than twice the window it had to fit in.
After: const ejFrac = clamp((phi - (sysStart + 0.04)) / 0.12, 0, 1); The sweep now spans the measured valve-open window — 0.12 of the cycle, the value the code already carried as its own floor before the sysDur term overrode it.
Before: state.vLV = lerp(state.vLV, targetV, clamp(dt * 18, 0, 0.9)); The volume's chase rate: 18/s, a ≈ 55 ms time constant against a ≈ 67 ms window.
After: state.vLV = lerp(state.vLV, targetV, clamp(dt * 45, 0, 0.9)); 45/s — a ≈ 22 ms time constant, about three time constants inside the open window: the same completion standard the filling side meets over diastole, where its 5.5–13/s chase runs for ≈ 0.5 s.

Nothing else in the file changed. The valve rules, the EDV and ESV targets, the pressure model and the telemetry arithmetic are all exactly as shipped.

What still does not hold — said plainly

EF reads 53%, not ≈ 62% — and no ejection-side change can alter that. The tin's 62% is 70 ⁄ 114: the printed stroke volume divided by the model's initial EDV. The model's settled EDV is 132 mL — its own filling target at defaults — and 70 ⁄ 132 = 53%. Both sanctions were repairs to the volume path; neither touched the filling target or the printed claims, so the three numbers on the tin cannot all be true at once in this model. The two the pump actually delivers — stroke volume and cardiac output — now measure exactly as printed.

ESV settles at 62 mL, not the model's target 44. The sweep now completes on schedule, but the exponential chase still leaves the last ≈ 18 mL inside the ≈ 67 ms window. Stroke volume lands on the tin because the model's EDV (132 mL) exceeds the tin's implied EDV (114 mL) by roughly the same amount — two offsets, partially cancelling, recorded here rather than hidden.

Still standing, unchanged by either fix: the Valvulopathy preset's mitral valve never opens (measured open fraction 0.00 after both fixes — its +4 mmHg regurgitation bump on EDP is the valve disease one preset over, and neither sanction reached it); the Athlete preset's ~105 mL claim (measured 90.8); the aortic valve's ≈ 10% open window itself, which is the pressure model's early crossover and the reason the sweep had to be fitted to it; and the EF / SV display clamps, which remain in the code — though in every scenario re-driven after both fixes, the reported EF clears its 12% floor on real arithmetic.

Where it bends — named honestly

The mitral valve can never open (the fatal bend). Diastolic LV pressure is floored at EDP = 6 + 5 × preload (≈ 11 mmHg at 100%) plus an atrial-kick bump, while LA pressure idles at 5.5 × preload (≈ 5.5 mmHg) plus the same a-wave — the gradient the code demands (+0.8 mmHg) is unreachable by construction, at every preload (the gap is ≈ 6 − 0.5 × preload mmHg before the a-wave, and the a-wave raises LV pressure too). Everything downstream — SV, EF, CO, the PV loop, the filling phase — is broken by this one inequality. The tricuspid valve has the same disease in milder form (open 12% of the cycle, on atrial kick only).

Clamps that masquerade as readings. EF is clamped to [12%, 85%] and raw SV to a minimum of 8 mL before display smoothing. Settled EF is the 12% floor in nearly every scenario; settled SV is within a millilitre or two of its floor at rest. Floors are a legitimate way to keep a display sane — undisclosed, sitting exactly where the user looks, they become the finding.

Pressures are slider-driven, not flow-driven. Peak LV pressure is a product of power-law terms in preload, contractility and resistance, and the aorta is a Windkessel chasing it — a good model of pressure that never consults the ejected volume. That is why 111/81 can coexist with a cardiac output of 0.65 L/min, and why the implied TPR of the settled state (≈ 132 mmHg·min/L) is ~8× physiological while the claimed state's (≈ 17) is spot on.

Ejection starts by filling. The ejection branch lerps volume toward a target that begins at EDV, so a ventricle left below EDV by the missing filling phase first gains volume during “ejection,” then loses what little time remains — one more reason the loop is a sliver.

ESV chases EDV. Across the preload sweep ESV rose 76 → 140 mL alongside EDV, flattening the Frank–Starling response. In the physiology, ESV is set by contractility and afterload, roughly independent of preload.

Phase labels outrun the valves. “Diastolic filling” is announced while the mitral valve is shut, and “isovolumetric relaxation” — a phase that really lasts ≈ 60–80 ms — occupies 62% of the cycle, because the label logic has no name for “both valves shut and nothing is happening,” which is this model's diastole.

What is genuinely good. The MAP formula is exact; the Windkessel decay and dicrotic notch are real modelling; valve states are decided by pressure crossovers rather than a timeline, which is the right architecture (and is what makes the mitral bug a one-inequality fix rather than a rewrite); VFib collapse and the defibrillator behave as described; the ESPVR slope tracks contractility; and every slider moves pressure in the physiologically correct direction. The exhibit's skeleton is sound — one pressure offset keeps its heart from ever filling.
