Computing.

A computer is not magic, and it is not one complicated thing — it is a few very simple things, wired together in staggering numbers. Two values: 0 and 1. Six little gates that decide. Wires. This room builds the whole idea on one bench: count in twos, play the gates until their tables fill themselves in, wire two gates into an adder, watch three gates recognise a pattern no single gate can see — then open the builder and wire circuits of your own. Everything after that is scale.

01 / COUNTING IN TWOS

Eight switches hold any number to 255.

A bit is one two-way choice: off or on, 0 or 1. Line eight of them up — a byte — and give each place a value that doubles as you move left: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. A number is just the sum of the places switched on. That is the whole of binary: ordinary counting where each column is worth two of the column before it, instead of ten. Flip the switches and make some numbers — 42 is the traditional one to try.

The binary bench — flip the switches

Why two values and not ten? Because a switch is honest about two states and vague about ten. A wire is either carrying current or it isn't; asking it to hold ten distinct voltages is asking it to be a measuring instrument, and measuring instruments drift. Two states can be told apart by a machine that is half-broken — which is most machines, eventually.

Count to 31 on one hand

YOUR FINGERS ARE BITS

Hold up a hand. Each finger is a bit: up is 1, down is 0, and the places run thumb to pinky — 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Thumb and pinky up is 17. Everything up is 31. Two hands count to 1023, which is why the binary bench's eight switches feel familiar: you have been carrying ten bits around your whole life, mostly using them to count to five the slow way.

Five fingers, thirty-one numbers. Ten fingers, a thousand and twenty-three.

Why the places double

THE SAME RULE AS ORDINARY COUNTING, SMALLER

Ordinary numbers work identically, with ten symbols instead of two: each place is worth ten of the place before it, which is why 42 means four tens and two ones. Binary is the same machine with the smallest possible symbol set — two — so each place is worth two of the one before. Nothing about the bench above is a new kind of number. It is the old kind, counted in twos.

02 / THE GATES

Six little rules, and not one exception among them.

A gate reads one or two bits and answers one bit, by a rule it never breaks. AND wants both. OR wants either. NOT flips its one input. XOR lights when the inputs differ. NAND and NOR are AND and OR with the answer flipped — and either of those two alone can be wired to imitate all the others, which is why real chips are built from them by the billion. Pick a gate, flip its switches, and watch its truth table mark the row you are standing on: the table is not a chart to memorise, it is the gate's whole biography, four lines long.

03 / GATES THAT ADD

Two gates, side by side, and arithmetic exists.

Here is the room's party trick, and it is not a trick. To add two bits, you need two answers: the sum bit — what lands in this column — and the carry — what spills into the next. Look at the four cases and the pattern jumps out: the sum bit is 1 exactly when the inputs differ, which is XOR's whole job; the carry is 1 only when both inputs are 1, which is AND's. So a half adder is one XOR and one AND sharing the same two inputs. Flip the switches and watch 1 + 1 become 10 — not ten, but one-zero: a carry and a zero, which in binary is two.

The half adder bench — add two bits

carry (AND)
sum (XOR)

Chain adders that also accept a carry-in — full adders — and the same two-gate idea adds numbers of any length, one column at a time, the carry rippling down the row. Your phone does this with numbers 64 columns wide, billions of times a second, in a chip holding billions of gates. The marvel is not that the trick is clever. The marvel is that the trick is all there is: everything a computer has ever calculated was added up, at bottom, like this.

04 / THE TOY NET

No single gate knows the pattern. The wiring does.

Three bits make eight patterns. The little net below is wired — a NOT, an AND, and an AND — to recognise exactly one of them: 1, 0, 1, both ends lit and the middle quiet. No gate in it can see the whole pattern; each one reads its wire and applies its rule. The recognition happens nowhere and everywhere: it is a property of how the gates are connected. Flip the three switches, walk all eight patterns, and watch the lamp — then notice the readout underneath, which counts how many bits match. A pattern that misses by one bit is a near miss, and near misses are where troubleshooting lives.

The toy net bench — find the pattern it loves

This is Steven's troubleshooting aphorism built out of gates, the one hanging in Nerd Heaven: most people work a problem by rules — gates, like a map. The real skill is the pattern recogniser that sees similarities across gates, including the branches a rule would have eliminated. The net above is three gates and already does a primitive version of it: it holds the whole pattern at once, and its near misses still carry information.

05 / THE LOGIC BUILDER

Your turn at the wiring.

The benches above are wired for you. This one is a bare board: four switches, a parts bin of gates, and lamps. The method is two taps — tap any output (a switch, or a gate's OUT) to pick its wire up, then tap any input slot to plug the wire in. Tap a filled slot with empty hands to unplug it. Everything evaluates live, as you wire. An unwired input reads 0, the way a loose wire reads nothing. New here? Press Load the XOR trick and the board builds the difference detector out of the basic gates — an OR, an AND, a NOT, and a joining AND — then flip A and B and watch four gates do what one XOR does.

The board

Switches — tap to flip, tap OUT to carry the wire

Parts bin — tap to place a gate

Lamps — tap to add one (up to four), wire it like any input

Two honest behaviours, stated before you meet them. Wire a gate's output back into its own input — directly, or around a loop of gates — and its lamp answers ?: a loop like that has no steady answer, and the board says so instead of guessing. And the board holds up to twelve gates: real chips hold billions, but twelve is enough to build anything this room teaches, including a full adder, if you fancy the exercise.

06 / BITS AND SIZES

The ladder with two honest rungs.

Above the byte, sizes climb by the thousand — mostly. There are two ladders, and the difference is a standing source of quiet fraud. The decimal ladder climbs by exactly 1000: a kilobyte is 1000 bytes, by the same SI rule as a kilogram, and it is the ladder disk makers print on the box. The binary ladder climbs by 1024 — 2 to the 10th — because memory is built in powers of two, and its honest names are kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte. The ladders start 2.4% apart and drift: by the terabyte they disagree by nearly a tenth, which is why a "1 TB" drive shows up in your computer as about 0.91 of its own binary self. Neither ladder is lying. They are just not the same ladder, and now you can convert on both.

The sizes bench — one amount, every rung

Unit honesty is a collection-wide habit, not this room's hobby: Everyday Measure owns it — the tonne that isn't a ton, the gallon that depends on the ocean it crossed. This bench is that room's computing branch.

07 / A PROGRAM IS A RECIPE

Instructions a cook who cannot guess could follow.

An algorithm is a recipe written for a follower who does exactly what the steps say — no more, no less, and no common sense. That is a computer's great strength and its whole personality: it will follow a million steps without tiring, and it will follow a wrong step a million times without noticing. So the steps must be exact, complete, and in an order that works. The bench below runs the most watchable algorithm there is: bubble sort. Walk the row, compare each pair, swap any pair that is out of order, and walk again, until a whole walk makes no swaps. Big cards drift to the end like bubbles rising. It is not the fastest way to sort — it is the way whose every step you can check, which is the point of a first algorithm.

The sorting bench — bubble sort, one pass at a time

The tea test

WHY "MAKE TEA" IS NOT AN ALGORITHM

"Make tea" is an instruction a human survives by guessing: fill the kettle — with water, presumably; boil it — in the kettle, presumably; pour — over the tea, in a cup, presumably. Every gap is filled by a lifetime of kitchens. A machine fills no gaps. Its tea recipe must say: fill the kettle with water; switch it on; wait for the click; put a bag in a cup; pour. Miss the "with water" and the machine will happily boil an empty kettle forever, exactly as told. Programs are recipes that passed the tea test — and bugs are the steps where they didn't.

Exact, complete, in order. The machine brings the obedience; you bring the sense.

Counting in twos

The binary bench is eight switches with places worth 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Flip any switch and the readout shows the sum being built, place by place; type an ordinary number instead and the switches set themselves by the same rule run backwards — biggest place that fits, subtract, continue. The two directions are the same fact, which is the bench's whole lesson: binary is not a code laid over numbers, it is numbers, counted in twos.

The gates and their tables

Six gates: AND, OR, XOR, NAND, NOR (two inputs each) and NOT (one). Choose a gate and its switches and lamp appear; flip the switches and the lamp obeys the gate's rule, while the truth table beside it highlights the row you are standing on — so the table fills in as a record of what you have tried, not a chart handed down. Every row of every table on this bench is computed by the same rule file the room's tests check, so the table you see is the table that was verified.

Gates that add

The half adder shares two inputs between an XOR (the sum bit) and an AND (the carry), and its readout spells the addition in both number systems: the outputs read together, carry first, are the answer in binary. The four switch settings are the whole truth table of addition for one column — and the room's tests run all four, because 1 + 1 = 10 is exactly the case a sloppy adder gets wrong.

The toy net

The net is three gates — NOT on the middle bit, AND across the ends, AND joining the two — wired to recognise 1, 0, 1. The diagram lights its wires as you flip the inputs, the table of all eight patterns marks the one the net loves, and the readout counts matching bits so a near miss shows up as information rather than failure. Nothing in the net is simulated by a shortcut: the page evaluates the actual gates, in the actual order, which is why its near misses are honest ones.

The logic builder

A free-form board: four switches (A–D), up to twelve gates from the parts bin, up to four lamps. Tap an output — a switch's OUT or a gate's OUT — to pick its wire up; tap an input slot to plug it in; tap a filled slot with empty hands to unplug. Gates can be removed with their × button, which unwires everything they fed. The XOR trick button builds a working XOR from OR, AND, NOT, and AND through the same wiring steps your taps use. Evaluation is live on every change; a gate wired into a loop with itself reports ?, because a loop has no steady answer and the board will not invent one.

Bits and sizes

Type an amount, pick its unit, and the bench converts it to bits and reads it back on every rung of both ladders — decimal (1000-based: kilobyte, megabyte, gigabyte, terabyte) and binary (1024-based: kibibyte, mebibyte, gibibyte, tebibyte). The rungs are exact: a kibibyte is 8192 bits because it is 1024 bytes of 8 bits, and the bench's tests pin those numbers so the conversions cannot drift into the marketing version.

A program is a recipe

The sorting bench runs bubble sort on five cards: one pass walks the row comparing neighbours and swapping the out-of-order pairs; the readout narrates each swap, and Sort it all repeats passes until a pass changes nothing — the algorithm's own stopping rule, not a fixed count. Shuffle deals one of several fixed scrambles so the exercise is repeatable. The tea card carries the section's moral: an algorithm is a recipe that survives being followed by a cook who cannot guess.

Honest limits — including one wrong picture

Three limits, stated rather than hidden. The infographic in the gates section contains gate-logic errors in its own labels — an XOR shown lighting on 1 and 1, a NOT that does not flip — and is reproduced exactly as Steven chose to publish it, with the errors named on the page; the benches, by contrast, are computed from rules the tests verify, and where picture and bench disagree, the bench is right. The toy net recognises one fixed pattern — it is a teaching model of recognition, not a learning machine; nothing on this page trains, adapts, or guesses. And the sizes bench's two ladders are both real: the page converts on each and names which is which, because the only dishonest rung is the one that won't say which ladder it's on.
