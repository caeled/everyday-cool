# Collection origin

Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 19 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that evening he named the gap himself — "I keep having a nagging feeling that there is a STEM subject we are missing" — and ordered the four candidates saved so all four will be built ("save that list I want to do them all"). Everyday Engineering went first, Everyday Math second, Everyday Earth third; this room is the fourth and last, on his tap of the option card: "Build Everyday Computing — finish the four." Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction as the twenty-ninth catalog entry and the tenth workshop inside The STEM Room; this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room is built to Steven's own framing in the saved queue: "Binary and gates. Counting in twos, AND/OR/XOR made playable, pattern recognition on top — ties straight into Steven's gates aphorism and the Nerd Heaven panel." The aphorism is his troubleshooting epistemology, articulated the same afternoon: most people work a problem by rules — logic gates, like following a map — while the real skill is pattern recognisers seeing similarities across gates, including eliminated branches; the inference "something here might apply there" is, in his words, "the real vudu." The room's toy net is that paragraph built out of three gates, near misses counted and all. Three additions arrived from Steven while the room was being built and are folded in: a free-form **logic builder** (place the simple gates, wire outputs into inputs, watch lamps evaluate live — anchor `#logic-builder`); the **gates-pattern infographic itself**, included on the page as a room asset; and a **link from Nerd Heaven's aphorism panel to the builder**, recorded in that room's COLLECTION.md local-changes list. The room ships web-first under the plan Steven set the same evening: the four queue rooms go live on the web one at a time and reach the desktop app together in a single later release — so no app build carries this room yet, by design. With this room live, all four are up, and that single app release is what comes next.

**The infographic is reproduced as published, errors and all, on purpose.** `gates-pattern.jpg` — the AI-generated "Mini neural net — simple gates" image made for Steven's November 19, 2026 posts (LinkedIn, Facebook, Substack) and hung the same day in the Nerd Heaven room, from which this copy is taken byte-for-byte — contains gate-logic errors in its own explainer text: it shows an XOR of 1 and 1 lighting its lamp, and a NOT inversion that does not invert. Steven was told before the posts went out and chose to publish the picture as-is, the flaw sitting oddly well inside a piece about pattern-spotting outrunning rule-checking. This room keeps the record straight the same way the posts' owner did: the page reproduces the image with its AI-generated disclosure, names the errors in a card beside it, and states the division of labour plainly — the picture is art, published as made; the benches and the builder are instruments, logically correct and covered by `test.cjs`. When the two disagree, the page tells the visitor to believe the bench.

It enters the catalog under "Science" with `hubParent: stem-room`, immediately after Everyday Earth, so the STEM Room's bench now reads Physics, Chemistry, Electronics, Measure, Engineering, Math, Earth, Computing, Body, Radio. The room follows the Science block's shape: benches that work right on the page and show their working, a verified shelf of legitimate places, Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the live page. Its cross-links: Everyday Electronics (the circuits that carry the gates), Everyday Math (the numbers), Everyday Measure (unit honesty), Nerd Heaven (the aphorism panel — which now links back here, to the builder), and Secret Squares with Everyday Language along the code thread.

Everything interactive is computed in the visitor's browser from rules baked into `computing.js` — the page sends nothing anywhere. Facts, sources, and the deliberate scope lines (the six gates taught and no others, the net's one fixed pattern, the builder's honest "?" for feedback loops, the two size ladders) are recorded in THIRD-PARTY.md.

The rules live in `computing.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Banner: `everyday-computing-banner.jpg`, AI-generated watercolor generated 19 November 2026 as part of adding this room, in the style and dimensions (2736×912) of the collection's other banners: the plush-style robot — stitched seams, blue button eyes — at a wooden workbench, wiring a small circuit board where three brass-and-glass gates stand (a D-shaped AND gate, a curved OR gate, a triangle gate) joined by copper wires to a small round lamp glowing amber, while the digits 1 and 0 drift up from the board like sparks. The prompt allowed no lettering beyond the single digits 1 and 0, and the first generation shipped: those drifting digits are the only characters in the image.

Game (21 November 2026): at Steven Powell's direction, the room gained its first game — `wire-the-adder.html`, **Wire the Adder**: six levels wiring gates into circuits that add (pick the gate that knows XOR; XOR from AND/OR/NOT; half adder; full adder; majority vote; a two-bit adder), graded live against the full truth table. Circuits are stored as a readable netlist — gate types and pin sources as plain strings — and the proof runs every level's reference wiring through the shipped evaluator: all 44 rows pass, and no level starts solved. `wire-the-adder-lesson.html` is its back door (gates, adders as counting machines, why NOT is special, the coding choices), opened from inside the game by a Lesson pop-up; the room's knowingly-mislabelled gates infographic is untouched. `index.html` gained a game card at the end of the adder section. The game also visits the Physics Game Table, the Arcade shelf, and both lesson shelves.
