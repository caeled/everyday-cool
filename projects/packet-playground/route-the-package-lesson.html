THE BACK-DOOR LESSON

Route the Package — what was built, what was measured, what bends

Where it came from

Steven's idea, 21 November 2026, five minutes after Route It shipped: “Route the package similar to the packet game could also be done. Logistics in general. Get the package to the distribution centers then from there to an address.” Minutes later he extended it himself: hub-and-spoke networks, shipping, and even bus schedules are the same pattern — Go Thataway generalised, head for the next node that takes you generally toward the destination and correct at each transfer. It waited in the queue for his go; the go came with Batch D on 26 November 2026, and this page is the receipt.

What was built

One page: a nine-hub network (plus the Defiance depot and the Fort Wayne address) drawn on a canvas map, eighteen truck lanes priced in hours and dollars, five last-mile van legs, and a dispatcher loop — at each stop the player picks the next hop from buttons that list every open lane, its cost, and its warnings. Each game deals two facts of life from a seeded deck: one hub is snowed in (announced up front; no lane into it runs) and one mid-network lane is mis-sort-prone (flagged honestly on its button; taking it mis-sorts the package, once). The network's own best — fewest hours, and fewest dollars, over the lanes actually open — is computed live by Dijkstra's algorithm and printed beside your totals from the first move.

The audit — 11 of 11 checks, on the shipped page

  • Both pars are honest. The page's Dijkstra results were recomputed by an independent implementation in the audit harness, from the page's own graph data: game 0 (with Nashville snowed in) promises 5 h / $20, and the independent computation agrees to the digit.
  • The closed hub is truly closed. Across six deals, every node's offered hops were enumerated: the snowed-in hub is never offered, from anywhere.
  • A full delivery, driven through the real buttons — Defiance → Toledo → last-mile van — landed at 5 h / $20, exactly par on both currencies, and the verdict panel said so.
  • The mis-sort is a real loop. In game 0 the flagged lane was Toledo → Indianapolis; taken deliberately, the package was mis-sorted straight back to Toledo — one full circle, paid for in hours and dollars, captioned as what it is: the routing loop, in cardboard.
  • Zero page exceptions across the whole drive.

What bends

  • The prices are teaching numbers. Real carriers price by weight, zone, density, and contract; the hours are plausible, not surveyed. What is true is the shape: hub-and-spoke, the last mile as a separate and pricier problem, failures announced and routed around, and the occasional label read wrong.
  • The mis-sort is announced risk, not a trap. The lane is flagged before you take it, and it fires exactly once — the game teaches the decision (cheap and risky versus dear and sure), not the ambush. Real mis-sorts carry no flag; the lesson says so rather than pretend otherwise.
  • The map is honest geography at postcard resolution. The cities are real and roughly placed; the lanes are the game's, not a carrier's network.
  • One package, one dispatcher, no clock pressure. Real networks route millions of parcels against capacity and cut-off times. The skeleton the game teaches — next-hop decisions with local knowledge only — is the part that scales.

Verdict

Route It taught routing from inside the wire; this twin teaches it from inside the world, where the same skeleton wears cardboard and diesel. The pair now stands together in Packet Playground, which is where Steven said the idea belonged: packets and packages, the pair teaches itself.
