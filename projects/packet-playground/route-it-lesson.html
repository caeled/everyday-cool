Route It — the lesson

Route It is a routing game: a packet crosses a small network one decision at a time, and at every stop, somebody — you — reads a routing table and picks the next hop. This page is the other half of it: what a packet and a hop actually are, why the table only ever shows the next turn, why packets carry a self-destruct counter, and the arithmetic of the 256-address window. Readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

Packets and hops

Almost nothing crosses a network in one piece. A photo, a page, a message — all of it is chopped into packets: small addressed envelopes of data, each carrying a source address and a destination address. Between you and the destination sits a chain of routers — devices whose whole job is to receive packets and forward them. One router-to-router step is a hop.

Here is the part the game is built around: no router knows the whole path. A router does not hold a map of the network. It holds a table of directions — for this destination, hand the packet to that neighbour — and trusts the next router to know its own next step. Routing is dead reckoning for packets: head the general direction, correct at every hop, and the destination is reached by a chain of local decisions, none of which saw the whole journey. When you played Level 1 and the packet arrived in exactly the promised three hops, that chain is all that happened.

A routing table is a map that only shows the next turn

A routing table is a list of rows: a destination prefix — a neighbourhood of addresses, like 10.0.6.0/24 — and the next hop to hand packets for that neighbourhood to. When a packet arrives, the router finds the rows whose prefix contains the destination address and uses the longest matching prefix — the most specific row wins, because a row about one street is better information than a row about the whole city. The row that matches everything, 0.0.0.0/0, is the default route: "if nothing else matches, send it thataway."

Two things follow, and both are in the game. First, a table can list several next hops for one prefix — Level 2's Junction offers Hilltop or Riverside for the same street, and both are honest answers with different costs. Second, a table can be stale: it describes the network as it was, not as it is. Level 3's dead link and Level 4's loop are the same failure wearing two coats — the table said a thing the network no longer agreed with.

TTL: the counter that makes loops survivable

If two routers each believe the other is closer to a destination, a packet can bounce between them forever — and "forever" is a real engineering problem, because the packet consumes the link the whole time. The fix rides inside every packet: a time to live, a number that every router decrements by one. When it reaches zero, the packet is dropped. Loops still happen; they just die young.

The correction is also in the table: Birch lists a second next hop, Alder. Taking it — You → Ash → Birch → Alder → Elm → Mailbox — delivers in 5 hops. Nothing about the network changed. Only the choice did, and the notebook is what told you a choice needed changing: a path that revisits its own stops is a circle, and circles do not arrive.

Two roads: hops and latency are different costs

Level 2's two honest routes to the same street, with the game's own link numbers:

North: 1 + 9 + 9 + 1 = 20 latency, 4 hops South: 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 8 latency, 5 hops Counting hops, the north road wins. Counting time, the south road wins by two and a half times. "Shortest path" is meaningless until you say what you are measuring — real routing protocols each pick a metric (hop count, bandwidth, delay) and optimise for that.

And Level 3 is the same lesson from the other side: the cheapest-looking row in Birch's table pointed down a link that no longer existed. A route is a claim about the world, and the world gets a vote. The packet was saved the way packets are always saved — not by a better plan made at the start, but by a correction made at the hop where the plan broke.

The 256-address window

Level 5 is played on one street, 192.168.7.0/24 — the same window as the room's Neighborhood Bench ("More prefix. Smaller neighborhood."). A /24 leaves 8 host bits, and 2⁸ = 256 addresses:

256 addresses − .0 (the network) − .255 (the broadcast) = 254 doors .0 has all-zero host bits: it names the street itself. .255 has all-one host bits: it calls every door at once. Both are jobs, not houses — a packet "delivered" to either has not been delivered to anyone.

The delivery rule that follows is the one every device on a real network applies before anything fancier: compare the destination with your own prefix. Same street → deliver directly to that address. Different street → hand it to the gateway, the router on your street whose job is everywhere else; the packet's destination address stays the far address the whole way. In the game, your cousin's computer at 192.168.9.30 differs in the third number, so it is not a local door no matter how near it feels — .9 is a different street from .7, and only the gateway goes there. (More on the arithmetic of prefixes: subnetting on Wikipedia.)

How it's built

The game's core is small enough to say plainly. The network is data: a set of named nodes, each with an address and a table (prefix → ordered next hops), plus a list of links with costs. Delivery is a pure walk over that data: look up the destination in the current node's table by longest-prefix match, move along a link, decrement the TTL, write the stop in the trace, repeat until the packet is delivered, lost, or out of TTL. Level 3's failure is also data — a link and the hop count at which it dies — so the "announcement" and the dead button are the same fact the proof checks. Level 5's traps are not scripted either: one function classifies any address against the street (local, gateway, network, broadcast) and the game, the verdicts, and the proof all use that one function. Before the game shipped, a scripted player in a headless test played every level by the intended strategy and delivered every packet; played Level 4 by first choices only and watched the packet die by TTL at exactly 8 hops; and checked all 254 host addresses on the street, one by one, as local.

Simplifications, named. The game's tables are static — typed in advance and never updated. Real networks learn their tables: routers exchange reachability with protocols like RIP (hop-count metric, small networks), OSPF (link-state, inside one organisation), and BGP (path-vector, between organisations — the protocol that holds the Internet's streets together, and the one whose stale or wrong announcements cause real Level 4s at planetary scale). Those protocols are also how a Level 3 failure would eventually heal itself here: the detour would be learned, not just listed.

Latency is a fixed cost per link in the game. Real links slow down under load — congestion — and queues drop packets for reasons that have nothing to do with tables. The game also sends one packet at a time, never fragments a packet too big for a link, and gives every packet in a level the same TTL (real senders start at values like 64, 128, or 255 depending on the system). Addresses here are private teaching addresses; the public Internet adds NAT and far longer paths — a real cross-country delivery is often 10–20 hops, each one decided exactly the way you decided these.
