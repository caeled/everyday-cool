THE PACKAGE GAME · ROUTE IT'S PHYSICAL TWIN

Route the Package

A parcel is waiting at the depot in Defiance, Ohio, addressed to a street in Fort Wayne, Indiana. No truck drives it straight there — it moves the way everything in a hub-and-spoke world moves: one next hop at a time, corrected at every stop. You are every dispatcher it meets. Head the general direction, mind the weather and the mis-sorts, and don't forget the last mile — a hub is not an address.

    Help ▾ — how to play, and what is honest here
    • How to play: at each stop, choose the package's next hop from the buttons. Every lane lists its hours and dollars. Vans run the last mile from the hubs that offer one — the address is in Fort Wayne, so a van only makes sense from a hub near it. Deliver the package, then compare your hours and dollars with the network's own best (computed live, over the lanes actually open).
    • The closed hub is announced before you start: weather takes one hub out of the network each game, and no lane into it runs. Rerouting around a failure is the job, in both worlds.
    • The mis-sort lane is flagged on its button — it has mis-sorted twice this week. Take it and the package may land at the wrong hub (once per game, and the game tells you when it happens). Cheap and risky, or dear and sure: a dispatcher's real choice.
    • On a touch screen: the whole game is buttons — it plays by tap. The map is a picture of the buttons' world.
    • Honest limits: the geography is real (these are real cities, roughly where they belong) but the lanes, hours, and prices are teaching numbers, not a carrier's tariff. Real networks price by weight, zone, and contract; the shape — hub, spoke, last mile, failure, mis-sort — is the true part.
