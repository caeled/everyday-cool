The Dewey Decimal System

Melvil Dewey published his system in 1876: ten classes, each split by tens, then decimals that never have to stop. Most public libraries still shelve by it — a subject turned into an address. The ten classes:

  • 000 Computer science, information & general worksThe junk drawer that caught the future: the system predates computers, so computing moved into the general-works shelf.
  • 100 Philosophy & psychologyThe big questions, and the mind that asks them.
  • 200 ReligionChristianity takes most of the shelf (220–280); every other faith is gathered together at 290.
  • 300 Social sciencesHow people live together: law, money, schools, customs.
  • 400 LanguageWords about words.
  • 500 ScienceThe natural world, counted and measured.
  • 600 TechnologyScience put to work: medicine, engineering, farming, cooking.
  • 700 Arts & recreationThe made and the played: painting, music, sport.
  • 800 LiteratureShelved by the language it was written in first, the subject second.
  • 900 History & geographyWhere and when: places, journeys, and lives — biography often lands at 920.

The system has its vagaries, and librarians love it anyway. Topics migrate as the world changes, but old books keep the numbers they were given, so a subject can live at two addresses in the same building. The decimals never end — birds trail from 598 to 598.2 and beyond. A life can shelve at 92, at 920, or with its subject, depending on the house rule. Fiction usually escapes entirely, pulled out and alphabetised by author. And every library bends the system a little to fit its own shelves, so the same book can honestly have different addresses in different towns.

  • Shelf It! — a Dewey guessing gameTen rounds: shelve the book, or read a number and name its subject. Its lesson page prints the full catalog — all one hundred divisions — and tells the system's story, wrinkles included.

Library Science

This room is a library, so its last shelf is about libraries themselves: the profession of classification — deciding what things are, what they are called, and where they belong, so a stranger can find them. The Internet Archive and Project Gutenberg, shelved under Books above, are this shelf's working tools.

  • Dewey Decimal Classification — WikipediaMelvil Dewey's system in full: its history, its ten classes, and the arguments it still starts.
  • Library of Congress Classification — WikipediaThe other great system: built for one enormous library in Washington and adopted by universities everywhere.
  • Library of Congress Classification OutlineThe official outline of the LCC, straight from the Library of Congress itself.
  • Library classification — WikipediaClassification as a craft: why libraries arrange by subject at all, and the systems that compete to do it.
  • Cataloging — WikipediaHow a book becomes a record: description, subject headings, and the rules that keep catalogs consistent.
  • Card catalog — WikipediaThe wooden drawers that ran libraries for a century: their history, and what replaced them.
  • Melvil Dewey — WikipediaThe founder's life — the system, the profession he built around it, and the parts of the record that complicate the story.
  • S. R. Ranganathan — WikipediaIndia's great librarian, who treated classification as a science of its own and rebuilt it from first principles.
  • Five laws of library science — WikipediaRanganathan's five laws, 1931 — "every reader his or her book" — still the profession's conscience.
  • Controlled vocabulary — WikipediaWhy catalogs pick one word for a thing and stick to it: synonyms, authority files, and finding anything at all.
  • WorldCatSearch the catalogs of thousands of libraries at once and find which library near you holds a book.
  • MARC — Library of CongressThe record format that taught catalogs to talk to computers, documented by the library that created it.
