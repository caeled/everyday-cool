Shelf It! — the lesson

In 1876 a young librarian named Melvil Dewey published a short book with a large idea: every subject in the world could be given a number, and the number could be the book's address on the shelf. A century and a half later, most public libraries on Earth still shelve by his system. Shelf It! is a guessing game built on it — and this page is the other half: how the system works, the whole map of its hundred divisions, an honest account of its quirks, and how the game was built. It is readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

How Dewey works

Ten classes, and every one splits by tens

Start with everything a library might hold, and sort it into ten great classes — the 000s through the 900s. Each class splits into ten divisions: the 500s (Science) open into 510 Mathematics, 520 Astronomy, 530 Physics, and so on. Each division splits again into ten sections: 590 Animals narrows to 598, birds. Three digits get you to a shelf neighbourhood; the sorting rule is always the same — read the number left to right, and each new digit narrows the subject.

The decimals never end

After the third digit comes a decimal point, and after the point the number can grow as long as the subject needs. Birds are 598. Hawks and eagles are 598.4. One particular kind of hawk can carry a number longer than a phone extension. That endless tail is the system's real engine: new subjects never need a new scheme, they just grow a longer number inside the neighbourhood where they belong. A Dewey number is less like a page number and more like a set of directions that can zoom in forever.

598 → 598.4 → 598.42 … Animals (590s) → birds (598) → birds of prey (598.4) → finer still. Nothing in the system says where the digits have to stop — the librarian stops when the shelf is specific enough.

The number is about the subject, not the book

Dewey classifies what a book is about, not what it is made of. A novel about a sea voyage is still literature; a manual for repairing the boat is technology. Under the number, most libraries add a few letters from the author's name (a Cutter number), so books at the same address line up alphabetically. Number first, author second — subject is the street, the author is the house number.

Worked examples — walking a book home

Example 1: the chicken book

Keeping Chickens in a Small Yard — where does it go? Not with the animals at 590: Dewey files things by their human use as much as their nature, and chickens are farm stock. Walk it down: the 600s are Technology, the applied side of knowledge; 630 is Agriculture; inside the sections, 636 is animal husbandry. The game stops at the division — 630 — but a real shelf would carry it further, to 636.7 for poultry in particular. Every step is a question: applied or pure? farming? animals? which animals?

Example 2: reading 796 cold

Mode 2 of the game hands you a bare number. Read it like an address: the first digit, 7, puts you in the 700s — Arts & recreation. The 9 narrows to 790, Sports, games & entertainment. The 6 is a section digit: 796 is athletic and outdoor sports. You never needed the whole map — just the habit of zooming one digit at a time.

Example 3: the biography that lives in two places

A life of Marie Curie: is it biography (920) or physics (530)? Libraries genuinely differ. Some gather all lives together at 920 (older shelves used 92); others shelve the life with the subject, so Curie stands with the physicists and a general's memoir stands with the wars. The game's deck keys Lives of the Inventors to 920 — a collection of lives, with no single subject to attach to — but if your local library keeps it elsewhere, your library is not wrong. It is doing what every library does, below.

From the screen to the shelf

The catalog — card drawer once, search box now — hands you a number. The number is not the book's name; it is the book's address, and the same number is printed on the sticker on the book's spine. Walking it is a skill of its own, and it goes in pieces. The aisle-end signs carry the hundreds ranges, so 636 lives somewhere in the 600s aisle. Inside the range you narrow by tens, then by ones. Past the decimal point, read digit by digit, one at a time: 636.7 files after plain 636 and before 637, and 636.72 comes after 636.7 — the tail is a path, not a quantity. The letters after the number (the Cutter letters) are the author's name in code, the final narrowing from the subject's street to the book's own house.

And when you arrive, look left and right before you reach. The books standing beside yours are its subject-neighbours — the ones the search box would never have introduced you to. Half the good reading in a library is found that way: not the book you came for, but the one shelved next to it that you didn't know you wanted until you saw it standing there.

The vagaries — an honest account of the wrinkles

Dewey's system is a map of knowledge drawn in 1876, in one country, by one man's library. It has been revised ever since — there have been more than twenty editions — but a map that old keeps some of its first geography. Librarians know the wrinkles by heart and mostly love the system anyway. Here they are, plainly:

Computers live in the junk drawer

The 000s were "general works" — encyclopedias, bibliographies, things about knowledge itself. When computers arrived, there was no class waiting for them, so computing was fitted into the general shelf, at 000–006. The newest major subject in the library sits in its oldest leftover space. It works fine. It is also a perfect fossil of how classification actually happens: the future goes wherever there is room.

The 200s belong mostly to one religion

Of the ten divisions in Religion, seven (220–280) are Christianity in detail — the Bible, theology, practice, history, denominations — and every other faith on Earth shares the single division 290. That is not a conspiracy; it is 1876 in Massachusetts, where Dewey's readers were overwhelmingly Christian and his shelves were stocked to match. The imbalance is still there, still noticed, and still argued about at library conferences.

Literature is filed by language, not by subject

The 800s sort first by the language a work was written in: American and English literature at 810–820, French at 840, Latin at 870. So a French novel about ancient Rome stands with the French books, not the Roman ones, and all the world's poetry never gathers in one place. Dewey chose the writer's tongue over the book's topic — a defensible rule that surprises every reader who goes looking for "books about" something and finds them scattered by grammar.

Numbers migrate; books don't

Each new edition of the schedules moves subjects to better homes — computing has been rehoused more than once, and whole topics have crossed classes as the world's understanding changed. But a library rarely re-labels its old stock. The result: the same subject can sit at two different addresses in the same building, an old number on the older books and a new one on the new arrivals, with the catalog quietly knowing both.

Biography wanders, and fiction escapes

As Example 3 shows, a life can live at 92, at 920, or with its subject, depending on the library's house rule. And fiction, in most public libraries, opts out of the system entirely: novels are pulled from the 800s and shelved by author surname in a fiction section of their own. The most-borrowed books in the building often carry no Dewey number at all.

Every library bends it a little

Local practice is the system's open secret. Libraries shorten numbers so small collections stay browsable, expand them where a subject is popular, pull picture books and graphic novels into sections of their own, and keep beloved exceptions forever. Dewey supplies the map; each town redraws its own copy. The same book can honestly live at three different addresses in three different towns — and does.

How it's built — the coding choices

Shelf It! is one HTML file with no libraries, and its arrangement is the lesson it teaches: one table drives everything. The hundred divisions sit in the code as a single data table, fenced between two marker comments (/* === CORE === */ and /* === END CORE === */) along with the deck of books and the scoring rules. Mode 1 reads the table forwards (book → division), Mode 2 reads it backwards (division → subject), and the answer key for both modes is the table itself — there is no second list to disagree with it. The table on this page was printed from that same data, so the lesson and the game cannot drift apart.

Two smaller choices are worth naming. The decoy answers are never random: the choice-builder picks the three nearest neighbouring divisions in the same class, because a guess between 630 and 640 teaches more than a guess between 630 and 910. And before the game shipped, a script checked the table the way the game uses it — all one hundred divisions present, every book's keyed answer in the table and appearing exactly once among its choices, and a scripted perfect player scoring 10 out of 10 in both modes. The proof plays the shipped code, not a copy of it.

The game page also carries a subject search — type a subject, get its Dewey number — and it reads the same table two ways. First it matches the query against the hundred divisions' own names; then it consults a hand-kept keyword map from everyday words (the ones a person actually types: chickens, dreams, soccer, taxes) to divisions. Where a word's precise home is a section below its division — chickens at 636, inside 630 Agriculture — the map stores the section too, and the result shows both numbers, so the search teaches the decimal tail instead of hiding it. A word the index doesn't know gets an honest "no address" and a suggestion to try a broader word; the search never invents a number. It is a teaching index, not the full DDC schedules — Dewey's own version of it, the Relative Index, runs to thousands of entries.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the real Dewey schedules are vastly deeper than this page — the full set runs to thousands of pages across four volumes, with sections, subsections, and auxiliary tables for building numbers (by place, by period, by language) that the game never touches. The game stops at the hundred divisions; a working librarian lives in the decimals. The deck's forty books are invented teaching examples, plausible but not real publications, and a few real books would argue with their keyed division — classification has judgement calls, and different cataloguers make them differently. Local practice varies, as the vagaries section says. The game page's subject search is likewise a teaching index — a hand-kept map of about a hundred everyday words, not Dewey's Relative Index, which runs to thousands of entries and knows subjects this page has never heard of. And the system itself is alive: the Dewey Decimal Classification is maintained by OCLC, revised continuously, and is a trademarked, licensed product — this page teaches its shape and spirit, and is not the schedule.
