Battleship — the lesson

Battleship is a search problem wearing a navy uniform: five ships hidden on a hundred squares of water, and nothing to go on but the answers to your own questions. This page is the other half of the game — the colouring trick that halves the search before the first shot, why a miss is worth almost as much as a hit, how targeting halves the interval again, and how the enemy captain across the table is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

“A search pattern is a heading: you do not search everywhere — you search the half that can still be true, then halve it again.” The stance of this room, from Go Thataway’s dead reckoning to this chart. A bearing narrows the world to a line; a pattern narrows it to fifty squares.

The game, briefly

By most accounts Battleship begins as a pencil-and-paper game passed around by soldiers in the First World War — two hand-drawn grids, ships sketched in secret, coordinates called across a trench or a wardroom. Milton Bradley turned it into the plastic peg-board classic in 1967, all red pegs and white pegs and little grey ships, and the talking Electronic Battleship followed in 1977. The rules have barely moved in a century, because the problem underneath them is a good one: the fleet on each side is the same five ships — Carrier 5, Battleship 4, Cruiser 3, Submarine 3, Destroyer 2, seventeen squares of ship in all — and everything you will ever know about the enemy’s seventeen, you learn one called square at a time.

The parity argument: search by halves

Colour the chart like a chessboard, alternating black and white squares. Now the whole game turns on one small fact:

the smallest ship is 2 squares long Any two squares side by side — along a row or down a column — always hold one black square and one white. So every ship of length 2 or more, anywhere on the chart, in either direction, covers at least one black square. No exceptions: it is a property of the colouring, not a hunch about the enemy.

That one fact halves the ocean. A captain who fires only on black squares is guaranteed to score a first hit on every ship afloat, while searching:

100 squares ÷ 2 = 50 squares to check Fifty black squares, and each of the five ships must touch at least one of them. The game’s own proof does not take this on faith — it enumerates every legal placement of every ship length from 2 to 5 on the 10 × 10 chart — 600 placements — and checks each one: 600 cover a black square, 0 do not.

This is the same move as a binary search, played on water instead of in a sorted list. Binary search works because each question is chosen to throw half the remaining possibilities away whatever the answer is. The parity pattern is the board-game form of the idea: instead of asking about all hundred squares, you ask only about the fifty that can still teach you something new — because any ship the pattern hasn’t touched yet must be touching one of them. The game’s search panel keeps the count in front of you while you play: Black squares left to check: N of 50. When it reaches zero, there is nowhere left for an unhit ship to be.

A miss is information

New players feel a miss as a wasted turn. It isn’t. Every shot — hit or miss — converts one unknown square into a known one, and the hunt runs on knowns. A miss on a black square removes that square from the fifty and rules out every ship placement that needed it; a miss while targeting can be worth more than a hit, because it tells you which way the ship doesn’t run. The chart you are really building, shot by shot, is not a picture of their fleet. It is a shrinking map of where their fleet can still be — dead reckoning in reverse, worked from evidence instead of from a starting point. Go Thataway’s walker corrects a position with a fix; the Battleship captain corrects a search with a splash.

Targeting: halve the interval again

The moment a shot lands, the game changes mode — from hunt to target — and the halving starts over, smaller. One hit tells you a ship is here but not which way she runs: probe her neighbours. Two hits in a line tell you the line; walk it. And the sharp case is the one the enemy captain in this game is built to use first:

two hits, open water between → fire the middle square Hits at C7 and C10 leave C8 and C9 unknown. Firing C8 (or C9) splits the gap: whatever the answer, the unknown stretch left to settle is half what it was. It is the midpoint step of a binary search, fired out of a gun.

Gaps like that turn up constantly, and the parity hunt is why: black squares sit two apart along a ship’s length, so a hunt that lands twice on the same long ship often lands on squares with exactly one unsearched square between them. The pattern that found the ship hands the targeting its interval to halve. Nothing about this is luck, and none of it needs to know where the enemy put anything — it runs entirely on the shooter’s own record, which is the discipline the next section holds the game’s captain to.

The proof: the captain’s numbers

Before any human played this version, a program played it — hundreds of times. The proof lifts the exact core out of the shipped game file, deals it seeded random fleets, and lets the game’s own captain shoot until each fleet is gone. Its receipts:

Read the random row carefully, because it is the honest baseline: firing at random squares you haven’t tried takes, on average, 95.5 shots — nearly the whole chart — to account for seventeen squares of ship. The parity pattern and the targeting together cut that to 51.7, and even the captain’s worst fleet in four hundred took only 66. The proof also audits the captain’s discipline, not just its score: across all 400 simulations it never fired the same square twice and never once fired off the black squares while hunting. When the end-of-game tally shows what share of your shots fell on black squares, it is holding you to the same audit.

How it’s built — the coding choices

A pure core, so a proof can play it

Everything that decides anything — placement legality, firing and sinking, the parity colouring, the captain’s shot-choosing — lives in one pure core at the top of the game file, separate from everything that draws. The proof doesn’t re-implement the game; it extracts that core from the shipped file and runs it in Node, so the numbers above are measurements of the code you are actually playing, not of a model of it. Seeded randomness (the same mulberry32 generator as the rest of the room) means a fleet or a whole battle can be replayed exactly.

The captain is a state machine with one rule: no peeking

The enemy captain is never handed your board. It keeps a single record — its own shots and their outcomes — and its chooser reads nothing else. Its state is just the list of unsunk hits: squares it has hit that no sinking has accounted for yet. Empty list, it hunts black squares. Non-empty, it targets, in a fixed order of preference: a gap between two hits on one line (middle square first), then the open end of a run of hits, then a neighbour of a lone hit. When a ship sinks, the game announces her name and squares — the same announcement you get — and the captain strikes those hits from its list. Its discipline is not a promise in this text; it is the audit in the proof table, run over 400 fleets.

Sinking fires exactly once

A ship’s hit count lives on the ship, not on the squares, so a repeated shot at an old hit cannot inflate it: the resolver answers repeat and changes nothing, which the proof checks directly. sunk is returned by exactly one shot — the one that takes the ship’s last square — and it carries the ship’s name and squares with it, which is what lets both players (and the captain) update their picture of the chart honestly.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: turns are the classic single shot each — the salvo variant (one shot per ship afloat) is not used, and neither is any “fire until you miss” house rule. Ships may touch — sit directly alongside or end to end — because the placement rule only forbids overlap and running off the chart; some published editions and many house rules forbid touching, and if yours does, the honest fix is one extra check in canPlace. The captain picks among equally good squares at random rather than by any deeper probability weighting — it does not, for instance, prefer black squares that more surviving ship placements pass through, which a stronger player (or a computer with time to count placements) would; its 51.7 average is what the plain pattern earns. When a ship sinks, her exact squares are announced, as in the classic game — real captains would only know the ship was gone, and would have to guess which of their hits were hers. The two length-3 ships (Cruiser and Submarine) are interchangeable in everything but name. And the sea, it must be said, is suspiciously square.
