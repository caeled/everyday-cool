Go Thataway — the lesson

Go Thataway is a walking game: five cross-country legs navigated the old way, by bearing and pace count, with no dot on a map that knows the truth. Your stride veers, your reckoning drifts, and the only cure is the oldest one — look up, take a fix, and correct. This page is the other half of the game: the navigation it runs on, the arithmetic of drift, the solutions its own proof walks found, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

Bearings: a direction with a number

A bearing is a direction measured in degrees, clockwise from north: north is 0°, east 90°, south 180°, west 270°, and the compass points divide the circle between them — NE is 45°, ENE (“east-north-east”) is 67.5°, and so on through the sixteen points the Map Room’s trainer teaches. Bearings are always read as three figures in real navigation (“zero-four-five”), because a misheard digit is a wrong walk.

Every bearing has a back bearing: the direction home, exactly 180° away. Walk out at 45° and you return at 225°. Add 180 if your bearing is under 180; subtract 180 if it is over. The game never makes you compute one under pressure — but the return leg is half of every real trip, and it is why the Map Room’s “Find the way back” card sits next to “Take a bearing.”

back bearing = bearing ± 180° Out at 40° (Level 2’s tower), home at 220°. Out at 310°, home at 130°. If the sum passes 360, wrap it around: 310 + 180 = 490 − 360 = 130.

Pace counting: your legs are a ruler

A map gives distances in metres; the ground has to be measured with the instrument you’re standing on. That instrument is your stride. To calibrate it, walk a known 100 m — a measured track, a football pitch and change, a trail the map measures for you — count your steps, and divide: 100 m ÷ your count is your pace length. A common adult figure is about 0.75 m a step, and that is the pace the game gives you, stated on the HUD.

Two honest complications. First, a pace is one step in this game — but many walkers and most Scouts count a pace every time the same foot lands, which is a double step, roughly 1.5 m. Either convention works; mixing them mid-walk is how counts go wrong, so pick yours and keep it. Second, your pace is not a constant: it shortens uphill, tired, and in mud, and lengthens on a road. Calibration gives you your flat-ground number; experience teaches you the corrections. The game’s levels keep the ground flat and vary your pace only by a small random few percent per step — the simplifications box at the end names what that leaves out.

Dead reckoning, and why the error compounds

Dead reckoning is navigation by bookkeeping: from a place you know, add up every leg — bearing and distance — and the running total is where you must be. It is the only method that works under trees, in fog, and between fixes, and every navigator in history has used it. Its flaw is structural: the bookkeeping is exact, but the legs aren’t. Each leg is walked by a body, and bodies veer.

The arithmetic of a veer is unforgiving because it is just a tangent. If your heading is off by an angle θ, then after walking distance d you are sideways of your intended line by:

sideways error = d × tan θ A 2° veer over 100 m: 100 × tan 2° ≈ 3.5 m sideways. Small angle, long lever — the error grows in direct proportion to the distance, which is the whole problem.

And a steady veer is the kind case. A real heading also wanders — you settle a degree or two off, hold it for a while, drift back — and wander compounds worse than a fixed error, because it behaves like a random walk: its typical size grows with the square root of the steps and it never politely averages away. The game models both: each level has a bias (the steady veer) and a wander (a slow random walk in your heading, a few hundredths of a degree per step, that persists until something corrects it). Something correcting it is the next section.

Fixes and handrails: correction beats precision

A fix is a moment of ground truth: you identify where you are from something whose position you know — a landmark you can see, a junction, a bridge — and the bookkeeping restarts from fact. Everything between fixes is estimate; the fix is the audit. This is why the game rations them and why the proof below shows them winning: one fix in the middle of a long leg does more for your arrival than any amount of careful marching, because it zeroes the accumulated error instead of merely slowing its growth.

A handrail is a feature you can follow — a creek, a fence line, a ridge, a road — that steers you continuously, so drift mostly stops accumulating while you’re on it. Level 4 is built on one: “head north-east until you meet the creek, then follow it down to the bridge.” The first leg is rough on purpose, because it only has to reach a line, not a point; the second leg barely needs a compass at all. Route-finding by handrails — rough bearing to a line you can’t miss, then the line to the destination — is how most real cross-country travel actually works.

Aiming off: missing on purpose, in the right direction

When your destination sits on a handrail — the bridge on the creek — aim deliberately to one side of it. If you aim straight at the bridge and drift, you reach the creek not knowing whether the bridge is upstream or downstream of you: a coin flip, at the end of a long walk. Aim off instead — say, 10° to the left of the true bearing. Now when you strike the creek you know the bridge is downstream, every time, and you turn the one correct way. You have traded a small, known extra walk for the removal of a guess. Navigators call it aiming off, or a deliberate offset, and it is the purest form of the game’s stance: a rough plan that corrects itself beats a precise plan that can fail two ways.

One honesty note: magnetic north

The game walks by true bearings — the map’s north. A real compass needle points to magnetic north, which sits somewhere else, and the difference (the declination) is a local fact: a few degrees in much of the United States, more in some places, and it changes slowly over the years. A real walk with a real compass applies the local correction — your map’s margin prints it — before any of this page’s arithmetic means anything on the ground. The game leaves declination out so the drift you fight is your own; the Map Room’s Keep Going shelf links to Compass Dude, who teaches the correction properly, with pictures.

Worked examples — from the game’s own proof walks

Before any human played Go Thataway, a script walked it. The game’s core is written so a program can hold the compass: the test lifts the exact core out of the shipped file, walks the briefed bearings from a dead-reckoning notebook (it plans from where it believes it is, never from the truth), and takes a fix whenever one is available and its error has grown past 8 m. These are its actual numbers — receipts, not illustrations.

Example 1: Level 2, the Long Field — the napkin predicts the miss

The tower is 300 m off at 40°, and the level’s veer is 3°. The napkin says the open-loop walker should stop about 300 × tan 3° ≈ 15.7 m sideways of the tower. The script, walking with its fixes disabled, stopped 17.7 m out — outside the 12 m arrival ring. The extra two metres are the wander, doing what wander does. Same walker, fixes allowed: it spent one fix mid-field, re-plotted from the truth, and arrived 7.1 m out, in 401 paces against a straight-line count of 400. One look up, honestly taken, was worth more than 300 m of careful marching.

Example 2: Level 5, Thataway — “about ENE” is not enough, twice corrected is

The finale briefs you the way people actually brief: “about ENE” — 67.5° — for a tower that truly bears 71°, 420 m away, with a 5° veer in your stride working the whole walk. Open loop, the script stopped 24.1 m out, beyond the 20 m ring: close enough to be annoying, which is exactly where uncorrected reckoning leaves you. With its two fixes spent when its error passed 8 m, it arrived 16.5 m out, in 540 paces against a straight-line count of 560. The briefing never got more precise. The walker just corrected, twice.

Example 3: the integrator does not cheat

Turn every drift off — no veer, no wander, no pace variation — and the core must reduce to pure geometry. The test walks four legs that way (63° for 173 paces among them) and compares against the closed-form answer: the landing points agree to within 4 × 10⁻¹³ m. The drift in this game is a deliberate, declared model laid over exact arithmetic — never sloppiness in the sums.

How it’s built — the coding choices

A seeded walk, so a proof can replay it

Every step’s veer, wander, and pace length come from a small seeded random generator (mulberry32) inside the core. Same seed, same walk, step for step — which is what makes the proof walks above possible at all: the test doesn’t sample the game’s behaviour, it replays it, deterministically, and reports where the walker stopped. When you retry a level, the seed shifts, so the drift you meet is a fresh walk rather than a memorised one.

Step-by-step integration, with the drift declared in the open

Walking is integrated one pace at a time: each step’s heading is the called bearing plus the level’s bias, plus the current wander (itself a random walk, updated per step), plus a whisper of independent noise; each step’s length is your pace length varied by a few percent. Nothing is hidden in a closed-form shortcut — the true track the game reveals at the end is the literal sum of the steps you took, and Example 3 above is the receipt that the summation itself is exact when the declared drift is zero.

Two positions, honestly kept apart

The core tracks only the truth. Your believed position — the notebook — is kept by the page, advanced by pure geometry from your own calls, and corrected only by the events that would correct a real notebook: a fix, or arriving somewhere you can name. The game never lets the notebook peek at the truth, which is why the script in the proof can stand in for you: it is given exactly the information you get, and no more.

The fix logic

A fix succeeds only if a known feature — landmark, waypoint, or the destination itself — is within the level’s sight range of where you actually stand (not where your notebook claims). It then reports your true position and the fresh bearing and distance to your current mark, computed from the truth. A failed fix costs nothing but the walk to somewhere more knowable. The rationing is the lesson: fixes are the scarcest resource in navigation, and the game prices them at five points each so “look up early, look up honestly” is a strategy, not a slogan.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the field is flat and featureless to walk — no slope, mud, fences, or fatigue, all of which shorten a real pace and bend a real line; the game varies pace length only by a small random few percent and never tires. The wander is a per-step random walk in heading; a real veer is often one consistent pull (the game’s bias term) tangled with terrain that pushes you around obstacles. Landmarks are always identifiable within sight range, in clear weather, with no similar-looking tree one valley over — real fixes can be wrong, and a wrong fix is worse than none. Reaching a waypoint or taking a fix gives your position exactly; on the ground you’d know it to within a small area, not a point. Bearings are true bearings throughout (see the declination note above). The pond is the only ground that can stop you, and it stops you politely, at its edge. Distances are metres and paces are single steps throughout, as stated.
