Every map starts with the same small dot: the place you are standing. This room has a real map you can explore, honest notes on what its buttons do with your location, lessons on reading any map at all, and a compass game for finding your way.

The map needs the internet; the lessons and the trainer do not. Nothing here needs an account.

01 / THE LIVE MAP

A real map, right here.

This is a working map of the whole world, drawn by OpenStreetMap, the map that volunteers everywhere build together. It opens on Defiance, Ohio, home base of this collection. Drag to wander. Scroll or pinch to zoom. The little ruler at the bottom corner is the scale bar, and it redraws itself every time you zoom; watch it while you try.

Find me asks your device where it is and uses the answer only to center this map. Saying no is fine; the ZIP box does the same job with a place name instead of your location. The full plain-language version is in the footer.

02 / READING AND TRAIL SKILLS

Useful Maps

A map is a drawing with rules, and the rules are learnable. These are the ones that unlock every map you will ever meet, paper or screen: how to read what the map is saying, and how to use it out on a trail, where Scouts learn to find the way and find it back.

The legend

On every map · Reading a map

A map's symbols mean nothing until the legend speaks for them: this line is a road, that blue is water, this little tent is a campground. Every map has its own legend, so check it first, even on a map that looks familiar. On the map above, the legend is the map itself: zoom in and the symbols introduce themselves with labels.

First thing to read on any new map

The scale bar

On every map · Reading a map

Scale is the map's promise about distance: this much paper, that much world. The bar at the corner of the map above stretches and shrinks as you zoom, always telling the truth about the ground under it. Paper maps print it as a bar too, or as a ratio like 1:24,000, which means one inch on the map is 24,000 inches of real trail.

Trust the bar, not your thumb

The north arrow

On every map · Reading a map

Maps keep north at the top unless the arrow says otherwise, and the compass rose is the arrow grown up: a star that shows every direction at once. North is 0°, east is 90°, south 180°, west 270°, and the points in between have names of their own. The trainer below teaches all sixteen.

North up, unless told otherwise

Contour lines

On trail and topo maps · Reading a map

Hills drawn as rings. Each ring is a line of equal height, so a hilltop is a set of closed rings, one inside the next. Rings packed close together mean steep ground; rings spread wide mean a gentle walk. Government trail maps (topographic maps) draw the whole landscape this way, and the Keep Going shelf links to them.

Close rings, steep climb

Orient the map

Trail skill · Scouts learn this first

Before a paper map can help, turn it until north on the paper points at real north. Set the compass on the map, turn both together until the needle and the map's north agree, and suddenly every hill and bend on the paper lines up with the ground in front of you. An oriented map reads like a picture of where you stand.

Turn the paper, not yourself

Take a bearing

Trail skill · Walking a straight line

A bearing is a direction with a number: the degrees you walk to go somewhere. Point the compass's direction-of-travel arrow at where you want to end up, turn the dial until its north lines up with the needle, and the number at the arrow is your bearing. Then walk keeping the needle lined up. Northeast is 45°; the trainer below makes the whole rose familiar.

A direction you can walk

Pace counting

Trail skill · Knowing how far

Maps give distances; legs have to measure them. Count a pace every time the same foot lands, and learn how many of your paces cover 100 yards on flat ground (measure it once on a football field or a measured trail). Then count as you walk. Scouts move a bead or a pebble from pocket to pocket every hundred paces so the count survives conversation.

Your legs are a ruler

Find the way back

Trail skill · The return leg

Every direction has an opposite, exactly 180° away: northeast out, southwest back. If you walked a bearing of 45° to get somewhere, 225° walks you home. The trainer below names the opposite after every right answer, because the way back is half of every trip.

Out one bearing, home on its opposite

Go Thataway — the game

The game · Dead reckoning on foot

Five walks across country with nothing but a bearing and your own pace count. Your stride veers, your reckoning drifts, and the map shows only where your notebook thinks you are — until you take a fix on a landmark and learn the truth. Correction beats precision, every time. Play Go Thataway → Its back-door lesson shows the arithmetic of drift and the proof walks that finished every level before it shipped.

Go thataway — and get there

Battleship — the game

The game · Search by halves

Two fleets hide on two 10×10 charts. The smallest ship is two squares long, so every ship afloat must cover a black square of the checkerboard — hunt the black squares and you search half the ocean, guaranteed. A miss is information; a hit starts the target walk. The house captain hunts the same honest pattern against you. Play Battleship → Its back-door lesson proves the parity claim by enumerating all 600 legal placements, and prints the captain's measured average: 51.7 shots against random fire's 95.5.

Search half the chart — on purpose

Build a Map

Reading maps is half the room; making one is the other half. Bring your own places and plot them on the map above. A CSV file of pins (columns: name, lat, lon, and an optional notes) becomes a marker for every row. Give rows a shared group name and they connect in file order as a path; add close set to yes and the last point joins back to the first, closing the shape. A PDF is read for coordinate pairs written like 41.2845, -84.3558, and every pair found becomes a pin. Or switch on click-to-add and drop pins by hand: drag them to move them, and a dashed trail joins your pins in order with its total distance.

Files are read right here in your browser and never uploaded anywhere. Your pins live only on this page, until you close it.

The compass-rose trainer.

04 / KEEP GOING

Shelves worth your time.

  • OpenStreetMap — the map this room runs on, drawn by volunteers. Zoom into your own street; if a path is missing, anyone can add it. Free, no account needed to look.
  • The National Map viewer (USGS) — the US government's topographic maps in your browser: contour lines, trails, and place names for the whole country, free.
  • USGS: The National Map — what topographic maps are, and how to download them as files to keep, from the agency that has surveyed the country since 1879. Free.
  • Compass Dude — patient, free lessons on using a real compass: parts, bearings, and declination, with pictures. Run by a compass enthusiast, ads aside.
  • Find It — the sister room: tap Coffee, Pharmacy, Hospital and more, and get the nearest ones on a map with directions (included in the full collection).
  • Weather Everywhere — the weather room; knowing where you are pairs well with knowing what is coming (included in the full collection).
  • Tonight's Sky — the sky room; sailors and Scouts alike have navigated by it (included in the full collection).
  • Numbers in the Wild — the math room; bearings and scale are numbers wearing boots (included in the full collection).

About location and privacy, plainly: the Find me button asks your device for its location and uses the answer only to center the map; it is not stored and not sent anywhere. ZIP and town searches are sent to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service to find the place, and the map's pictures (tiles) come from OpenStreetMap's servers, which see the area you are viewing, the way any map site does. The map library, Leaflet, loads from the unpkg content network. Files you open with Build a Map are read right here in your browser and are never sent anywhere; the pins you make exist only on this page until you close it, and the PDF reader (pdf.js, by Mozilla) loads from unpkg only when you open a PDF. No accounts, no trackers, no ads, and nothing about you is kept by this page.
