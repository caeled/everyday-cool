Sphere Bench: the joke is the door, the geometry is what's behind it

On November 18, 2026, xkcd published #3326, "Voyager Crash": Sad news: NASA has announced that Voyager 1 has crashed into the crystal sphere holding the fixed stars. The drawing shows the probe's dashed path ending in a burst against a vast faceted shell — a sphere built out of flat plates. Steven spotted it in his feed, and his bench got its subtitle and its subject in one move: "Archimedes AI — building a new sphere from sometimes square angles."

That is the working principle of this whole room, stated by Steven plainly: it starts with humor one could investigate — the joke is the door, the geometry is what's behind it. The joke here is a good door, because the faceted sphere in the cartoon is not a cartoonist's invention. It is what you actually get, every time, when anyone — a Greek with dividers, a dome builder, a starship crew, a civilisation wrapping its sun — tries to build something round out of flat pieces. This lesson walks through the three doors the joke opens: the fiction that borrowed the shape, the measurement that started it, and the counting that governs every closed sphere ever built.

First door: the fiction you may already have met

Three fictions borrow this geometry, and they borrow it accurately enough to be worth naming. Fiction is labelled fiction here — but notice how much real geometry each one is carrying.

Spelljammer's crystal spheres (Dungeons & Dragons, 1989). In the Spelljammer setting, every solar system floats inside a gigantic crystal sphere, and ships sail the phlogiston between them. The fantasy needs the sphere to close — no gaps, no edge — and "a shell of flat-ish pieces that closes with nothing left over" is exactly Euler's subject, two sections down. The ancient world believed the real version: the stars fixed to a rotating celestial sphere, which is the sphere Voyager "hit" in the comic.

The Tholian Web (Star Trek, "The Tholian Web", 1968). Tholian ships weave a cage around the Enterprise out of straight strands of energy, filament by filament, until the lattice closes and the ship inside it cannot leave. That is the bench's whole thesis performed as a weapon: a round volume, enclosed entirely by straight lines. No curved piece is ever needed — enough straight pieces, at enough small angles, make a prison that is round for all practical purposes.

Dyson spheres (a thousand stories since 1960). Fiction's default picture is a solid shell around a star — a sphere the size of an orbit, built in one piece. The solid shell is the one version that cannot work, for a geometric reason this bench demonstrates rather than asserts (last section). What Freeman Dyson actually proposed in his 1960 paper was a swarm: a loose cloud of independent collectors, each on its own orbit. The stories kept the name and lost the engineering; the bench keeps both.

Second door: Archimedes measures the circle

Around 225 BCE, Archimedes wanted the circumference of a circle and had almost nothing to work with: no trigonometry, no decimal notation, just fractions and square roots. His method — Measurement of a Circle, proposition 3 — is the squeeze. Draw a regular polygon inside the circle: its perimeter is too short. Draw one outside: too long. π is trapped between them. For a circle of radius 1 the two bounds are:

n · sin(π/n)  <  π  <  n · tan(π/n)

Then double the sides and squeeze again. Archimedes started from the hexagon and doubled four times — 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 sides — computing each new perimeter from the last with square roots he had to bound by fractions as he went (his bound on √3 alone, 265/153 < √3 < 1351/780, is a small masterpiece). At every step he rounded outward — the inside estimate down, the outside estimate up — so the trap could never accidentally exclude π. His published result:

223/71  <  π  <  22/7   — that is, 3.1408 < π < 3.1429

The bench's first panel runs his procedure with exact modern arithmetic. The trap it draws at 96 sides (3.141032 < π < 3.142715) is a hair tighter than his — the honest difference between having a sine key and having to be the sine key — and the page shows his fractions beside the exact values rather than quietly replacing them.

Third door: counting a sphere — Euler and the twelve pentagons

To build a sphere from flat pieces, start with the roundest flat-faced solid there is: the icosahedron, 20 equilateral triangles. Cut each face into ν² smaller triangles and push the new corners outward onto the sphere, and the facets shrink toward roundness. The counts at each frequency are exact, and they are not a coincidence:

F = 20ν²   ·   E = 30ν²   ·   V = 10ν² + 2   ·   V − E + F = 2

That last identity is Euler's formula, and it holds for every closed convex solid, at every subdivision, forever. The bench doesn't ask you to believe it: the proof table counts vertices, edges and faces from the built mesh and checks the identity against the counts. Subdivide to ν = 4 — 320 triangles — and it still balances to 2.

The soccer ball is the same story told with two shapes: 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons. Why twelve? Flat hexagons tile a bathroom floor with zero curvature — three meeting at a corner make exactly 360°. A sphere needs its surface to curve away in total by 720° (Descartes' theorem: the angle defect of any closed convex solid sums to 720°, a full two turns). Swap one hexagon for a pentagon and you introduce exactly 60° of defect. 720 ÷ 60 = 12. So any sphere built from hexagons needs exactly twelve pentagons — no more, no fewer, at any size, at any fineness. That is why the comic's crystal sphere, a soccer ball, and a geodesic dome are all cousins: the pentagons never multiply; all the curvature lives in those twelve.

The counting, built: geodesic domes

Cut Panel 2's sphere at the ground and stand it up, and you have the most successful piece of this geometry ever built: the geodesic dome. Buckminster Fuller patented it in 1954 and spent the rest of his life promoting it; the shape had appeared earlier — Walther Bauersfeld built a geodesic planetarium shell at Jena in the 1920s — but Fuller named it, proved it, and evangelised it. The bench's third panel is the same mesh with the bottom half dropped: the triangles are its panels, the edges are its struts, the vertices its joints, and every count is taken from the cut mesh.

The first one most people meet is a greenhouse. A dome greenhouse makes sense for reasons that are geometry before they are gardening: the triangulated shell is stiff for its weight, so it spans a useful space with no internal posts; there is no flat wall for the wind to push on, so loads spread through the whole shell instead of concentrating on one face; and the curved roof meets the sun at some good angle all day long. Stated carefully: a dome is not magic insulation, and snow still has to be reckoned with — it is the structure that is efficient, strength per kilogram of material. Garden domes are usually the ν = 2 hemisphere on the bench: 65 struts in only two distinct lengths — 0.546533 and 0.618034 of the radius. Cut two piles of pipe, drill them alike, bolt the shell together. That is the whole manufacturing secret, and it is a counting fact before it is a building fact.

The same counting scales. The Eden Project in Cornwall (opened 2001) grows a rainforest inside geodesic domes — the largest indoor rainforest in the world — in a worked-out china-clay pit. Spaceship Earth at Epcot (1982) skips the cut and stands the whole geodesic sphere on legs, 180 feet across. And the white golf-ball domes on hilltops are radomes: geodesic shells around radar antennas, rigid enough to keep the weather off, built from materials the radar beam passes straight through. Domes of all kinds — one mesh.

One honest wrinkle, and the bench shows it rather than hiding it: the clean hemisphere exists only at even frequencies. At ν = 2 and ν = 4 the subdivision puts vertices exactly on the equator, the rim lands at height 0, and the dome is precisely half the sphere — 40 of its 80 triangles at ν = 2. At ν = 3 no vertex sits on the equator, so the cut stops a row of triangles short and the rim zigzags between heights 0.172 and 0.188 of the radius. Real 3-frequency kits meet the same fact and answer it by cutting a 3/8 or 5/8 sphere instead, or by standing the dome on a short stem wall. And Euler's formula keeps the books for the open shell too: a dome is a disk, not a ball, so its count balances at V − E + F = 1 — one less than the sphere it was cut from, at every frequency. The proof table checks that as well.

The Dyson panel: shell vs swarm

Newton's shell theorem says that inside a uniform spherical shell, gravity cancels exactly — every pull from one side is balanced by a pull from the other. One consequence is rarely stated as bluntly as it deserves: a star exerts no net force on a shell built around it, and nothing centres the shell. Centre it perfectly and it floats; nudge it once and it drifts at constant speed, forever, until its inner wall meets the star. No material fixes this — it is not a strength problem. It is the geometry of the situation, and the bench's third panel steps it through: one nudge, a monotone drift, and contact. (A solid shell has a second, independent problem: no known material could hold one up against the star's gravity at the shell's own scale. But the drift alone is already fatal, and the drift is pure geometry.)

Dyson's swarm has no centre to lose. Each panel is in an ordinary orbit, answerable only to gravity in the ordinary way, and the swarm's bookkeeping is trivially honest: panels covering a fraction c of the sky around the star intercept a fraction c of its light. The bench measures that by casting rays, and the proof table checks the measurement against the setting.

The honest notes. Four simplifications, named. First, the π display: the historical squeeze 3.1408 < π < 3.1429 is Archimedes' published fractions (223/71 and 22/7) at four decimals — the raw 96-gon perimeters, computed with the exact trigonometry he didn't have, come out slightly tighter (3.141032 and 3.142715). The page shows both and the proof checks both; nothing was rounded into his numbers to make them match. Second, the Dyson panel is a flat 2-D slice: the shell is a ring, the swarm shares a single orbit, and the drift is drawn at constant speed because the shell theorem's point is precisely that there is no force to change it. Real swarm engineering — many orbits, inclinations, station-keeping — is beyond the panel and not needed for the argument. Third, the swarm's radial deviation measures at 2.84e-14 rather than exactly 0 because the panels' positions are computed with sines and cosines in floating point; that number is the machine's rounding, not the orbit's. Fourth, the dome's rim: the panel keeps every triangle that lies entirely at or above the cut, so at odd frequencies — where the subdivision puts no vertex on the equator — the rim stops one row of triangles short and zigzags slightly (at ν = 3, between 0.172 and 0.188 of the radius). The page prints the rim heights instead of quietly levelling the base; real kits solve the same problem with a 3/8 or 5/8 cut, or a short stem wall.
