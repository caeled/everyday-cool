## The Mathematics Wave · 23 November 2026

At Steven Powell's direction, seven mathematics exhibits built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) were integrated into this room byte-for-byte: `graph-pathfinding-arena.html`, `vector-calculus-fields.html`, `probability-markov-lab.html`, `linear-algebra-eigen.html`, `hyperbolic-geometry.html`, `strange-attractors-3d.html`, and `fourier-epicycles.html`. No guest file was edited; sha256 hashes of the integrated copies match Steven's uploads exactly. Muse audited each by driving its shipped code headlessly and wrote the seven lessons: `graph-pathfinding-arena-lesson.html`, `vector-calculus-fields-lesson.html`, `probability-markov-lab-lesson.html`, `linear-algebra-eigen-lesson.html`, `hyperbolic-geometry-lesson.html`, `strange-attractors-3d-lesson.html`, and `fourier-epicycles-lesson.html`.

Headline audit results, printed in full in the lessons: Pathfinding's A* and Dijkstra are exactly optimal in 4-way mode, but the page crashes during its own start-up (a null solver read in init; the animation never starts until a control revives it), Bidirectional A* returns suboptimal paths (52 vs 48 on the mud patch; suboptimal in 10/30 fuzz grids), and the default Manhattan heuristic with the diagonals toggle is inadmissible (overestimates by up to +7.03), breaking the page's "exact shortest path" guarantee in a combination the page itself offers. Nabla's loop integrals satisfy Green's theorem and the divergence theorem to four decimals on every preset and its Taylor coefficients are exact, but its Magnetic Dipole preset's "divergence-free" claim is false (∇·F = +2.449 measured, flux +2.946). The Markov lab's stationary solver matches independent linear algebra to 1×10⁻¹⁰ and its ruin walkers match the textbook, but burst mode respawns absorbed walkers so its bars hold ≈31% transient mass against their plotted equilibrium, and Ehrenfest's convergence belongs to the cumulative average, not the chain. The Eigen lab is exact — every real eigenpair to residuals ≤ 2.5×10⁻¹⁶, SVD reconstruction to 2.2×10⁻¹⁶ — with one false toggle label ("Normalize Orbit Steps (xₙ / ‖xₙ‖)" computes xₙ/‖xₙ‖ × ‖x₀‖) and an unstated 10⁻⁶ discriminant tolerance that misnames near-degenerate matrices. Hyperbolic Geometry reproduces the analytic Poincaré model to near machine precision (distance worst error 9.1×10⁻¹⁴ over 1,332 pairs; all six tessellations exact) but `hypDist` silently caps at 16.811 and its "Ideal" button reaches 96.4% of π. Strange Attractors ships six canonical systems with genuine RK4 (Lorenz Lyapunov measured +0.905 vs canonical ≈0.906); its one overstated label is Halvorsen's "Periodic Knot" (λ ≈ +0.05). Fourier Epicycles' DFT is exact (coefficients ≤ 1.3×10⁻¹² from independent on all seven presets) but its "~9% Gibbs" label is false as shipped (4.2% at its own defaults, decaying with N) and its arc-length resampler rewrites the spectra it feeds the transform.

## Fractal and Warp · 24 November 2026

Two of Steven Powell's own Python originals from his Bordeom workshop were ported to the web and wired into this room: `fractal.html` with its lesson `fractal-lesson.html`, and `warp.html` with its lesson `warp-lesson.html`. Both originals are Steven's Python (numpy and pygame); the web ports are by Muse, with the maths carried over line for line — Fractal's compute, smooth escape colouring, iteration budget, and six computed dive targets, and Warp's nine index-picture generators and five seamless palettes. Each page runs the original's own self-test as a live proof table: Fractal 13/13 proofs, Warp 15/15 proofs. They are a pair: in the Python, `fractal.py` imported its palettes from `warp.py` (`from warp import PALETTES`), and both pages here carry the same shared palette module — Fractal's Acidwarp-style palette cycling runs on Warp's palettes.

Fractal is a Mandelbrot / Julia zoomer whose dive mode falls into spiral valleys (Seahorse valley, Triple spiral valley, Spiral, Deep spiral, Elephant valley, Misiurewicz spirals — six targets found by computation, refined by zooming repeatedly toward the most detailed pixel). Warp is a tribute to Acidwarp, the 1992–93 DOS screen saver by Noah Spurrier and Mark Bilk: nine index-picture generators (rings, spiral, waves, plasma, kaleido, interference, tunnel, warp, julia) draw one still picture of palette indexes, and nothing is redrawn — the palette rotates and the colours do all the moving.

## Sphere Bench · 24 November 2026

At Steven Powell's direction, a new Muse original was built for this room and wired in: `sphere-bench.html` with its lesson `sphere-bench-lesson.html` — **Sphere Bench — building round things from flat pieces**, subtitled by Steven (verbatim on the page): "Archimedes AI — building a new sphere from sometimes square angles." The idea came out of his feed the same morning: an xkcd comic about Voyager 1 crashing into the crystal sphere that holds the fixed stars, and the thread under it — Spelljammer's crystal spheres, the Tholian Web from Star Trek, Dyson spheres — all of them, in Steven's words, "circles being built with another shape," and his ruling on the room: "its geometry for godsakes! the geometry of the circle."

The bench has three panels. Panel 1: Archimedes measures the circle — a unit circle trapped between an inscribed and a circumscribed regular polygon, the sides doubling (3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 96) while the two perimeters squeeze π, reading Archimedes' own 3.1408 < π < 3.1429 at 96 sides. Panel 2: build a sphere from flat pieces — an icosahedron subdivided at frequency ν = 1..4 with live V, E, F counts (F = 20ν²) and Euler's formula balancing at every step, plus a soccer-ball view whose twelve pentagons are counted from the shipped geometry: exactly 12 pentagons, 20 hexagons, at every subdivision. Panel 3: shell vs swarm — a solid faceted Dyson shell, nudged, drifts until it contacts its star (inside a uniform shell there is no net pull, so nothing recentres it), while the swarm of independent flat panels holds its orbits and catches a fraction of the starlight equal to its coverage. The page's `runProofs()` asserts all of it with numbers — the Archimedes bounds straddling π and narrowing monotonically, F = 20ν² and V − E + F = 2 at every ν, the pentagon count, the shell's drift to contact against the swarm's bounded orbits, the light fraction — 11/11 proofs passing against the shipped code.

The hero artwork `media-generation-sphere-bench-archimedes-0-e887416b-bdb9-492d-9a9f-7e0a5cf5e695.webp` is AI-generated at Steven's suggestion ("Archimedes AI measuring"): the collection's robot in a Greek himation and laurel, measuring a large circle with brass dividers, polygons inscribed and circumscribed. The lesson carries the fiction doorways by name — Dyson spheres, the Tholian Web (Star Trek), and Spelljammer's crystal spheres (D&D) — as the examples kids may already have met in fiction, each tied to the geometry it borrows, with the xkcd Voyager joke as the hook; fiction labelled as fiction, geometry as geometry.

## The Deep Dives — Algebra, Geometry, Trigonometry · 24 November 2026

At Steven Powell's direction (the deep-dive commission, 24 November 2026), three Muse originals were built for this room and wired in: `algebra-deep-dive.html` with its lesson `algebra-deep-dive-lesson.html`, `geometry-deep-dive.html` with its lesson `geometry-deep-dive-lesson.html`, and `trigonometry-deep-dive.html` with its lesson `trigonometry-deep-dive-lesson.html`. Steven's commission named the contents — the classic high-school formulas, several methods of solving the same problem (matrices in algebra particularly, at his word), a bench for moving terms around an equation and watching the effect, and a tracer that runs test numbers through an equation step by step — and carried a stretch he gave to Muse rather than the guest builders: first locate the public discussion of each subject across the Metasphere (Instagram, Facebook, Threads), take the five most-discussed posts in each, and put that social layer into the workshops. The research is banked at `~/workspace/your_files/math-social-bank_2026-11-24.md` (60 posts per subject, engagement-ranked, pulled 24 November 2026); each deep dive's From the Discussion panel carries its subject's five with links and the counts as found that day, labelled as a dated snapshot.

**Algebra Deep Dive — four roads, one answer.** The discussion's most-argued problem, x² + 6x − 16 = 0 (2,000 comments), solved four computed ways (factoring, quadratic formula, completing the square, graphing); a derivation bench that completes the square on the visitor's own a, b, c; the Olympiad cubic x³ + x = 30 by integer-root hunting; systems of equations three ways — elimination, Cramer's Rule with the 3×3 determinant expanded visibly, and Gauss–Jordan with every row operation printed (matrices took two of the discussion's top five, and the three roads must agree before the verdict lights); a move-the-terms balance lab starting from the crowd's 4(y − 3) = 8, with a Cheat button that changes one side only so the TRUE/FALSE verdict catches it; and a number tracer that reduces an expression innermost-first with each intermediate value in place. The page's `runProofs()` passes 19/19 against the shipped code.

**Geometry Deep Dive — derive πr² yourself.** The two most-discussed geometry posts are both derivations of the circle's area (2,600 and 400 comments), so the centerpiece is a derive-it-yourself bench: sectors of a circle rearranged toward a parallelogram, the error against πr² measured at each slicing (9.97% at n = 8 down to 0.010% at n = 256, roughly quartering per doubling, monotone across the slider), presented honestly as a limit argument with convergence numbers rather than a proof by picture. Around it: a 22-card formula canon, a Pythagoras lab with the squares drawn to scale and a converse tester, a rearrange lab solving four formulas for any of their variables (24 round trips, worst relative error 1.45×10⁻¹⁶), polygon angle sums and a transversal bench, and the memorize-versus-derive argument — cheatsheets and Cuemath's anti-memorization video both linked. 10/10 proofs pass against the shipped code.

**Trigonometry Deep Dive — check the sheet.** The trig feed is a wall of formula sheets whose comment threads cluster on appreciation and correction, so the centerpiece is a Check the Sheet bench: sixteen claimed identities tested at 200 seeded random angles, the fourteen true ones holding under 1×10⁻¹² (worst 6.54×10⁻¹³, at the tangent poles, counted and explained) and two planted fakes — sin(a + b) = sin a + sin b and sin²θ + cos²θ = 1/cos θ — convicted with printed counterexample angles. A unit-circle bench draws sin, cos, and tan as lengths and traces their graphs from the circle itself; a Solve-It lab handles the SSA case honestly (exactly 2, 1, or 0 solutions, labelled in words, never a NaN); a tracer runs sin 75° by two routes that agree to the last printed digit. 28/28 proofs pass against the shipped code, with 19 further driven checks recorded in the lesson. The lesson states plainly that 200-angle verification is overwhelming evidence, not a proof, and says where the derivations live.
