What you are looking at

Algebra Deep Dive is the algebra bench of Everyday Math: the classic high-school formulas on cards, and then — because the internet's favourite algebra argument is never about the answer, always about the method — one famous equation solved four different ways, a machine that derives the quadratic formula in front of you, a matrices lab, a balance you can push terms around on, and a tracer that walks a number through an equation one operation at a time. Everything is computed live from the numbers you type. Nothing is a picture of mathematics; it is mathematics, running.

Panel 1 — The formula canon

The major formulas of a high-school algebra course, one card each: what it says in symbols, what it says in one plain sentence, and one worked micro-example the page computes itself. Every card has a Load into the lab button that carries its numbers into whichever bench uses them — the quadratic formula card loads the arena, the distance card loads the tracer, and so on.

Panel 2 — The methods arena

The most-discussed algebra equation on the social feeds in November 2026 was x² + 6x − 16 = 0 (about 2,000 comments), and the comments argued about how to solve it. So here it is four ways — factoring, the quadratic formula, completing the square, and graphing — every step computed from the coefficients you enter. Change a, b, c and all four roads rebuild. Below it, the derivation bench completes the square on ax² + bx + c = 0 with your numbers substituted into every step, which is how the formula is born — and the Olympiad corner hunts the integer root of x³ + x = 30 the way the crowd did, then proves with the factor theorem that the other two roots are not real.

Panel 3 — Matrices & systems

Two of the five most-discussed algebra posts were about Cramer's Rule, so systems get three roads too: elimination / substitution, Cramer's Rule (determinants computed visibly, cofactor expansion shown for 3×3), and the matrix inverse by Gauss–Jordan, with the row operations printed. All three must land on the same solution — the page checks, and the proofs below measure it. A system with determinant zero is labelled singular, never papered over with NaNs.

Panel 4 — Move the terms

A balance workspace. Click a term chip to move it across the equals sign — its sign flips, and the page says so. Buttons add, subtract, multiply, or divide both sides at once. Both sides are evaluated live at your test value, with a TRUE / FALSE verdict on the equality. There is also a button that cheats — changes only one side — so you can watch the verdict catch it. That is the whole law of equation-solving: whatever you do, do it to both sides.

Panel 5 — Number tracer

Type an expression and a value for each variable, and the tracer evaluates it the way you were taught: innermost brackets first, one operation at a time, each intermediate value printed in place, until a single number remains. Try 4*(y-3) at y = 5 — the crowd's two-step equation, watched from inside.

Panel 6 — From the discussion

The five most-discussed algebra posts found on Instagram, Facebook, and Threads on 24 November 2026, engagement-ranked, with links and the counts as found that day — and a fair summary of what the comments actually argued about. Counts move; the argument (methods, not answers) is the durable part.

The proofs

The last panel runs runProofs(): every claim on this page — all four arena roads landing on the same roots, the derivation instantiating for a negative a, three system methods agreeing to 1e-9, legal moves preserving truth, an illegal move getting caught, tracer steps matching hand computation, a singular system detected — measured against the page's own code, in your browser, now.

Panel 2b · The derivation bench — where the formula comes from

This is the 180-comment post — "Derive Quadratic Formula," worked in red and blue ink — made interactive. The steps are a template instantiated with your coefficients (the lesson says plainly: this is not a computer algebra system). Try a negative a: the algebra does not care, and neither does the formula.

Panel 2c · Olympiad corner — x³ + x = 30

A Math Olympiad reel (190 comments) set the crowd hunting for a whole-number root. The hunt is a real method — the rational root theorem says any rational root of x³ + x − 30 divides 30 — and the factor theorem finishes the job: divide the root out and interrogate what's left.

Panel 3 · Matrices & systems — three roads, one solution

Road 1 · Elimination / substitution

Road 2 · Cramer's Rule

Road 3 · Matrix inverse (Gauss–Jordan)

Steven's favourite road is the matrix road, and the crowd backed him: two of the five most-discussed algebra posts were Cramer's Rule. Cramer's Rule is beautiful and honest about its price — one determinant per unknown, plus one for the system. The inverse road pays once and then answers every right-hand side you will ever meet.

Panel 4 · Move the terms — the balance

Click any term chip to move it across the equals sign — its sign flips, and the log says what happened. The verdict evaluates both sides at your test value after every move. Legal moves keep a true equation true at its solution; the cheat button breaks the law on purpose so you can watch the verdict catch it.

Panel 5 · Number tracer — walk a value through the equation

The tracer substitutes your numbers, then reduces the expression the way you were taught: innermost brackets first, one operation at a time, each intermediate value printed where it happened. Supported: + − * / ^, brackets, sqrt(...), and the variables x and y. Watch 4(y − 3) become 8 at y = 5 — exactly the right-hand side of the crowd's equation, which is why y = 5 solves it.
