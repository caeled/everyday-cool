Hyperbolic Geometry — the lesson

The exhibit is a working model of a universe where Euclid's fifth postulate is false: the Poincaré disk, a finite round picture of an infinite hyperbolic plane. Inside it, "straight lines" are circular arcs that meet the rim at right angles, triangles are too thin — their angles add to less than 180° — and through a single point off a line there pass not one parallel, not none, but infinitely many lines that never meet it. The exhibit lets you drag the geometry around: six regular tessellations {p, q}, a triangle lab, a parallel-postulate lab, geodesic billiards, horocycles and hyperbolic circles, and the same plane redrawn in the Beltrami–Klein and upper half-plane models. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the mathematics, and the audit that drove the shipped code headlessly and checked every one of its claims against the analytic Poincaré model computed independently.

Verdict: the mathematics is real, and it is exact where it counts. The shipped distance function matched the independent analytic formula on 1,332 point pairs to a worst error of 9.1 × 10−14. Triangle angles, the angle of parallelism, circle circumferences, horocycle tangency, both alternate projections, and all six tessellations checked out against formulas the audit computed from scratch — in several cases to the last printed digit. One function silently stops at the edge of the world instead of blowing up (boxed below), one button labelled "Ideal" delivers a triangle that is 96.4% of ideal, and the numbers hard-coded into the page's HTML before the script runs are stale. Everything the live exhibit actually displays, the audit could reproduce independently.

FALSE at the limit — the distance function has a ceiling it never mentions. hypDist clamps its translated radius at 0.9999999, so the largest distance it can ever return is 2·artanh(0.9999999) = 16.811. Fed two genuine disk points at (±0.9999, 0), whose true hyperbolic distance is 19.807, it returns 16.811. Fed a point on the boundary — true distance infinite — it returns 16.811. Fed points far outside the disk — (±2, 0), (±10, 0), where distance is not even defined — it returns the same 16.811, with no error and no NaN. The exhibit's interface keeps you away from this: draggable handles clamp at |z| = 0.985, where the largest reachable distance is about 9.8, so no number the page displays is wrong. But the function itself, asked about the edge of its world, quietly invents a finite answer instead of diverging — and in hyperbolic geometry, "distance to the horizon is infinite" is the whole point of the horizon.

Also not as labelled: the Triangle Lab's "Ideal (Area → π)" button places the vertices at Euclidean radius 0.968, inside the disk. The resulting triangle has angles of 2.151° each (sum 6.452°) and area 3.029 — 96.4% of π, with finite sides of length 7.951. A true ideal triangle has its vertices on |z| = 1, all angles exactly 0°, and infinite sides. Even dragging all three handles to their 0.985 clamp only reaches 98.3% of π. The button is a good demonstration of the limit; it is not the limit itself.

The mathematics

The disk, the metric, and why the rim is infinitely far away

The Poincaré disk is the open unit disk |z| < 1 with a stretched ruler: distances are measured by

d(z, w) = 2·artanh( |z − w| / |1 − z·conj(w)| ) = arcosh( 1 + 2|z−w|² / ((1−|z|²)(1−|w|²)) ) The second form is the one the audit used as its independent standard. As either point approaches the rim, the denominator (1 − |z|²) collapses and the distance runs away to infinity: the rim is a horizon, not a wall. From the centre, a point at Euclidean radius r is at hyperbolic distance 2·artanh(r) — so r = 0.5 is 1.099 away, r = 0.99 is 5.293 away, and r = 0.9999 is 9.903 away. The exhibit's reference card states this distance formula, and Receipt 1 shows the code really computes it.

The model is conformal: angles in the picture are true hyperbolic angles. That one fact powers the whole exhibit — the triangle lab can read angles off the picture honestly, the billiards lab can reflect rays by reflecting their picture, and a geodesic (the hyperbolic "straight line") is exactly a circular arc meeting the rim orthogonally, or a diameter.

Triangles: the defect is the area

Gauss–Bonnet, in curvature K = −1, says a hyperbolic triangle's area is not computed from its sides at all — it is the amount by which its angle sum falls short of 180°:

Area(△) = π − (α + β + γ) So no hyperbolic triangle can have area π or more, the ideal triangle (all vertices on the horizon, all angles 0°) is the largest possible triangle, and a tiny triangle — where the geometry is nearly Euclidean — has a sum near 180° and a near-zero area. Defect and area are the same number wearing two hats, which is why the audit checked the angles two independent ways (Receipt 2): if the angles are honest, the area claim follows.

Parallels: the angle Π(d) and the fan beyond it

Given a line L and a point P at hyperbolic distance d from it, Lobachevsky's angle of parallelism Π(d) = 2·atan(e−d) is the angle at P between the perpendicular to L and each of the two limiting parallels — the lines through P that meet L only at the horizon. Directions at P inside the 2Π(d) sector facing L cross it; every direction outside that sector gives an ultraparallel, a line through P that never meets L anywhere. The ultraparallel directions form a fan of 180° − 2Π(d), and every single one of them is a distinct line — infinitely many parallels, which is the exhibit's central scandal and Receipt 3's test.

Circles grow exponentially

C(r) = 2π·sinh(r)   ·   A(r) = 2π·(cosh(r) − 1) Against Euclid's 2πr and πr². At r = 2.8 the hyperbolic circumference is 51.47 — nearly three times the Euclidean 17.59 — and the area is 45.57 against 24.63. A hyperbolic circle drawn in the disk is still a Euclidean circle as a picture, but its Euclidean centre is not its hyperbolic centre: the picture's centre is pulled toward the rim. Receipt 4 measured both the growth and the offset. A horocycle is the limiting case — a circle whose centre has fled to the horizon — drawn in the disk as a Euclidean circle tangent to the rim from inside.

Tessellations: when (p − 2)(q − 2) > 4

A regular tessellation {p, q} tiles the plane with regular p-gons, q meeting at each vertex, so each corner angle is exactly 360°/q. In Euclid's plane that forces (p − 2)(q − 2) = 4 — only {4, 4}, {3, 6}, {6, 3} exist. In the hyperbolic plane the corners can be thinner, the condition relaxes to (p − 2)(q − 2) > 4, and infinitely many tessellations exist. The exhibit ships six of them; Receipt 5 measured each one's corner angle, edge length, and tile area from the generated tiles themselves.

The audit — what was driven, and how

The audit did not audit by reading. It extracted the exhibit's own shipped script, ran it in Node behind DOM stubs (a fake canvas, fake elements, no animation loop), and called its own functions directly: hypDist, hypAngle, geodesicCircle, geodesicIdealEndpoints, geodesicLerp, computeParallelData, sampleHypCircle, buildTessellation, the projection maps diskToScreen / screenToDisk, and every museum preset through applyMuseumPreset. Against those, the audit computed everything a second time from the analytic formulas above — the arcosh form of the distance, angles recovered from side lengths by the hyperbolic law of cosines, circumferences by summing 1,440 geodesic chords, areas by fanning shapes into triangles and summing their defects. Where the two computations agree, neither is quoting the other.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The distance ceiling (again, because it is the one real bend in the engine): hypDist's clamp at 0.9999999 and geodesicLerp's at 0.999999 are sensible engineering — artanh near 1 is where floating point goes to die — but they are silent. Nothing in the exhibit tells you its ruler tops out at 16.811. Within the interface's own clamps the ceiling is unreachable, so this is a latent bend, not a displayed falsehood.

The numbers in the HTML are stale placeholders. Before the script's first frame overwrites them, the page's hard-coded telemetry reads: angle sum 104.2°, area 1.323, distance 1.842, tile count 420; parallel distance 0.942, Π 41.5°, fan 97.0°; circle offset 0.184, circumference 11.29. The shipped defaults actually measure: 108.7°, 1.245, 2.110, a 460-tile budget; 1.161, 34.8°, 110.4°; 0.132, 11.30. In a live browser these wrong numbers survive for a single frame at most — but they are what a reader of the raw page, or a browser with scripting off, would be told.

Museum nicknames are nicknames. "{3, 7} Klein Quartic" and "{8, 3} Bolza Octagon" name famous surfaces — the Klein quartic (genus 3) and the Bolza surface (genus 2) — that are built from these tessellations by gluing the sides of a single polygon with specific identifications. The exhibit draws the infinite tessellation, not the glued surface; no gluing is performed or shown. The associations are real and standard, but the labels name relatives, not the objects on screen.

The "Euclidean circumference" comparison is a comparison of formulas, not of two circles. The circles lab sets 2π·sinh(rH) beside 2πrH — the same number r fed to two geometries' formulas. That is the honest way to show the growth, and Receipt 4 verified the hyperbolic side by measuring the drawn curve; just don't read the Euclidean number as the circumference of anything drawn on the page.

The dual overlay is drawn in halves. "Dual Tiling Overlay {q, p}" strokes, for each tile, the geodesic from its centre to each edge midpoint. The neighbouring tile strokes the other half of the same dual edge, so the full dual edge set does appear — but only where both neighbours are inside the finite tile budget and large enough on screen (the overlay requires span > 9 px), so at the patch's edge the dual simply stops.

Projection fine print: in the upper half-plane view, diskToScreen clamps Im(w) to ≥ 0, so a point outside the disk would be folded onto the axis instead of landing below it (the interface never supplies one). And screenToDisk only applies the exact Klein inverse once the Poincaré↔Klein morph is more than half complete — mid-morph, clicks are interpreted by a ruler that is still changing. Both are cosmetic, both are confined to transitions and to inputs the UI cannot produce, and both are named here because the rest of the projection code — Receipt 6 — is exact.

Curvature is a constant, not a measurement. The telemetry's "K = −1.000" is a label for the model the formulas implement, displayed, not computed. It happens to be true of every formula the audit checked — the defect-equals-area receipts are what K = −1 means — but the digit itself is asserted, and the exhibit is better understood with that straight.
