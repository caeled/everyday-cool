What the exhibit is

Six three-dimensional flows, drawn live in 3D with trails, an ensemble of nearby orbits, a twin-pair “butterfly effect” mode, a Benettin-style Lyapunov meter, a log-separation plot, and a Poincaré cross-section plane you can move through the attractor. Each system carries four named regime presets (fixed points, period windows, full chaos) and live parameter sliders. The six systems are Lorenz, Rössler, Aizawa, Thomas, Halvorsen, and Dadras.

dx/dt = σ(y − x)  ·  dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y  ·  dz/dt = xy − βz The Lorenz system, the exhibit's default. σ is the Prandtl number, ρ the Rayleigh number, β a geometric factor. At σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3 the flow never repeats and never leaves a two-winged shape — the original 1963 weather-model butterfly.

What I drove, and how

The audit did not read the code and nod. It extracted the shipped SYSTEMS table and the shipped rk4Step function out of the exhibit file itself (sha256 512b672f…) and ran them headlessly in Node, calling the page's own derivative and stepper functions directly. Against them it ran an independent RK4 implementation of the published systems at a fine step (the shipped step divided by 20, and down to dt = 0.0005 for the Lyapunov reference). Pointwise trajectory comparisons are kept to short windows (t = 2 and t = 5), before chaos makes two honest trajectories disagree simply by being chaotic; long-run comparisons use attractor statistics instead — bounding boxes, mean height, and the Lorenz z-maximum return map.

Receipt 2: the integrator is honest RK4, and its step sizes hold up

The badge says “RK4” and the code is RK4: four derivative evaluations per step, weighted 1-2-2-1 over 6, in a shared scratch-vector stepper. Each system carries its own step size, chosen for its speed. The audit ran the shipped stepper against a fine-step RK4 reference (shipped dt ÷ 20) from the shipped starting point:

Thomas's dt = 0.055 looks coarse on paper and is the audit's best surprise: its flow is slow (speeds near 1), so the large step is accurate. Speed multipliers in the transport bar subdivide the step rather than enlarging it, so going faster does not silently degrade the integration.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The Lyapunov meter is a weighted running estimate, not a pure average: the page's Benettin implementation renormalises every 6 steps from a shadow 10−6 away — the correct method — but after 45 time units it repeatedly multiplies its accumulated sum and elapsed time by 0.96, an exponential forgetting factor so the readout responds when you sweep a parameter. That is good interface design and honest engineering, but the number on screen is “recent λ”, not “λ since launch”. In the audit's replication the page's scheme read +0.845 where a fine-step, no-forgetting Benettin read +0.905.

First paint asserts before it measures: the static HTML opens with λ = +0.90, horizon 1.1, and “Chaotic · λ > 0” already printed. The live meter replaces them within frames, and the asserted values happen to be the correct canonical ones — but the first thing the page shows is the textbook, not a measurement.

The one overstated label: Halvorsen “Periodic Knot, a = 2.08 (limit)” — measured λ ≈ +0.05 and a still-wide late orbit (see the box at the top). Its neighbours a = 1.62 and 1.89 are likewise “calmer and banded” rather than cleanly periodic in the audit window. The direction of every one of those labels is right; the arrival is overstated.

Thomas is the quiet one: at the shipped b = 0.19 and shipped starting point, twin separation barely grows over a full viewing session (Receipt 4). The labyrinth is real and bounded, but if you spawn the twin pair on Thomas expecting the Lorenz tear-apart, you will wait a long time. That is a property of the system at these parameters, faithfully integrated — the shipped and fine steppers agree on the trajectory to 10−8.

Colour ranges are not data ranges: a few systems' colour-normalisation ranges sit inside their measured extents (Thomas colours saturate beyond |z| = 3.4 though the orbit reaches ±4.09; Aizawa's z colour range tops out at 1.45 though the orbit reaches 1.85). The trails are drawn from the true coordinates — only the colour grading clips.

What is not claimed: no fractal dimension, no period readout, no “butterfly effect” number beyond the twin separation itself — which the page plots live as log10|Δx| and fits with a least-squares slope converted by ln 10, the correct conversion. The absence of invented numbers is itself a receipt.

The mathematics, briefly

A strange attractor is a shape a flow is pulled toward forever without ever repeating: bounded, deterministic, and sensitive — two starts a millionth of a unit apart end up on opposite wings, which is why the Lorenz model's descendant, the weather forecast, has a horizon at all. That horizon is 1/λ: with λ ≈ 0.9, a millionth-sized ignorance becomes order-one in about fifteen time units, exactly as the twin measurement above shows. Below ρ = 1 the Lorenz flow has one home, the origin; above it, two spiral homes; above ρ ≈ 24.74 those homes lose their grip and the butterfly is what remains. A Poincaré section — recording only the moments the orbit pierces one plane — compresses the flow into a map, and the z-maximum version of that map is how the audit could watch ρ = 99.96 collapse onto three repeating values while ρ = 28 scattered across three hundred.
