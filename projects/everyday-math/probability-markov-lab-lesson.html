Markov Chains — the lesson

Verdict: the chain mathematics is honest — and one button quietly runs a different chain than the one on the chart. The exhibit's stationary solver matches an independent solution of πP = π to ten decimal places on every ergodic preset it ships; its Gambler's Ruin walkers reproduce the textbook ruin probabilities and expected game lengths; and its long-run weather frequencies pass a chi-square check. Two things need naming up front: the “+500 Transitions” burst on Gambler's Ruin recycles absorbed walkers back to $2, so the empirical bars it draws can never reach the equilibrium the same panel plots; and the Ehrenfest urn is a period-2 chain whose current distribution never settles at all — only its running average does, which is the only thing the page ever displays. Both are named in the red box below, with numbers.

Overstated — “the” stationary distribution of Gambler's Ruin does not exist: that chain has two absorbing states, its eigenvalue 1 has multiplicity 2, and every distribution [a, 0, 0, 0, 1−a] satisfies π = πP. What the solver returns, [0.5, 0, 0, 0, 0.5], is the absorption split for a walker starting at $2 — which is where the exhibit starts its walkers, so the number is right for the game as set up. The bottom panel is honest about this (it retitles itself “Empirical Visits vs Absorbing Equilibrium”), but the telemetry card still labels the same number “Stationary π*”. Read it as “where a $2 start ends up”, not as a unique equilibrium of the chain.

What the exhibit is

Stochastic is a four-lab probability bench — a Galton board, a Monte Carlo π estimator, this Markov chain machine, and a Bayesian updater. Lab 3 gives you a graph of states, arrows weighted by transition probabilities, and a crowd of walkers (45 by default) hopping from state to state. Underneath, a panel plots how often the walkers have visited each state against a white marker: the stationary distribution π, the distribution that reproduces itself when the chain takes one more step. Five chains ship with it:

  • Weather Forecast — Sunny / Cloudy / Rainy, a 3-state ergodic chain.
  • PageRank Web — a 4-node “random surfer” chain: Hub, Wiki, Blog, Archive.
  • Gambler's Ruin — a fair coin-flip walk on $0–$4, absorbed at both ends.
  • Ehrenfest Urn — 3 balls shared between two urns; the state is how many sit in one of them.
  • Custom 3-State Lab — an editable chain whose outgoing weights you can drag, with the row re-normalised as you go.

The mathematics

A chain that forgets everything except where it is

A Markov chain is a random walk with no memory: the next state depends only on the current state, through a fixed table of probabilities P(i → j). Stack those probabilities into a matrix P. Every row must sum to exactly 1 — from wherever you are, you must go somewhere with certainty. If pn is the distribution over states after n steps, one step of the chain is one multiplication:

pn+1 = pn P Start anywhere, multiply often enough, and — for a well-behaved chain — the distribution stops changing. What it stops at is the stationary distribution.

The stationary distribution: π = πP

π = π P,    Σ πi = 1 π is the left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1. It answers: “in the long run, what fraction of the time does the chain spend in each state?” It exists and is unique when the chain is irreducible (every state reachable from every other) — and the step-by-step distribution actually converges to it only when the chain is also aperiodic. Both conditions matter, and this exhibit ships a chain that breaks each one, which is what makes it a good teacher: Gambler's Ruin is not irreducible (the absorbing ends trap you), and Ehrenfest is periodic.

How fast the distribution settles is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue magnitude, |λ2|: the distance to π shrinks roughly like |λ2|n. A |λ2| near 0 means instant mixing; near 1 means a long memory; exactly 1 means some part of the distribution never settles at all.

Gambler's Ruin: absorption, not equilibrium

A gambler starts with $i, wins or loses $1 on each fair coin flip, and stops at $0 (ruin) or $N (the goal). The standard results are exact: the probability of ruin from $i is 1 − i/N, and the expected number of flips before the game ends is i(N − i). For this exhibit's $4 goal: from $2, ruin and victory are 50/50 and the game lasts 4 flips on average; from $1, ruin is 75% and the game lasts 3. These come from the fundamental matrix (I − Q)−1 of the transient part of P, and the audit checked the walkers against them directly.

Ehrenfest: the periodic trap

In the Ehrenfest urn, one ball at a time jumps to the other urn. Every single jump changes the count by exactly one — so the parity of the state flips on every step. From an even state you are certainly at an odd state one step later, forever. The chain has period 2 and an eigenvalue of exactly −1. Its stationary distribution still exists and is the familiar binomial split [1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8] — but it is a time average, not a destination: the distribution at step n oscillates and never approaches π, while the average of the distributions up to step n does. Any display that plots cumulative visit frequencies — as this one does — will show serene convergence for a chain that, step by step, never converges. That is not a bug in the solver; it is a fact about what the chart is a chart of, and it is worth knowing before the chart persuades you.

The audit — what was driven, and how

The audit did not read the mathematics off the page. It extracted the exhibit's own shipped script, ran it headlessly in Node behind DOM stubs, and drove its own objects — MARKOV_PRESETS, Markov.loadPreset(), Markov.solveStationary(), Markov.chooseNext(), Markov.burstTransitions() and Markov.setTransitionWeight() — for every preset. The stationary vectors it produced were then checked against an independent solution of πP = π (π obtained by solving the linear system (PT − I)π = 0 with Σπ = 1, computed separately), against eigenvalues computed independently, and against the closed-form ruin formulas via the fundamental matrix. Simulation claims were checked by running the exhibit's own chooseNext() — the exact function its walkers use — for stated numbers of steps.

How the solver does it: not by raw power iteration, but by a damped one — each step replaces v with ½v + ½(vP), for up to 600 steps or until the change falls below 10−10. Damping maps every eigenvalue λ to (1 + λ)/2. That is a deliberate and legitimate device, and Receipt 5 shows exactly what it buys — and what it hides.

One honest detail in the live (non-burst) animation deserves credit: a walker absorbed at $0 or $4 does not vanish or freeze the counting — it keeps tallying a visit to its absorbing state on roughly each hop cadence. That is exactly what the chain itself does, since Pii = 1 is a genuine self-loop step. It is why the live empirical bars for Gambler's Ruin do drift toward [50%, 0, 0, 0, 50%], while the burst bars, per the red box, cannot.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The burst recycling (the red box, restated as the headline bend): only Gambler's Ruin is affected, only in burst mode, and the 15% respawn is a deliberate product choice disclosed in a code comment but nowhere in the interface. Everything else the burst button does — Weather, PageRank, Ehrenfest, Custom — checked out in Receipt 4.

“PageRank” is a name, not the algorithm: Google's PageRank is the stationary vector of a link matrix modified by teleportation — the famous damping factor (usually 0.85) that lets the surfer jump anywhere and guarantees a unique answer even with dangling pages. This preset ships a hand-written 4×4 row-stochastic matrix with no teleportation term and no graph it was derived from. Its π is computed correctly (Receipt 2), and as a picture of “the surfer ends up where the links lead” it teaches the right idea — but if you went looking for the 0.85, it is not in the building.

Ehrenfest's thirds are rounded: the matrix carries 0.333 and 0.667 where the true urn has ⅓ and ⅔. Row sums stay exactly 1, and π moves by less than a ten-thousandth (Receipt 2). The exact chain's π is the clean binomial [1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8]; the exhibit's is that, to three decimal places.

Visits are counted from before the first hop: resetting the walkers tallies each walker's starting state as a visit, so the empirical bars begin life as a picture of the starting arrangement (round-robin across states, or all-at-$2 for absorbing chains), not of the chain. With hundreds of hops this washes out; in the first seconds it is most of what the bars show. Likewise the TV sparkline and the “Stationary” verdict are statements about that cumulative tally throughout.

The solver's damping is a solver's device, not the chain's behaviour: ½v + ½(vP) converges for chains — Ehrenfest — whose actual distributions never do (Receipt 5). Anyone reusing solveStationary() as a mixing simulator would import that confusion; as a π-finder it is exactly right, on every preset, to ten decimal places.
