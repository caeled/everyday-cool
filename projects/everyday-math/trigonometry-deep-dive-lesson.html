Everyday Math · The lesson behind the Trigonometry Deep Dive

One circle, and everything on the sheet is a way of looking at it

Open any trigonometry cheat sheet — and the internet is a wall of them, handwritten, photographed, carousel'd, shared by the thousand — and you are looking at one object from many sides: a circle of radius 1, with a point riding it. The point's coordinates are cos θ and sin θ. Everything else on the sheet is a consequence: the identities are the circle's symmetries written as equations, the laws of sines and cosines are what the circle says about triangles, and the graphs are the point's height, floor-length and wall-length unrolled in time. This lesson walks that arc, then reports what the bench measured — including the two claims on its list that are false, and the exact angles that convict them.

First door: the identities are symmetries

Start with the one identity that is just the circle's own equation. The point sits on x² + y² = 1, and its coordinates are (cos θ, sin θ), so:

sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 — Pythagoras, wearing the circle's clothes

Divide that line by cos²θ and 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ falls out; divide by sin²θ and 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ falls out. Three "different" Pythagorean identities, one circle, read three ways. The angle-sum family is a rotation seen from two frames: rotating by a and then by b must equal rotating by a + b, and writing that single fact in coordinates produces sin(a + b) and cos(a + b) at once. Double-angle is the same fact with b = a; half-angle is the Pythagorean identity solved for the half-turn. None of this is a list to memorise — it is one picture, unfolded. The bench's second panel is that picture: drag the point, and the three functions are three lengths — a height, a floor-length, and a length up the tangent wall — which is all sine, cosine and tangent ever were.

The graphs make the same point in the other direction. Sweep the angle and the height draws the sine wave, the floor-length draws cosine, and the wall-length draws tangent — including its famous misbehaviour at 90°, where the radius stands parallel to the wall and the length simply leaves. The wave is not a picture of the circle; it is the circle, unrolled.

Second door: Check the Sheet — the crowd's habit, mechanised

Steven's commission for this workshop had a social layer: find the trig the public actually discusses. The search (Instagram, Facebook, Threads, 24 November 2026, engagement-ranked, 60 posts pulled) came back as a wall of formula sheets — the five most-discussed are listed in the next section. And under the sheets, the comment threads cluster on two things: appreciation, and correction. A sheet with one wrong identity gets caught, publicly, by strangers doing spot-checks. That is a genuinely good epistemic habit, and Panel 3 of the bench is that habit as a tool: sixteen claimed identities, two of them planted errors of exactly the kinds the threads catch, none of them labelled. Test at your own angles; blast with 200 random ones; the false claims break in the open, with the convicting angles printed.

Here is what the bench's own verifier measured, run headlessly against the shipped code (seed 20261124, 200 random angles per claim; the full table is at the bottom). The true identities' worst disagreements are float rounding — the largest anywhere in the canon is the tangent difference formula at 6.54×10⁻¹³, and most sit at or below 10⁻¹⁵. The planted claims are not close calls:

PLANTED: sin(a + b) = sin a + sin b — worst error 2.58. Counterexamples: a=66.2°, b=347.2° → 0.8029 vs 0.6936 · a=228.2°, b=329.0° → −0.2953 vs −1.2596 · a=299.5°, b=266.0° → −0.4299 vs −1.8681. By hand: a = b = 30° gives sin 60° = 0.8660 against 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.
PLANTED: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 ÷ cos θ — worst error 18.8. Counterexamples: θ=66.2° → 1.0000 vs 2.4769 · θ=347.2° → 1.0000 vs 1.0254 · θ=228.2° → 1.0000 vs −1.4989. By hand: θ = 60° gives 1 against 1/cos 60° = 2, an error of exactly 1.

The first fake is the most famous error in the subject — the wish that sine would distribute over a sum like multiplication does. It survives in students' notebooks because it looks like algebra; it dies at the first angle anyone tests, which is the whole lesson of the bench: an identity is a claim about every angle, so any angle is allowed to vote. The second fake is a misprint's shape — one reciprocal wandered in from a neighbouring line of the sheet — and it fails even harder, because its right side isn't even confined to [−1, 1]'s neighbourhood: at 228.2° it claims the sum of two squares is negative.

Third door: solving — and the case that won't sit still

The solver's receipts are in the table, but the SSA case deserves its paragraph, because it is where school trig quietly lies by omission. Give two sides and a non-included angle — A = 30°, a = 5, b = 8 — and the geometry genuinely contains two triangles: the side opposite the unknown angle can swing and land in two places, B = 53.13° or B = 126.87°, both closing perfectly. Shorten a to 4 and sin B lands exactly on 1: the swing ends at a right angle, one triangle, a knife-edge. Shorten a to 3 and sin B would have to be 4/3 — no triangle at all, and the bench says so in words instead of returning a NaN-flavoured shrug. Most textbook exercises are rigged to the one-answer case; the bench refuses to rig. The equation solver's two-answer habit has the same root: sin x = 0.5 cuts the circle at 30° and 150° — two crossings, two answers — and at k = 1.5 the line misses the circle entirely, which the bench also says in words.

The tracer: sin 75°, the long way, arriving on time

The tracer's showpiece is the computation the formula sheets exist to enable: sin 75°, an angle with no bold exact value on the standard table, built from two that have them. Step by step: sin 45° = 0.7071067812, cos 30° = 0.8660254038, their product 0.6123724357; cos 45° = 0.7071067812, sin 30° = 0.5, their product 0.3535533906; the sum, 0.965925826289068 — and the direct sin 75°, computed independently, is 0.965925826289068. The identity route and the direct route agree to under 10⁻¹² (the measured difference prints as 0.00e+0 at the bench's precision for the default inputs). Cos 75° walks the same road with its minus sign and lands on 0.258819045102521 both ways. This is what an identity is for: not a line to memorise, but a machine that turns angles you know into angles you don't.

The honest notes. Five, named. First — evidence is not proof. The verifier's 200 random angles are overwhelming evidence: a false identity would have to conspire to agree at 200 unrelated angles to survive, and the planted ones are caught within the first few draws. But a proof is a different instrument — a finite chain of deductions from definitions that covers every angle at once, including the uncountably many no sampler will ever visit. The derivations live in the references below (the angle-sum formulas fall out of one rotation-matrix multiplication; the Pythagorean family out of the circle's equation, as the first section showed). The bench checks; the proofs are where checking becomes knowing, and the page has never claimed otherwise. Second — pole-skips. Near an angle where tan or sec explodes, both sides of a true identity are enormous and their computed difference measures the arithmetic's strain, not the claim's truth; the verifier skips angles within a guard band of the poles and prints the skip count (up to 17 of 200 for the cotangent identity). The tangent identities' worst errors — 2.88×10⁻¹³ and 6.54×10⁻¹³, the canon's largest — are exactly that strain, visible and bounded, two orders inside the tolerance. Third — the knife-edge. The SSA right-angle case is decided by comparing sin B against 1 within 10⁻¹²; a visitor who types sides that put sin B within that hair of 1 is standing on a genuine mathematical cliff-edge, and the count may flip either way — the bench labels the case rather than hiding the cliff. Fourth — the drawn tangent. On the circle bench the violet segment is clipped at the canvas edge when |tan θ| is large, with an arrow marking that it runs off; the readout always prints the true value. The graphs similarly clip at ±3.2 so the sine and cosine stay legible — tangent's excursions off the top are the honest shape of the function, drawn as departure rather than faked as data.
