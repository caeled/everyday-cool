EigenSpace — the lesson

Verdict: the linear algebra is exact. EigenSpace is a 2×2 transformation playground — drag the basis vectors, watch the grid warp, and read off eigenvalues, eigenvectors, singular values, and a full SVD, all computed in closed form. The audit drove the shipped code headlessly, called its own computeEigen and computeSVD functions directly on every preset it ships plus an eighteen-matrix battery of its own, and checked every returned pair against the one test that cannot be argued with: the residual ‖Av − λv‖. Every real eigenpair the page returns, on every preset and nearly every battery matrix, satisfies Av = λv to machine precision — residuals of 0 to 2.5 × 10−16. The trace and determinant identities hold exactly, the SVD rebuilds the original matrix to within rounding, and the awkward cases — rotation's complex pair, the shear's missing second eigenvector, the singular projection's zero eigenvalue — are each handled the way the mathematics says they should be. Two bends are named below, and one label on the page is simply false; none of them touches a number the page actually computes.

Matrices whose eigenvalues differ by less than about 0.001 near λ ≈ 1 fall into this crack. Details in Receipt 5.

The mathematics

Directions that don't turn

A 2×2 matrix is a machine that moves every point in the plane: the basis vector î goes to the first column, ĵ to the second, and everything else follows by linearity. Almost every direction gets turned by that motion. The special directions that don't — that come out pointing exactly where they pointed in, only stretched, shrunk, or flipped — are the eigenvectors, and the factor is the eigenvalue:

Av = λv Read it as a promise the audit can test: take the page's own v, multiply by the page's own A, and compare against λ times v. The size of the difference — the residual ‖Av − λv‖ — is the receipt. A true eigenpair has residual zero; a computer's arithmetic gets it to about 10−16.

For a 2×2 matrix the eigenvalues come from one quadratic — the characteristic polynomial — built from just two summary numbers, the trace (the diagonal sum) and the determinant (the signed area factor):

λ = ( tr ± √( tr² − 4·det ) ) / 2,    λ₁ + λ₂ = tr,    λ₁ · λ₂ = det The sign of the discriminant tr² − 4·det decides the whole taxonomy. Positive: two real eigenlines. Zero: one repeated eigenvalue — either every direction is an eigenvector (a pure scaling) or only one line survives (a shear, the defective case). Negative: no real eigenline at all — the eigenvalues are a complex conjugate pair, and the transformation turns everything, the way a rotation does. EigenSpace's solver is exactly this formula, with a case split for each branch, and the audit tested every branch.

Eigenvectors stretch; singular vectors turn, stretch, turn

Eigenvectors answer “which directions survive this matrix unchanged in direction?” The SVD answers a question that always has an answer, for every matrix, even singular and defective ones: “which perpendicular pair of directions does the matrix send to another perpendicular pair, and by how much?” Those stretch factors are the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂, and the decomposition A = UΣVT says every linear map is a rotation, then axis stretches, then another rotation — with one honest wrinkle the exhibit's own animation names: when det < 0, one of the “stretches” is negative, a stretch with a flip, because no combination of pure rotations and positive stretches can turn the plane over. The audit rebuilt A from the page's own SVD angles and signed σ₂ for all eight presets; the reconstruction error never exceeded 2.2 × 10−16 per entry.

Power iteration: lean on the matrix until the loud direction wins

Apply A to a vector, then again, then again. Each application multiplies the eigenvector components by their eigenvalues, so the component along the largest-|λ| eigenvector outgrows the rest by a factor of |λ₂/λ₁| per step — the repeated orbit spirals onto the dominant eigenline. That is the exhibit's “Power Iteration Orbit.” It is a picture of the process, not a solver: the page extracts no eigenvalue estimate from the orbit and promises no step count — and the audit's job was to check what the picture actually does in the three regimes where power iteration is fast, slow, and impossible. Receipt 4 has the measurements.

The audit — the shipped code, driven headlessly

The audit extracted the exhibit's own JavaScript and ran it in Node, calling computeEigen, computeSVD, matVec, matDet, and matTrace directly — no reimplementation of the page's math. Independent checks were the characteristic polynomial recomputed from scratch, the trace and determinant identities, and the residual ‖Av − λv‖ for every returned pair. The orbit tests replicated the page's own orbit loop step for step, including its stopping rules (halt if a step's length exceeds 60 or falls below 10−4).

Eigenvalue ordering is by dominant magnitude, |λ₁| ≥ |λ₂| — so for [[−3, 0], [0, 1]] the page's λ₁ is −3, not 1. Receipt 3: the SVD rebuilds the matrix, and its guide line is exact

The View & Guide tab states det(A) = λ₁·λ₂ = ±σ₁·σ₂. The audit verified the stronger, signed version on all eight presets: det = σ₁ · σ₂signed exactly, where σ₂signed carries the determinant's sign (Fibonacci: 1.61803 × −0.618034 = −1; Reflection: 1 × −1 = −1; Projection: 1 × 0 = 0). Rebuilding A as U·Σ·VT from the page's own returned angles and singular values reproduced every preset matrix with per-entry error at most 2.2 × 10−16. Rank and condition number behaved: the singular projection reports rank 1 and κ = ∞; the rotations report κ = 1. One wrinkle in the machinery, stated plainly: rank is decided by thresholds (σ₂ below 10−5, or σ₂/σ₁ below 10−5), so a matrix that is invertible but within a hair of singular will be badged “Singular · Rank 1.” That is a defensible numerical-rank convention — every serious library does something like it — but it is a convention, and near the line the badge is the threshold's opinion, not a theorem.

Receipt 5: scaling, signs, and the one cracked case

Scaling: every eigenvector the solver returns is a unit vector — the audit measured |v| = 1.000 on every returned pair. Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, so a convention must be chosen; unit length is the standard one, and the page applies it uniformly (its null-space helper divides by the row length).

Signs: an eigenvector and its negative are the same eigenline, so the sign is pure convention — and this page's convention has two layers, which the audit separated by calling the solver fresh versus repeatedly. On a fresh call, the sign is canonical: first component positive, or if that component is ~0, second component positive (so [[−3, 0], [0, 1]] fresh returns v₁ = [1, 0], and the squeeze returns v₂ = [0, 1]). But the page keeps the previous frame's directions and flips a new vector if it points against its predecessor — deliberately, so arrows don't flicker while you drag. Measured consequence: call the solver on the SVD preset and then on the squeeze, and the squeeze's v₂ comes back [0, −1] instead of the canonical [0, 1]. Same line, same λ, opposite arrow — history-dependent by design. During a drag that is a feature; anyone comparing the readout against a textbook should know the arrow's head is chosen for continuity, not by a fixed rule.

The cracked case: the case split uses absolute tolerances — discriminant greater than 10−6 means “distinct,” within ±10−6 means “repeated.” For [[1, 0], [0, 1.0004]] the true discriminant is 1.6 × 10−7, inside the band, so the page takes the defective branch: it averages the eigenvalues into λ = 1.0002, finds one vector, and reports no second eigenline — for a matrix that has two. The returned vector's residual against the averaged λ is 2 × 10−4, four orders of magnitude worse than anything else in this audit, and the displayed λ rounds to “1.00 (1D)” — a reader would never know two lines were merged. Because the tolerance is absolute, the crack's width scales with the matrix: eigenvalues differing by less than roughly √10−6 · (scale) near the diagonal get merged. It is the standard floating-point dilemma — a truly repeated eigenvalue computed from rounded entries rarely has discriminant exactly 0 — but the page states no tolerance anywhere, so the merge is silent.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The normalize label (the one false item): “Normalize Orbit Steps (xₙ / ‖xₙ‖)” — the code computes xₙ / ‖xₙ‖ × ‖x₀‖. Points sit on the circle of radius ‖x₀‖ (1.393 on the Fibonacci preset), not the unit circle the formula names. The direction sequence — the thing the feature exists to show — is identical either way.

The near-degenerate merge: distinct eigenvalues closer than the 10−6 discriminant band are reported as one defective eigenvalue (their average), with the second eigenline denied. Measured on [[1, 0], [0, 1.0004]]: reported λ = 1.0002 (1D), residual 2 × 10−4. Everywhere outside that band, residuals were ≤ 2.8 × 10−14.

Spiral preset's |λ|: the preset button says |λ| = 0.85; the measured modulus is 0.851469. The telemetry readout itself shows the honest figure (|λ| = 0.851); only the button's shorthand rounds it down. A rounding, not an error — but receipts are receipts.

“Rotation, stretch, rotation” has a flip inside it: the guide's SVD sentence says every matrix is a rotation VT, perpendicular stretches Σ, and a final rotation U. For det < 0 that is only true with a signed stretch — the exhibit's own SVD animation admits it, relabelling its middle chip “Σ Stretch+Flip.” The audit's signed reconstruction (det = σ₁·σ₂signed) confirms the flip lives in Σ, so both U and VT stay honest rotations. The guide sentence is the simplified version; the animation tells the fuller truth.

Signs are continuity, not canon: eigenvector arrows are flipped to agree with the previous frame, so the same matrix can show v or −v depending on what you dragged through to get there. Fresh-load signs follow a fixed canonical rule. The eigenline — the mathematics — never changes.

Rank is a threshold's opinion near the line: rank drops to 1 when σ₂ < 10−5 or σ₂/σ₁ < 10−5. A barely-invertible matrix will wear the “Singular” badge. The condition number beside it (κ = ∞ only when σ₂ is under 10−6) tells the more graded story.

Small print: the page's static HTML ships placeholder readouts (σ = 1.98, 0.67; κ = 2.95; SVD angles −32.4° / +23.8°) that do not match the default matrix's true values (1.95, 0.68; 2.85; −40.2° / +31.4°). They are overwritten by the real telemetry on the first animation frame, so no visitor ever reads them — they are scaffolding, not claims. And the orbit's stopping rules mean a fast-growing orbit ends early (length > 60) and a fast-shrinking one ends at 10−4: the picture stops, correctly, where the canvas stops being able to say anything.
