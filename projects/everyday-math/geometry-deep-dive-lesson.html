Everyday Math · The lesson behind the Geometry Deep Dive

Formulas are compressed derivations — this lesson decompresses three of them

Every formula on a cheatsheet is a derivation that got folded flat. A = πr² is a circle being sliced and rearranged; a² + b² = c² is three squares balancing on one triangle; (n − 2) · 180° is a fan of triangles hiding inside a polygon. The Geometry Deep Dive exists because the social feeds are having exactly this argument at scale — and the two loudest posts in it are not cheatsheets. They are derivations.

First door: the argument in the feed

On 24 November 2026 we pulled the sixty most-discussed geometry posts across Instagram, Facebook and Threads and ranked them by comments. The top of the list splits cleanly into the feed's two cultures. The derivation crowd holds the crown: Zack D. Films' animation of the circle-area derivation drew 2,600 comments and 630K likes, and his second derivation — circle area via the triangle analogy — took third place with 400 comments. The cheatsheet savers hold the volume: a sixteen-item mensuration carousel at 1,900 comments, a fifteen-formula grid at 300, a fourteen-formula grid at 260, and dozens more below them. And in the same feed, Cuemath argues to parents that children don't need to memorize geometry formulas at all (98 comments).

The workshop's verdict, and this lesson's arc: the argument is a false fork. You derive a formula to own it — and then you keep the sheet, because nobody re-derives πr² in the middle of an exam. So the page opens with the canon, complete and unapologetic, and then spends its centerpiece deriving the canon's most famous entry with the error term left in.

Second door: πr², watched happening

Cut a circle into n equal sectors and straighten each sector into its triangle — two radii and a chord. Lay the triangles apex-alternating in a row and they form a near- parallelogram. Its dimensions are computable exactly: the base is n half-chords, n · r · sin(π/n), and the height is the apothem, r · cos(π/n). As n grows the base tends to πr and the height to r — which is the whole derivation, and also its honest limit: at every finite n the straightened figure is a little short. The bench prints the shortfall instead of hiding it. Measured on the bench itself, for r = 5 (true area πr² = 78.5398):

The shortfall shrinks at every step — the page's proof table verifies monotonic decrease across the whole slider range, n = 4…256 — and roughly quarters with each doubling of n, the signature of the slivers being second-order small. The second derivation is the same sum wearing the triangle costume of the third-ranked post: unroll the circumference into a base of length 2πr, stand the radius up as the height, and the area is ½ · 2πr · r = πr² — exact in the limit, short by the identical slivers when the base is chopped into chords. The third view refuses to derive anything: it counts grid squares and labels itself an estimate. At 32 cells across (r = 5) it counts 732 full squares and 124 touched ones, bracketing the truth — 71.4844 ≤ πr² ≤ 83.5938 — with a half-credit estimate of 77.5391 against the true 78.5398. A bracket is not a derivation, and the page says so on its face.

Third door: Pythagoras with the squares left on

The theorem is usually recited; the lab draws it. The squares on the three sides are constructed from the same coordinates as the triangle and fitted to the canvas by their bounding box, so no slider setting can push one off the edge — drag the legs to 25 and the whole figure rescales instead of breaking. The balance a² + b² − c² sits at float zero (≈10⁻¹⁶) at every setting. The converse tester closes the loop the cheatsheets leave open: 3-4-5 is right, 4-5-6 is acute (16 + 25 = 41 > 36), 2-3-4 is obtuse (4 + 9 = 13 < 16), and 1-1-3 is not a triangle at all — four verdicts, each delivered by the numbers, each re-verified in the proof table.

Fourth door: formulas that move

The rearrange lab treats four canon formulas — circle area, cylinder volume, Pythagoras, trapezoid area — as equations to be operated on, not strings to be memorized. Every variable of every formula can be isolated: divide both sides by π, subtract b², take the root, each move printed as it happens. The check is substitution: solve, plug back, and compare against the quantity you started with. Across all 24 combinations (four formulas × every solvable variable × two sample sets) the worst relative error measured was 1.45 × 10⁻¹⁶ — the rearrangements lose nothing, and now that is a measurement, not an assertion. Trace mode runs the same numbers in the forward direction, one operation at a time, which is the direction homework actually arrives in.

What was measured

The bench page's own runProofs() — ten claims, all passing when this lesson was written: the 24-combination rearrangement round-trip (≤ 1.45e-16 relative); the slice shortfall table above and its monotonic decrease; the base/height limits at n = 100,000 (base 15.707963 against πr = 15.707963, height 5.000000 against r = 5); the grid bracket; the four integer triples balancing exactly; the four converse verdicts; polygon interior sums for n = 3…12 (180° through 1800°) with the exterior sum pinned at 360°; transversal consistency — supplementary pairs summing to exactly 180°, vertical angles equal, the second line's angles identical to the first's — sampled at 21 angles from 20° to 160°; and two canon spot-checks by independent decomposition (trapezoid area by formula vs rectangle-plus-triangle; annulus vs the difference of two discs). The page was also driven headlessly through every control — all three derivation views, both Pythagoras sliders and all four triple buttons, every rearrange formula and target including the trapezoid's second base, trace mode, the polygon sweep, the transversal at 120°, and the pair namer — with zero script errors.

Honest notes

  • The rearranged-parallelogram picture is a limit argument, not a proof by picture. The wedges tile the circle exactly at every n — what converges is the parallelogram they resemble, and the page shows convergence numbers rather than asking the drawing to carry the claim. A rigorous proof needs the limit stated, which the numbers here support but do not replace.
  • The grid counter is an estimator and is labelled one. Its bracket is a theorem (full squares lie inside; touched squares cover), but the half-credit estimate inside the bracket is a heuristic — finer grids narrow the bracket, none closes it.
  • Two build faults were caught by driving, not by reading. The trapezoid formula at first shipped with no way to solve for its second base — the target menu listed a variable the solver didn't know, and selecting it threw. And the Pythagoras triple buttons at first demanded legs beyond the sliders' range, so 5-12-13 silently clamped to 5-10. Both are fixed and both fixes are covered by the drive described above; they are recorded here because a page about checking your work should show its own corrections.
  • Engagement counts are as found on 24 November 2026 and will have moved since; they rank the discussion, they do not grade the mathematics. The Cuemath reel is included below the top five deliberately — it is the argument's other pole, not a sixth-place finisher.
  • The drawn transversal is schematic. Its eight numbers are computed exactly from the one given angle; the drawing leans correctly (the up-ray makes the stated angle with the line) but it is an illustration of the table, not a protractor.
