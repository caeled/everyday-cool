Nabla — the lesson

Verdict: the calculus is real and unusually accurate — the probe's loop integrals satisfy Green's theorem and the divergence theorem to four decimal places on every preset, the Taylor coefficients are exact, and the surface gradients check out against numerical differentiation. One preset claim is flatly false (below), and several printed formulas disagree with the code they label. Nabla is three exhibits in one page: a 2D vector-field laboratory with a draggable paddlewheel-and-flux probe that reports divergence, curl, circulation and flux live; a Taylor-series lab that expands six functions about a draggable centre and shades the radius of convergence; and a 3D gradient-descent race in which SGD, Momentum, RMSProp and Adam run down four classic loss landscapes. This page is the other half: the mathematics, and the audit that drove the shipped code headlessly and checked its numbers against independent analytic results.

The shipped field, F = (3xy, 2y² − x²) · 3.2/(r² + 0.55)², is not divergence-free: the audit measured ∇·F = +2.449 at (0, 1), +1.732 at the default probe point (0.78, 0.42), and +0.184 at (−1.3, 0.7) — and its own divergence heatmap will show it, if you turn that overlay on. A real magnetic dipole field is divergence-free everywhere away from the dipole itself; this field is a pretty swirling polynomial field wearing a dipole's name. The loops look the part. The divergence does not. Every other preset's headline claim checked out; this one does not.

The mathematics

Divergence and curl: two questions you can ask a field

A vector field F(x, y) = (P, Q) assigns an arrow to every point. Two combinations of its partial derivatives answer the two physical questions:

∇·F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y   ·   (∇×F)z = ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y Divergence is net outflow per unit area — positive at a source, negative at a sink, zero where flow merely passes through. The z-component of curl is circulation per unit area — twice the angular velocity a tiny paddlewheel dropped in the flow would spin with. Nabla's probe is literally that paddlewheel, with a ring around it.

The two big theorems turn those local quantities into boundary measurements. Green's theorem: the circulation around a closed loop equals the integral of curl over the region inside it. The divergence theorem (in 2D, the flux form of Green): the outward flux across the loop equals the integral of divergence inside. Nabla computes both sides independently — local div/curl by finite differences at the probe centre, circulation and flux by walking 32 points around the probe's ring — which makes the exhibit self-checking, and made it auditable: if the theorems hold in its own numbers, the engine is right.

Taylor series: a function, rebuilt from its derivatives at one point

The Taylor lab builds PN(x) = Σ f(k)(a)/k! · (x − a)k for sin, cos, ex, ln(1 + x), 1/(1 − x) and atan. Inside the radius of convergence R — the distance from a to the nearest singularity, including complex ones, which is why atan(x), smooth on the whole real line, still has R = √(1 + a²) — the polynomial converges to the function. Outside, adding terms makes it worse, violently. The lab shades (a − R, a + R) and lets you watch both behaviours with one slider.

Gradient descent: walking downhill on −∇f

The descent mode races four optimizers on four surfaces. All four follow the negative gradient; they differ in memory. SGD steps raw; Momentum accumulates velocity; RMSProp rescales each coordinate by a running average of squared gradients; Adam does both, with bias correction. The surfaces are chosen to separate them: Rosenbrock's curved valley punishes raw steps, Rastrigin's eggbox traps everyone somewhere, and Himmelblau has four equal minima, so which minimum an optimizer finds is part of the result.

The audit — the shipped code, driven headlessly

Method: the exhibit's own JavaScript was extracted from the shipped file (sha256 a23de6eb…) and run in Node. Its evalVectorField, evalDivCurl, Taylor coefficient builders, evalPoly, surface f/grad pairs, dropOptimizersAt and stepOptimizersOnce were called directly — nothing was retyped or re-implemented — and every number was compared against independent analytic formulas differentiated separately (central differences at h = 10−6, plus closed forms where they exist). The probe's loop integrals were recomputed with the shipped 32-point rule and compared against the theorems' right-hand sides, integrated independently over the probe disk on a 200 × 360 polar grid.

First, a scope note: the exhibit ships no constant field, radial source F = (x, y), or pure rotation F = (−y, x) preset — the three textbook test fields. Receipt 2: the finite differences are honest, and small

evalDivCurl uses central differences with h = 0.0025 — no smoothing, no fudge. Worst disagreement with the independent h = 10−6 reference across all presets and points: 4.0 × 10−5 (vortex curl). At the vortex centre, shipped curl 8.68417 vs analytic 2·1.65/0.38 = 8.68421 — a relative error of 4.6 × 10−6. The readouts on screen are the field's real derivatives to the precision they're displayed at.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The magnetic preset is not divergence-free — the false box at the top. Its flux through the default probe loop is +2.946, not 0. Treat it as a swirly field with interesting curl, not as a model of a magnet.

Printed formulas that don't match the code: (1) The page's hard-coded opening formula reads F = (−y, x)/(0.35 + 0.28r²) — the code runs 1.65/(0.38 + 0.28r²); the correct string replaces it on the first frame, but the static HTML is stale. (2) The formula bar's shear line says +0.25y; the code uses +0.22y. (3) The magnetic formula omits the code's ×3.2 factor. (4) The saddle button says F = (x, −y); the code scales by 0.68 (the formula bar gets this one right). (5) The dipole formula omits the ×1.65 strength and the +0.26 softening that keeps it finite. None of these change a single computed number — the code is the accurate party in every case — but a student copying formulas off the screen will copy three of them wrong.

The static telemetry is placeholder fiction: the HTML as shipped displays curl +1.240, circulation +2.020 and speed 1.000 before the first frame. The shipped code at the default probe actually computes F = (−1.16, 2.15), curl +3.486, speed 2.437. The live numbers overwrite the placeholders immediately; the file's first paint does not tell the truth.

Loop integrals are disk averages, not the local value times area: the insight box says the paddlewheel “measures local ∇×F (circulation per unit area)”. At the default radius 0.72 the loop honestly averages the disk: vortex circ 5.638 vs local curl × area 5.678 (0.7% off), shear −2.441 vs −2.568 (5%), magnetic −5.573 vs −6.042 (8%), lattice −3.109 vs −3.447 (10%). Shrink the probe radius and the two agree — that shrinking is the definition — but at the radius the page opens with, they are cousins, not twins. The telemetry panel, to its credit, displays both numbers separately and never conflates them.

The paddlewheel spins fast: the wheel's angle advances at 1.45 × curl per second. A physical paddlewheel spins at ω = curl/2. “Proportional to local curl” — the code's own comment — is true; the proportion is about 2.9× physical. Visual licence, named.

Heatmaps are squashed, arrows are log-scaled, particles are capped: the div/curl heatmaps pass the value through tanh(0.42·div) / tanh(0.38·curl) and cap opacity — direction and sign are faithful, magnitude is impressionistic past |value| ≈ 3. Arrow length is 6 + 9.5·log(1 + |F|), capped at 20 px: direction exact, length compressed. Tracer particles integrate with genuine midpoint RK2, but their speed is capped at 5.5 units — in the presets that cap rarely binds (peak |F| ≈ 2.5); with strong custom nodes stacked, trails in the core will understate the true speed.

Descent small print: the Saddle Ridge surface's displayed formula omits the code's +2.8 vertical shift (gradients and minima locations unaffected; displayed f values sit 2.8 higher than the printed formula gives). Its two minima markers, (0, ±2.28 / −2.18), are approximate: the true northern minimum is at y ≈ 2.32 with f = 1.3568, and at the printed marker |∇f| is still 0.059 — the optimizers themselves roll past the marker to y ≈ 2.30–2.32. “Momentum” is the heavy-ball form v ← βv + ∇f (no (1 − β) factor), so its effective step is larger than SGD's at the same slider setting — standard practice, worth knowing when the race looks uneven. Gradient clipping at |∇f| = 80 keeps Rosenbrock's cliffs from exploding; it binds only on the steepest walls.

What is not here: no constant, radial-source, or pure-rotation preset — the canonical first examples must be built in the Custom sandbox (one source node approximates (x, y) away from its softened core). And there is no line integral along an open path — only the probe's closed loop. Closed is where the theorems live, so the choice is defensible; it is still a boundary of the exhibit.
