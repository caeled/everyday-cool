What you are looking at

Trigonometry Deep Dive starts from one claim: the unit circle is the whole subject. Every formula on every trig cheat sheet — and the internet is a wall of trig cheat sheets — is a symmetry of one circle of radius 1. This bench doesn't ask you to take the sheets' word for anything. It draws the circle, reads the functions off it as lengths, and then checks the sheets: every identity on the wall gets tested at hundreds of angles, in the open, with the failures printed.

Panel 1 — The formula canon

The major high-school formulas, as cards: the SOH-CAH-TOA definitions, the reciprocal and quotient identities, all three Pythagorean identities, the angle-sum and difference family, double- and half-angle formulas, the laws of sines and cosines, the area formula, degrees↔radians, arc length and sector area, and the exact values at the classic angles. Most cards carry a button that loads the formula into one of the benches below, so a card is a door, not a poster.

Panel 2 — The unit circle bench

Drag the point around the circle (or use the slider). The three functions are drawn as what they are: cos θ is a length along the floor (amber), sin θ is a height (cyan), tan θ is a length up the tangent wall at x = 1 (violet). Turn on snap and the point clicks to the classic angles, where the readouts show the exact values — √2/2 and friends — next to the decimals. Below, the sweep runs the angle through two full turns and the circle itself draws the three graphs: the height of the point becomes the sine curve, the floor length becomes cosine, the wall length becomes tangent, kinks, jumps and all.

Panel 3 — Check the Sheet ⭐

The centerpiece, and the answer to how trig actually circulates online: as formula sheets, in comment threads that mix appreciation with correction — the crowd fact-checks each other's sheets. So this bench is a fact-checker. Pick a claimed identity from the list — the list does not tell you which claims are true. Test it at angles you choose, or blast it with 200 random angles. True identities hold to float precision everywhere. False ones break, and the bench prints the exact angles that break them. Two of the claims on the list are wrong in the ways real sheets are wrong; finding them is the exercise. One honest limit, stated now: holding at 200 angles is overwhelming evidence, not a proof — the lesson page says what a proof is and where the derivations live.

Panel 4 — Solve-It

Triangles: enter any three parts of a triangle (with at least one side) and the bench solves the rest with the laws of sines and cosines. The famous trap is handled in the open: give it two sides and a non-included angle (SSA) and there may be zero, one, or two different triangles that fit — the bench computes the count and shows every solution, instead of quietly handing you one. Equations: solve sin x = k or cos x = k on [0, 2π). The little circle shows why there are usually two answers — a horizontal (or vertical) line cuts a circle twice — and when |k| > 1 it says plainly that there are none, never a NaN.

Panel 5 — Move through the equation

Pick a formula, give its variables test numbers, and step through the evaluation one move at a time: each intermediate value lands in place before the next is computed, and at the end the bench compares the long way against the direct answer. Try sin 75° the identity way — sin(45° + 30°) — and watch four small values add up to the number your calculator would have given you in one step.

Panel 6 — From the discussion

The five most-discussed trig posts found across Instagram, Facebook and Threads on 24 November 2026, engagement-ranked, with their counts as found that day. They are nearly all formula sheets — and their comment threads are where the corrections live. Panel 3 is that culture, turned into a tool.

The model, stated plainly

Nothing here is simulated. The circle bench reads coordinates off exact sine and cosine; the verifier evaluates both sides of a claim with the same functions and compares; the solver is the two laws applied in the open. The verifier skips angles where a formula itself blows up (near a pole of tan or sec, both sides are enormous and a comparison means nothing) — skips are counted and printed, never silent. Trigonometry Deep Dive — the unit circle is the whole subject

A wall of formula sheets walked in off the internet. This bench checks them.

Trigonometry circulates online as cheat sheets — handwritten, photographed, shared by the thousand — and their comment threads mix thanks with corrections, because sheets have errors and the crowd catches them. So: here is the canon, drawn from one circle; here is the circle, with sine, cosine and tangent read off it as lengths; and here is a checker that tests any claimed identity at hundreds of angles and prints the angles where the false ones break. Then solve triangles and equations with the same two laws, and step through the formulas with your own numbers inside them.

Panel 1 · The formula canon — every card is a door

Definitions · SOH-CAH-TOA
sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse
cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse
tan θ = opposite / adjacent
In a right triangle. On the unit circle the hypotenuse is 1, so sin and cos become the point's own coordinates — Panel 2 draws them as lengths.
Reciprocal identities
csc θ = 1 / sin θ
sec θ = 1 / cos θ
cot θ = 1 / tan θ
Three more names for the same three lengths, flipped. Each is undefined exactly where its partner is zero.
Quotient identities
tan θ = sin θ / cos θ
cot θ = cos θ / sin θ
The wall length is the height divided by the floor length — slope, in one word. This claim is on the verifier's list.
Pythagorean identity
sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
The point sits ON the circle x² + y² = 1 — this is Pythagoras wearing the circle's clothes. The most-animated identity on the internet (see Panel 6).
Pythagorean identity II
1 + tan²θ = sec²θ
Divide the first identity by cos²θ and this falls out. Also on the verifier's list.
Pythagorean identity III
1 + cot²θ = csc²θ
Divide by sin²θ instead. All three are the same circle, read three ways.
Angle sum · sine
sin(a + b) = sin a·cos b + cos a·sin b
sin(a − b) = sin a·cos b − cos a·sin b
The hardest-working formulas on the sheets — and the site of the most famous error in the subject. The verifier's list holds a claim about sin(a + b); handle with care.
Angle sum · cosine
cos(a + b) = cos a·cos b − sin a·sin b
cos(a − b) = cos a·cos b + sin a·sin b
Note the sign flip against intuition: plus in the angle, minus in the formula. Sheets get this wrong; the checker is watching.
Angle sum · tangent
tan(a + b) = (tan a + tan b) / (1 − tan a·tan b)
tan(a − b) = (tan a − tan b) / (1 + tan a·tan b)
The quotient identity applied to the two above. Undefined exactly where the denominator vanishes — the verifier skips those angles and says so.
Double angle
sin 2a = 2·sin a·cos a
cos 2a = cos²a − sin²a = 2cos²a − 1 = 1 − 2sin²a
tan 2a = 2·tan a / (1 − tan²a)
The sum formulas with b = a. Three costumes for cos 2a; pick by what you already know.
Half angle
sin(a/2) = ±√((1 − cos a) / 2)
cos(a/2) = ±√((1 + cos a) / 2)
tan(a/2) = sin a / (1 + cos a)
The ± is real: the sign depends on which quadrant a/2 lives in. The verifier tests the squared forms, which need no ±.
Law of sines
a / sin A = b / sin B = c / sin C
Every side is proportional to the sine of the angle across from it. The engine of Panel 4 — and of its ambiguous case.
Law of cosines
c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos C
a² = b² + c² − 2bc·cos A
b² = a² + c² − 2ac·cos B
Pythagoras with a correction term: when C = 90° the term vanishes and the old theorem is what's left.
Area of a triangle
Area = ½ · a · b · sin C
Base times height, with the height written as b·sin C. Any two sides and the angle between them.
Degrees ↔ radians
radians = degrees × π / 180
degrees = radians × 180 / π
180° = π rad · 360° = 2π rad
A radian is the angle that wraps one radius of arc along the circle. Every formula below this card assumes radians.
Arc length & sector area
arc length s = r·θ
sector area = ½ · r² · θ  (θ in radians)
The fraction θ/2π of the full circumference 2πr — and of the full area πr². Two formulas, one fraction.
Exact values at the classic angles — the ones the sheets bold
θ0°30°45°60°90°
radians0π/6π/4π/3π/2
sin θ01/2√2/2√3/21
cos θ1√3/2√2/21/20
tan θ0√3/31√3undefined

Read the sin row left to right: 0, 1/2, √2/2, √3/2, 1 — that's √0/2, √1/2, √2/2, √3/2, √4/2. Cos is the same row backwards. The proof table at the bottom checks every one of these closed forms against the machine's sine and cosine.

Panel 2 · The unit circle bench — the functions are lengths

Drag the point on the circle, or slide. The amber segment on the floor is cos θ. The cyan segment standing on its end is sin θ. The violet segment up the wall at x = 1 is tan θ — watch it run off the chart near 90° and come back from the other side: that's not a glitch, that's what tangent does.

Graph sweep

The sweep runs through two full turns. The bright part of each graph is what the circle has drawn so far; the dot riding each curve is the segment length at this instant, carried across.

Panel 3 · Check the Sheet ⭐ — the crowd's correction culture, as a tool

The list does not say which claims are true. Two of them are wrong in the ways real sheets are wrong. Your job — the comment section's job — is to find them.

Angles where the formula itself blows up (a pole of tan or sec) are skipped and counted — near a pole both sides are enormous and comparing them proves nothing. Skips are printed, never silent.

Pick a claim and test it. A single angle can only convict, never acquit — one counterexample kills a claim, but a claim that survives one angle has survived exactly one angle. The 200-angle blast is the closest thing to the crowd: many eyes, many angles.

Panel 4 · Solve-It — triangles and equations, with the traps labelled

Triangle solver

Fill in any three parts (at least one side). Standard notation: side a faces angle A, and so on. Angles in degrees.

Equation solver

Try k = 1.5 after a legal one. The bench will tell you there is no such angle — a horizontal line above the circle meets nothing — in words, not in NaNs.

The solver names the case it's in — SSS, SAS, ASA or the ambiguous SSA — and shows every triangle that fits your numbers.
