Algebra Deep Dive: the crowd argues about the road, so we built all the roads

On 24 November 2026 we ranked sixty algebra posts across Instagram, Facebook, and Threads by how much discussion they drew, and a pattern fell out that any teacher will recognise: nobody argues about the answer. Everybody argues about the method. The most-discussed algebra post of the season — about two thousand comments — was three people solving x² + 6x − 16 = 0 while the thread fought over factoring vs the formula vs completing the square. A worksheet's commenters demanded the same problems redone "by completing a square." Two of the top five posts were about Cramer's Rule. So this workshop is built the way the crowd actually argues: one equation, every road, same destination — and you are invited to check that the destinations really do match. They do; the measurements are at the bottom of this page.

First door: the canon, on cards

Before the arguments, the furniture. Panel 1 carries the major formulas of a high-school algebra course — slope and the line, the quadratic formula and its discriminant, the factoring patterns (difference of squares, perfect-square trinomials), exponent and radical rules, arithmetic and geometric sequences and their sums, midpoint and distance, absolute value, and the inequality sign-flip. Each card states the formula, says it once in plain language, and shows one worked micro-example computed by the page — and each has a Load button that carries its numbers into a working bench, because a formula you have only read is a rumour; a formula you have loaded and run is a tool.

The arena: four roads up the same hill

The arena solves ax² + bx + c = 0 four ways, every step computed from the coefficients you type. The honest comparison — the crowd's argument, answered fairly — is this:

RoadWhen it is the best roadWhat it costs / where it breaks
FactoringWhen the factors are kind — integer roots, small coefficients. Fastest road there is; also the best check on any other road's answer.Most quadratics in the wild don't factor over the integers. A road that often isn't there.
Quadratic formulaAlways available. The discriminant D reports the root situation before you compute anything.Arithmetic-heavy; sign errors breed in the ±. It tells you the roots and nothing about the shape.
Completing the squareWhen you need the vertex form — graphing, optimisation, conics — or when you want to know why the formula works.The most steps of the four. But it is the only road that is also a proof.
GraphingWhen the question is really "where does it cross?" — and for seeing D < 0 instead of being told.Reads roots to picture accuracy unless paired with a computation. A witness, not a calculator.

Under the arena sits the derivation bench: completing the square performed on ax² + bx + c = 0 with your numbers substituted into every step — divide by a, move the constant, add (b/2a)² to both sides, factor the perfect square, take roots with the ±. The last line checks the result against the formula itself. This was the fourth most-discussed post (180 comments, worked in red and blue ink); here it is a machine, and it works for a negative a exactly as happily as for a positive one — the proofs below measured that case deliberately, because sign handling is where derivations go to die.

The Olympiad corner runs x³ + x = 30 the way the crowd did (190 comments): hunt a whole-number root among the divisors of 30 — the rational root theorem says those are the only candidates — find x = 3 on the fifth test, divide it out with the factor theorem, and interrogate the remaining quadratic, whose discriminant is −31. The hunt is a method; the factor theorem is what turns a lucky guess into a finished proof.

The matrices centerpiece

Steven particularly likes matrices, and the engagement data says the crowd agrees with him: Cramer's Rule took two of the top five posts (300 and 130 comments). Panel 3 therefore gives systems of equations the same treatment as the quadratic — three roads, one solution, agreement measured. Elimination / substitution is the mechanic's road: kill entries below the pivot, back-substitute. Cramer's Rule is the jeweller's road: one determinant per unknown, the 3×3 determinant itself computed by cofactor expansion in full view. The inverse road — Gauss–Jordan on [A | I], every row operation printed — is the industrial road: pay once for A⁻¹ and every right-hand side after that is a multiplication. The panel's verdict line only lights up when all three agree, and a determinant-zero system is labelled singular in plain words. There is no fourth outcome where the page shrugs and prints NaN.

Cramer, 3×3:  D = a₁(det of its minor) − b₁(det of its minor) + c₁(det of its minor)  ·  x = D_x/D, y = D_y/D, z = D_z/D

Worth saying out loud, because the posts rarely do: Cramer's Rule is a beautiful formula and a poor algorithm — its cost grows factorially, which is why working software eliminates instead. The bench keeps it anyway, in the place of honour, because understanding a system through its determinants teaches you what a solution is in a way that row operations alone never quite say. Beauty and efficiency are different virtues; the workshop refuses to choose, and shows both.

The balance, and the tracer

Panel 4 is the one law of equation-solving made physical: whatever you do, do it to both sides. Terms are chips; clicking one moves it across the equals sign and flips its sign. The buttons add, subtract, multiply, and divide both sides at once. Both sides are evaluated at your test value after every move, with a TRUE / FALSE verdict — and a Cheat button changes only one side, so you can watch the verdict catch it at the old solution (in the proofs, the smuggled +5 leaves the sides at 28 vs 23 where x = 6 used to balance them). It starts from 4(y − 3) = 8 — the humble two-step equation a hand solved in blue marker to 100 comments — because the law is easiest to see where the arithmetic is kind.

Panel 5, the number tracer, is the same idea from inside the expression: type 4*(y−3), set y = 5, and watch the value flow — substitute, reduce the bracket (5 − 3 = 2), multiply (4 × 2 = 8) — innermost first, each intermediate printed where it happened, until one number remains and it happens to be exactly the right-hand side of the crowd's equation. That little click — that's why y = 5 is the solution — is the moment algebra stops being ritual and becomes reading.

One proof initially failed during construction, and the failure was in the proof, not the mathematics: the derivation bench lists its roots in +branch, −branch order while the formula lists them the other way for a negative a, and the first version of the check compared the lists position-by-position. The sets were always identical; the check now sorts before comparing, and this note stays here because that is exactly the kind of discrepancy this workshop exists to teach you to interrogate.

Honest notes. The derivation bench's symbolic steps are template-instantiated, not a computer algebra system: the page performs the completing-the-square procedure with your coefficients substituted into a fixed, correct sequence of steps — it does not parse or symbolically manipulate arbitrary algebra, and it doesn't pretend to. The factoring road searches integer factor pairs, so it honestly reports when no tidy factors exist instead of forcing one. The move-the-terms balance models each side as a·v + b (linear in the variable, after distribution) — quadratics live in the arena, not on the balance. The tracer's grammar is deliberately small: + − * / ^, brackets, sqrt, and the variables x and y. Social counts are as found on 24 November 2026 and will drift; the links are the durable part. The graph is drawn from the same coefficients the other roads compute with — the picture is a witness, and the proofs treat it as one (values and sign changes), not as a measurement instrument.
