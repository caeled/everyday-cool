Everyday Math · The lesson behind Calculus Without Fear

Calculus is two ideas — zoom in, and add up — and they undo each other

Calculus has a reputation as the subject where math gets scary. It isn't. Strip away the notation and there are exactly two moves: zoom in on a curve until it looks straight, and chop an area into slices and add them up. Everything else — derivatives, integrals, the fundamental theorem, the rocket equation — is those two moves, used carefully. This lesson walks through the five benches on the page in order, with the numbers the page measured.

Before you start: what you already need

Slope from algebra (rise over run), the area of a rectangle (width × height), and the idea of a graph. That's it. If those feel shaky, the Algebra Deep Dive is the place to warm up; the Trigonometry Deep Dive explains the sine curve that shows up on the benches.

For parents: every bench is built so you can sit beside a kid and ask “what do you think will happen if…?” before moving a slider. The numbers on screen are real measurements, so “let's check” is always a fair answer.

Bench 1 — slope by zooming in

A straight line has one slope everywhere. A curve doesn't — it's steeper in some places than others. So what does “the slope of a curve at a point” even mean? Calculus answers: pick a second point a little way along, a gap h away, and draw the straight line through both (the secant). Its slope is plain rise over run:

(f(a + h) − f(a)) / h — the difference quotient.

Now shrink h. For f(x) = x² at a = 1.5 the page measures the quotient as exactly 3 + h: 4 when h = 1, 3.1 when h = 0.1, 3.01, 3.001… It never needs h to be zero — you can see where it's headed. That destination, 3, is the derivative f′(1.5), and the line with that slope is the tangent. The zoom slider shows the same thing a second way: zoom in a thousand times on any point of a smooth curve and it is a straight line to the eye, and the tangent is that line.

The table's last column, gap ÷ h, settles to a constant — the error shrinks in step with h. That's called first-order accuracy, and it's why each tenfold shrink of h buys one more correct digit.

The float wall. The red bottom row tries h = 10⁻¹². Mathematically that should be better still; on a computer it is worse (for x² at a = 1 the page measured a gap of 1.78×10⁻⁴ at h = 10⁻¹², against 1.00×10⁻⁶ at h = 10⁻⁶). Computers keep about 16 significant digits, and subtracting two nearly equal numbers throws most of them away. Real limits live in the mathematics; computers can only approach them, and the honest thing is to show where they stop.

Bench 2 — the derivative is a function too

Do bench 1 at every point and write down each slope: you get a new graph, the slope graph, called the derivative f′(x). Drag across x² and the gold dots line up into the straight line 2x. Drag across x³ − 3x and the dots cross zero exactly where the curve has its hilltop and valley (x = −1 and x = 1) — slope zero means “flat for an instant”. Drag across eˣ and the slope graph lands right on top of the curve: eˣ is its own slope, which is why it shows up in growth and decay everywhere.

The power rule, discovered rather than told. The page measures the slope of x², x³, x⁴, x⁵ at x = 1, 2, 3, then divides each slope by xⁿ⁻¹. Every row comes out as n itself (to six decimals at x = 2: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5). So the slope of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹ — the first rule every calculus class teaches, found here by measuring first.

Bench 3 — area by stacking slices

The second idea. How much area sits under a curve? Cut it into n thin strips, pretend each is a rectangle, and add width × height. Where you measure each rectangle's height — its left edge, right edge, or middle — gives the left, right and midpoint Riemann sums (after Bernhard Riemann, who made the idea precise in the 1850s).

The page's error table shows a pattern worth noticing: double n and the left and right sums get about 2× closer, but the midpoint sum gets 4× closer. For x² on [0, 3] the midpoint sum falls short by exactly 9 / (4n²) — the page checks that to 15 digits. Picking the middle lets each slice's overshoot on one side cancel its undershoot on the other.

The limit of these sums as the slices get infinitely thin is the integral, written ∫ f(x) dx. The tall S is a stretched “S” for sum; the dx is the slice width. One honest wrinkle the proofs record: √x on [0, 4] converges slowest of the five curves, because its slope is vertical at x = 0 and the first slice is always the worst.

Bench 4 — the fundamental theorem

Here's the surprise that makes calculus one subject instead of two. Let A(x) be the area under f from 0 up to x. Push x a tiny bit further, by dx: the area grows by a sliver about f(x) tall and dx wide. So the change in A divided by dx — the slope of A — is f(x).

The slope of the area-so-far is the height of the curve. Zooming in (bench 1) undoes adding up (bench 3). The page checks it at 13 points on four curves, and the gap was never bigger than 3.3×10⁻⁷. The flip side is the shortcut every student uses: to find the area from a to b, find any function F whose slope is f, and subtract — ∫ₐᵇ f dx = F(b) − F(a). The page checks ∫₁² x³ dx = 2⁴/4 − 1⁴/4 = 15/4 against 20,000 slices.

Try f(x) = x − 1: for x below 1 the curve is under the axis, the shading turns red, and A(x) goes downhill. Area below the axis counts as negative — exactly what you'd want if f were a velocity and A a position.

Bench 5 — worked example: the rocket

This is a deliberately simplified rocket — not the vehicle the Rocketry & Thrust exhibit flies. The two share the Tsiolkovsky equation and g₀; this bench strips everything else away (constant thrust, uniform gravity, no drag) where Rocketry's simulator keeps drag, a standard atmosphere and 1/r² gravity. Flown straight up, it has constant thrust F, and burns propellant at a steady rate ṁ = F / (Isp·g₀), so its mass drops in a straight line: m(t) = m₀ − ṁt. Newton's second law gives the acceleration:

a(t) = F / m(t) − g₀, with g₀ = 9.80665 m/s².

The acceleration grows during the burn — same push, less rocket. Now the two ideas:

  • Velocity is the area under acceleration. Stack slices of a(t) and you get v(t). Doing the integral by hand (the slices become a logarithm) gives v(t) = vₑ·ln(m₀ / m(t)) − g₀t, where vₑ = Isp·g₀ is the exhaust speed. At burnout that is the rocket equation, Δv = vₑ·ln(m₀/m_f), minus the speed gravity stole along the way, g₀·t_b.
  • Altitude is the area under velocity. Integrate once more: h(t) = (vₑ/ṁ)·[(m₀ − m) − m·ln(m₀/m)] − ½g₀t², with m = m(t).

With the page's default rocket (1,000 kg at liftoff, 60% propellant, Isp 250 s, thrust twice its weight): vₑ = 2451.6625 m/s, ṁ = 8 kg/s, the burn lasts 75 s, and the burnout speed is 2246.44 − 735.50 = 1510.94 m/s at a height of about 43,972 m. With only 12 slices the page's stacked-slice answer is already within 1.5 m/s; with 20,000 slices it agrees with the closed form to better than a millimetre per second. The page also checks the chain backwards: the slope of v(t) is a(t), and the slope of h(t) is v(t).

What the model leaves out, on purpose: air drag (large for a real rocket low down), gravity weakening with height (about 1.4% weaker at 44 km), the thrust change between sea level and vacuum, and steering. The coast-to-peak figure assumes no air at all, so it is an upper bound. Rocketry & Thrust's liftoff simulator adds drag; the shape of the calculus is the same.

Bench 6 — where it shows up

Speedometers and odometers, medicine doses, compound interest, highway ramps, weather forecasts, video-game physics, CT scans and rockets. The cards on the page keep each one to a sentence or two; the common thread is always “a rate of change” (a derivative) or “a total built from small pieces” (an integral).

Honesty notes

  • A computer can't take a limit. Benches 1, 2 and 4 approach the derivative with small but finite h; the float-wall row shows where that stops working.
  • Bench 2's “measured” slopes use a central difference, (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h with h = 10⁻⁵, which is more accurate than bench 1's one-sided quotient. Bench 1 uses the one-sided version because it is the one you can draw as a secant.
  • Bench 4's A(x) is computed by 2,000 midpoint slices, not looked up; the exact antiderivative is printed beside it for comparison.
  • The rocket is a teaching model (above). The velocity slices use the midpoint of each time step; the altitude slices average the velocity at each step's two edges, so the altitude comes only from the computed velocities, not from the formula.
