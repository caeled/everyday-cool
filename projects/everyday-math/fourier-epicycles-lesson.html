Everyday Math · The back door to Epicycles

Epicycles — the lesson

Epicycles is a drawing machine built on one of the great facts of mathematics: any closed curve — a treble clef, a rocket, a scribble of your own — can be traced by a chain of circles, each one spinning on the rim of the one before it. The sizes, speeds, and starting angles of those circles are not fitted or guessed. They are computed, exactly, by a Discrete Fourier Transform of the curve itself. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the mathematics, and the audit that drove the shipped transform headlessly against an independent DFT and measured where the page's own claims hold — and the one place where the famous number on a preset label does not mean what it appears to mean.

Verdict: The transform is exact. On every one of the seven shipped presets, every coefficient the page computes agrees with an independent DFT of the same samples to at most 1.3 × 10−12 — and with all harmonics kept, the chain of circles redraws the sampled curve with an RMS error of about 2 × 10−12 pixels. The page's own on-screen Energy and RMS readouts match a direct reconstruction to the last digit printed. The bends are not in the DFT. They are in what gets fed to it — an arc-length resampling that quietly rewrites the spectrum — and in the Square Wave preset, which is not the textbook square wave its label invites you to picture.

FALSE as shipped — the “~9% Gibbs ringing” label: the Square Wave preset advertises “Live Gibbs ~9% ringing.” Measured at the preset's own default settings (15 harmonics, sorted by amplitude, the way the page opens), the reconstruction's peak is 124.66 against a plateau of 115 — an overshoot of 9.66, which is 4.2% of the 230-unit jump, the denominator the Gibbs constant is defined on. The textbook figure (overshoot ≈ 8.95% of the jump, peak ≈ 135.58) appears only under the other sort order at exactly N = 15 — and then it goes away: at N = 49 the overshoot is 0.7% of the jump, and at full reconstruction the peak is 115.96. True Gibbs ringing does not decay as you add harmonics; it converges to its 8.95% and stays. This one decays because the sampled signal has no true discontinuity for it to ring on — see Receipt 5. Right number, wrong phenomenon, and only at one setting.

What the exhibit is

Seven curated curves (Treble Clef, Pi Symbol, Hilbert Fractal, Square Wave, Heart, Rocket, Spiral Galaxy) plus a freehand mode: drag on the canvas, release, and your sketch is decomposed on the spot. Two views of the same mathematics run side by side — a single complex phasor chain, and a dual-axis mode with separate X and Y chains projecting onto a crosshair. A harmonics slider truncates the chain, a spectrum chart lets you mute, boost, or solo individual frequencies, and the telemetry card reports harmonics used, Parseval energy captured, and RMS error live. Every one of those readouts was checked in the audit below.

The mathematics

A curve is a signal

Walk once around a closed curve at a steady pace and write down your position as a complex number, zn = xn + i·yn, at M equally spaced moments. That list of numbers is a signal, and the Discrete Fourier Transform takes it apart into pure rotations:

ck = (1/M) · Σn zn · e−i2πkn/M Each coefficient ck is one epicycle: |ck| is its radius, arg(ck) its starting angle, and the integer k its speed — k full turns per circuit of the curve, backwards if k is negative. The exhibit's formula box states exactly this, with the 1/M scaling on the forward transform, and the audit confirms the code does what the box says.
z(θ) = c0 + Σk ck · eikθ Put the circles back together — centre c0 (the curve's average position), then each rotating radius laid tip-to-tail — and the final tip retraces the curve. With all M − 1 non-zero coefficients kept, the retracing is not approximate. It is the same list of numbers, re-expressed: a change of basis, not a fit.

Parseval: energy is the error budget

Drop some circles and the drawing degrades — measurably. Parseval's theorem says the mean squared distance between the full curve and the truncated one is exactly the sum of |ck|2 over the coefficients you dropped. So “Energy Captured” and “RMS Error” on the telemetry card are not two opinions; they are the same number read two ways, and the biggest circles are worth the most — which is why the page sorts by amplitude by default, and why the error falls so fast: on most presets the top 25 circles already hold over 99.9% of the energy.

The pace is part of the signal

Here is the subtlety the whole audit turns on. A Fourier decomposition describes not just where the pen goes but when — the same geometric curve walked at a different pace is a different signal with different coefficients. The exhibit resamples every curve uniformly by arc length: the pen is deemed to move at constant speed along the path, whatever the original drawing's timing was. That is a clean, defensible choice — and it rewrites spectra, as Receipt 4 shows with a signal whose true spectrum is known to the last digit.

The audit — what was driven, and how

The shipped script was extracted from the page and run headlessly in Node with DOM stubs. Its own functions did the work: the preset builders generated each curve, its resampleUniform resampled them, and its computeDFTFromPoints computed the coefficients — no re-implementation, no reading the code and nodding. Against that, the audit computed an independent naive O(M²) DFT of the same sampled points, reconstructed curves directly from the shipped coefficients at the sample instants θ = 2πn/M, and probed known signals (a pure circle, a counter-rotating circle, a two-tone curve), every preset, both decomposition modes, and the edge cases. Conventions found in the shipped transform: forward sign e−i…, 1/M scaling, signed frequencies k = −(M/2 − 1) … +M/2 for even M — the Nyquist frequency appears once, as +M/2, which is correct, since +M/2 and −M/2 are the same rotation sampled.

Nothing here is a bug: constant-speed walking genuinely is a different signal, and the DFT of it is exact (Receipt 1). But it means the spectrum on screen is the spectrum of the resampled walk, not of the curve as originally parameterised — a distinction that bites two presets by name, next. The resampler itself is honest geometry: its samples lie exactly on the drawn polyline, spaced equally in arc length. On a square outline sampled at M = 7, for instance, the samples land at (57.1, 0), (100, 14.3), … — on the edges, corners simply not visited unless a sample happens to fall there. Chord distances between neighbours dip where the path turns (Pi: minimum spacing 6.31 against a mean of 9.18; Galaxy: 10.91 against 14.33) — that dip is the corner, paid for in high frequencies.

Receipt 5: the Square Wave is not a square wave

The preset is a path: x sweeps linearly from −220 to +220 while y flips between −115 and +115 twice — and then, because the preset is flagged “mirror,” the resampler appends the whole path in reverse, so the pen retraces its steps home. Measured consequences:

Receipt 6: the Heart's “5-harmonic convergence,” measured

The heart's formula is a trigonometric polynomial: sampled uniformly in its own parameter t, it has exactly eight non-zero coefficients, k = ±1 … ±4, and nothing else — the audit verified that directly. But the exhibit samples it by arc length (Receipt 4), and the spectrum spreads: frequencies with |k| ≤ 4 hold 99.832% of the energy, not 100%. The preset label says “Rapid 5-harmonic convergence,” and its default is actually 8 harmonics. Measured: the five largest circles capture 99.20% of the energy with an RMS error of 15.47 px on a heart ~410 px tall — rapid in energy, visibly loose in pixels; at the shipped default of 8, the error is 7.10 px. The claim is a fair summary of the energy column and an optimistic one of the picture.

PerformanceThe transform is O(M²): ~5–18 ms per preset at the shipped sizes (256–384 samples) in headless Node, 137 ms at a hypothetical M = 1024. Nothing at the shipped sizes can feel slow; a freehand sketch is capped at 320 samples by the page's own sizing rule.

Simplifications and divergences, named

Mirrored presets are retraced, not closed: the Treble Clef and the Square Wave are open strokes. The page closes them by appending the stroke in reverse, so the pen draws the clef and then undraws it. The audit's fingerprint: in both mirrored presets the coefficients come in perfectly matched pairs, |ck| = |c−k| to within 1.3 × 10−12, while every genuinely closed preset is strongly asymmetric (differences up to 158 on the Heart). What you are watching is a single stroke and its echo — beautiful, and worth knowing when you read its spectrum.

The Hilbert “star” is one segment short of closed — and the resampler pays the difference: the builder generates 64 Moore-curve vertices (its own comment says “order 3” while the loop variable says 2; 64 vertices is what ships), rotated 45°. The first and last vertices sit exactly one step apart (41.42 units, the same as every other step), and the cyclic resampler supplies that final segment. Because every step is equal and M = 384 = 64 × 6, this preset's samples are the most uniform of all seven: spacing 6.903 at minimum, mean, and maximum alike.

Placeholder numbers in the static HTML: the page as first painted says “48 / 255” harmonics, 99.84% energy, 0.64 px RMS, and the slider's markup caps at 255 — none of which matches the Treble Clef it opens on (measured: 48 / 319, 99.947%, 2.68 px). The code corrects all of it on the first frames; the audit's numbers are the corrected ones. A reader who screenshots the first paint would catch numbers the exhibit itself disowns a moment later.

The Nyquist term is carried once, as +M/2: for M = 256 the term list runs k = −127 … +128. That is the correct bookkeeping — at the Nyquist frequency the + and − rotations coincide at every sample — but a student counting terms for a −128 to match the +128 will not find one, and shouldn't.

What was not audited: the additive audio synthesizer (it weights live partials by |ck|, capped at 22 voices — its inputs are the coefficients verified here, its sound was not measured) and the on-canvas rendering, which draws the same evaluate-the-chain sum the audit reconstructed from; the pen positions the audit computed are the ones the renderer consumes.
