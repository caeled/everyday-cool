Yahtzee — the lesson

Yahtzee looks like a game about luck, and it is — but it is luck with a price list attached. Every hand you will ever roll is one of exactly 7,776 outcomes, every one of them countable, and the game on the other side of this page counts them: no simulated rolls, no hard-coded odds table, just enumeration. This page is the other half of the game — the sample space, what holding dice really does to it, why expected value is the turn's real currency, why the scorecard matters more than the dice, and how the counting is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

A yacht game that became a probability engine

Yahtzee descends from a family of dice games — Yacht, Generala, and their cousins — and was sold by the E.S. Lowe Company from 1956; Milton Bradley bought Lowe in 1973 and took the game over. The story the company told is that a wealthy couple played it on their yacht and asked for copies for their friends. Whatever the marketing, the design is the interesting part: five dice, three rolls, thirteen boxes, each box fillable exactly once. The dice supply the randomness. The scorecard — thirteen one-way doors — supplies everything else: the strategy, the regret, and the endgame.

The whole universe is 7,776 outcomes

Roll five dice and the result is one of 65 = 7,776 equally likely ordered outcomes. Every probability in the game is a count over that universe — how many of the 7,776 complete the hand — divided by 7,776. The game's proof counts them with the shipped code itself, and here is what it finds on a single roll, nothing held:

Hand, in one rollOutcomesFractionProbability

Read the Yahtzee line slowly: there are only six ways to roll five of a kind — all 1s, all 2s, and so on — out of nearly eight thousand. That is why the box pays 50 and why people shout when it lands. And read the Full House line against it: a full house is fifty times as likely and pays half as much. The scorecard's fixed prices are not the probabilities — the whole game lives in the gap between the two.

Holding dice is conditional probability you can touch

When you hold dice, you shrink the universe. Hold four 6s and re-roll one die, and the universe is no longer 7,776 outcomes — it is 6, and exactly one of them completes the Yahtzee:

P(Yahtzee | four of a kind held, one roll left) = 1/6 ≈ 16.7% The "|" is the whole lesson: probability given what you already hold. Holding is how a player reaches into the sample space and reshapes it.

With two rolls left the arithmetic chains: convert on the first re-roll (1/6), or miss (5/6) and convert on the second (1/6 again) — 1/6 + 5/6 · 1/6 = 11/36 ≈ 30.6%. The game's engine gets that figure the hard way, by recursion over every outcome, and the proof checks the recursion against an independent ordered-outcome brute force: they agree to the last decimal. Holding three 6s with two rolls left, the same machinery gives 9.34% — a number nobody guesses well by feel, which is exactly why the odds panel counts it live instead of letting your gut price it.

The full turn — best play, counted exactly

A turn is three rolls with two hold decisions between them. The probability of completing a box "within the turn" is therefore not one count but a tree: roll, hold the best way for that box, roll, hold again, roll. The engine evaluates that tree exactly, over the 252 distinct dice hands, and the odds panel shows its figures before your first roll of each turn. The proof prints the whole table:

The Yahtzee figure deserves its line: the engine's recursion produces 0.046029 — the ≈ 4.6% that published analyses of the game report — from nothing but the rules and the enumeration. Three rolls and two good hold decisions multiply your Yahtzee chances sixty-fold over a single roll (0.077% → 4.60%), and still leave it a one-in-twenty-two event. Both halves of that sentence are the game.

Expected value is the turn's real currency

Probabilities tell you how often; they do not tell you what to do. For that you price each hold in expected points: for every possible hold, average the points the turn will bank over every outcome of the re-roll, assuming best follow-up holds and the best open box at the end. That is the game's advisor, and its arithmetic can be checked by hand on its own favourite example. You hold 6·6·6·6 and a stray 2, one roll left:

EV(hold the four 6s) = (1/6)(50) + (5/6)(27) = 185/6 ≈ 30.83 points Roll a 6 (1/6) and the hand banks the Yahtzee, 50. Roll anything else (5/6) and it banks a kind box at the sum of all five dice — 24 plus the new die, 27 on average. The advisor's distribution agrees: this hold ends in the Yahtzee box 1/6 of the time and in Three of a Kind the other 5/6.

Or hold 2·3·4·5 against a doubled 5: one die decides between the Large Straight (a 1 or a 6, 2/6 of the time, 40 points) and the Small Straight already in hand (4/6, 30 points) — EV = (2/6)(40) + (4/6)(30) = 33.33, and the advisor recommends exactly that hold. The proof verifies the advisor's expected values against a second, independently written brute-force evaluator — ordered outcomes, index-by-index holds, no shared code — on three states including a two-roll one: agreement to 1e-9 every time.

The dice have no memory. The scorecard has nothing else.

Every roll is independent: the dice do not know what they did last turn, and a "due" Yahtzee is a story, not a statistic. But you are not independent of your last turn, because the scorecard is state. Each box you fill removes an option from every future turn; each box you scratch removes a place to hide. Early, the card is all freedom and a scratched box is cheap. Late, the card is a corridor: the last two turns offer exactly two boxes, and the dice will be priced against those two and no others. That is why the scratch decision — banking a deliberate 0 — is a real move: you are not scoring this hand, you are choosing which future hands remain possible. The dice are memoryless. The decisions compound.

Why the advisor is per-turn, and says so

The advisor maximises the expected points banked this turn, with every box priced at face value. Whole-game optimal play is a different and much larger problem, because a box's true worth includes things its face does not show. The clearest is the upper bonus: the +35 at 63 means an upper box scored late, when it carries you over the line, is really worth its face plus up to 35 bonus points it unlocks — and an upper box scored when the bonus is already lost or already won is worth exactly its face. A Sixes box holding three 6s is 18 points on its face and, in the right week of the game, 53 points of consequence. The advisor cannot see that; it prices the 18 and tells you it is doing so, on the page, in the rules card. It also cannot see Yahtzee-bonus and joker effects. A per-turn advisor with named blind spots is an honest tool; a "best move" button that hid them would be a liar with good arithmetic.

How it's built — enumeration, memoized over 252 hands

Multisets, not rolls

The engine never simulates. It works with multisets — the hand [2,2,5] as one object, however the dice landed — of which there are only C(10,5) = 252 for five dice. Each multiset outcome of a re-roll carries a multinomial weight (how many of the 6n ordered rolls produce it), and the weights sum to exactly 6n — the proof checks that too. Probabilities with one roll left are direct weighted counts. With two rolls left, the engine recurses: for each outcome of the next roll, take the best hold for the target box, and evaluate again — memoized by hand, so each of the 252 hands is priced once per situation and then read from the table. The advisor runs the same machinery with points in place of completion: expected bank over all 32 holds, recursing the same way, with a second walk that records which box the money actually lands in and how often.

The joker, implemented as written

The bonus and joker live in one function that answers, for a hand being banked, what every open box may legally pay. A Yahtzee showing while the Yahtzee box holds 50: +100 (banked the moment the dice land), the matching upper box forced at its full sum if open; otherwise the lower boxes at full joker value — the hand counts as a full house and as both straights — and, if no lower box is open, a forced zero in an upper box. A Yahtzee box holding 0 switches all of it off. The proof drives each branch with crafted scorecards, including the two edges players argue about: five of a kind scores 0 as a full house without the joker, and 25 with it.

The proof, in one paragraph

Everything that decides an outcome lives in one sealed block of pure code in the game file, and the proof program lifts that exact block out and plays against it: 42 checks, all passing — the four one-roll fractions above to the exact outcome count; the full-turn Yahtzee probability at 4.60%; every box's scoring on crafted dice, edges included; the joker's branches on crafted cards; the advisor against the independent brute force; and a scripted thirteen-turn game, played by the advisor under a seeded deal, in which every box is used exactly once and the totals reconcile to the point. The full-turn odds are the engine's heaviest count — about half a second per box, computed live — so the game deals them into the panel one at a time while you look at your dice. "Counting…" is the enumeration actually happening.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the dice are a pseudorandom generator (mulberry32, seeded from Math.random when the page loads) — uniform, every face 1 in 6, independent between rolls, and named here rather than dressed up as physics. The advisor prices boxes at face value only: it does not price the +35 upper bonus (the shadow value described above) and does not price Yahtzee-bonus or joker effects — both blind spots are also named in the game itself. The two-roll and full-turn odds assume best play for that one box alone; chasing two boxes at once is a different, smaller probability, and the panel does not pretend otherwise. The +100 bonus is banked the moment the dice land on a Yahtzee while the box holds 50 — re-roll the hand away and the bonus stays (and landing it again pays again), which is this game's stated reading of the classic rule. Under a joker hand with the matching upper box filled and a lower box open, the upper boxes cannot be taken at zero — the joker hand must be played where it pays, per the classic joker rules as written on the game page.
