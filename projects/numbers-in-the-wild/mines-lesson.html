Mines — the lesson

Mines is the game that came with Windows, and it looks like a game about luck. It is a game about counting. Every number the board shows you is an exact census — a complete, honest count of the mines touching that one square — and a board full of censuses, followed carefully, will tell you where almost every mine is hiding. This page is the other half of the game: the patterns that do the telling, the arithmetic of difficulty, the one situation where the counting honestly runs out, and how the game is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The game that taught a generation the mouse

Minesweeper was created in 1989 by Curt Johnson and Robert Donner, and it reached most of the world bundled with Windows 3.1 in 1992. For millions of people it was the first program they ever used with a mouse — and it quietly taught the whole vocabulary: the precise left-click, the drag, and above all the right-click, which most software of the day barely used and this game made essential. Underneath the mouse lessons sits a pure deduction puzzle with no story, no opponent, and no randomness after the field is planted: just a hidden field, a set of exact counts, and you.

Every number is a census

Open a square and one of three things happens. It hides a mine, and the game is over. It is empty — a 0 — and the board opens the whole connected empty region and its numbered border for you, free. Or it shows a number, and that number is a census: of the (up to) eight squares touching this one, exactly that many hold mines. Not a hint, not a probability — a count, as exact as the count of chairs around a table.

a square showing 3 = exactly 3 mines among its ≤ 8 neighbours Edge squares have 5 neighbours; corner squares have 3. The census is always of the neighbours that exist.

One census rarely settles anything. The game is played in the overlap: two numbers side by side are counting mostly the same squares, and the difference between their counts is information about the squares they do not share. Everything below — every pattern, every deduction — is that one idea at work: compare the censuses, and let the arithmetic corner the mines. It is the same instinct as this room's other benches: a number is not a label, it is a measurement, and measurements can be cross-examined.

Reading the board — the working patterns

In the diagrams, F is a flag you have planted, · is a square still covered, and digits are revealed numbers.

The satisfied number — its flags are complete, so the rest is safe

F · · F 2 · · · ·

The 2 in the middle counts eight neighbours, and two of them already carry flags. Its census is fully accounted for — so every other covered square around it is safe, all six of them. This is the most-used move in the game, and the game automates it: click a satisfied number and it chords, opening all its remaining neighbours at once. Played straight, though: the chord trusts your flags. If one of them is wrong, the "safe" squares are not, and the chord can step on the mine your flag was supposed to be marking. The game's proof does exactly this on a scripted board and the chord detonates — a wrong receipt poisons everything downstream of it.

The 1-2-1 along a wall — subtraction between neighbours

· · · 1 2 1 ▓ ▓ ▓

Three numbers in a row against a wall (the board's edge works the same way; ▓ is wall, no squares beyond). Call the covered squares left, middle, right. The 2 counts all three and needs two mines among them. The left 1 counts left + middle and needs exactly one; the right 1 counts middle + right and needs exactly one. The only arrangement that satisfies all three censuses: mines at left and right, and the middle square is safe. No single number says that — the pattern says it, by subtraction. When the game's checker is handed exactly this position in the proof, it convicts both corners and clears the middle, which is how the patterns on this page stop being advice and become arithmetic.

The corner 1 — the smallest census on the board

1 · · ·

A number in the corner counts only three neighbours, the fewest on the board — which makes its statement the strongest per square. If one of the three is flagged, the other two are safe, full stop. Corners and edges speak about few squares, so what they say is worth more; the open middle of the board speaks about eight at a time and needs cross-examining. Start your reading where the censuses are smallest.

Density is the difficulty

The three fields differ in size, but size is not what makes Epic hard. Density is — the fraction of squares that hold a mine:

FieldSquaresMinesDensity
Easy9 × 9 = 811010/81 ≈ 12%
Hard16 × 16 = 2564040/256 ≈ 16%
Epic30 × 16 = 4809999/480 ≈ 21%

At 12% the empty regions are wide, the floods run far, and most numbers sit at the edge of open ground with few covered neighbours — short chains, easy reading. At 21% the covered squares crowd every census, chains run longer before they close, and the board deals out the situation in the next section far more often. Same rules, same counting — a different climate, set by one ratio. The buttons in the game print the densities because the density is the honest label for what you are choosing.

The honest wall — the forced guess

· 1 ·

Sometimes the counting runs out. Everything on the board is settled except this: a 1 whose only covered neighbours are two squares, with exactly one mine between them. Both arrangements fit every census on the board. There is no pattern, no subtraction, no further fact to gather — the position is a true 50/50, and the only move left is a guess. This game refuses to pretend otherwise. When a mine ends your game, a checker replays the position with the numbers alone and the lose screen tells you plainly which ending you got: that square was already convicted (the deduction was there and ran long), or that click was a forced guess (no logic, anywhere, could have cleared it).

A forced guess is a bet, and it deserves the treatment the Decisions Everywhere room gives bets: look at the price and the odds before you pay. Two refinements keep it from being a pure coin flip. First, the mine counter is global information: if it says exactly one mine remains and your 50/50 pair must hold it, then every other quiet covered square on the board is safe — the tie here settles squares elsewhere. Second, when you must guess, guess the square whose answer teaches more: opening a square that touches several numbers buys information even when it is safe, which is the same expected-value thinking as The Boring Bet Wins — you cannot always win the flip, but you can choose what the flip is worth.

How it's built — the coding choices

The field is planted after your first click

The board you see at the start holds no mines at all. Your first click names one square that must be safe, the field is planted around it (a partial Fisher–Yates shuffle of every other square), and only then do the counts get taken. The proof plants 1,000 fields per difficulty — 3,000 in all — and the clicked square is never a mine, and it recounts every square of 600 planted fields (163,400 squares) by brute force against the game's own counting, cell by cell.

Flood fill and the chord

Opening a 0 is a flood fill: walk the connected empty region, open it and its numbered border, stop at the numbers. The proof compares the game's flood against an independent reference implementation on 360 random zero-clicks across all three fields — the opened sets match exactly, every time, and a flagged square is never flood-opened. The chord is the satisfied-number pattern as a button, and it is played straight, as described above: it trusts flags completely, including wrong ones.

A pure core, and a checker that only claims what it can prove

Everything that decides an outcome — planting, counting, flooding, chording, winning, losing, and the forced-guess checker — lives in one sealed block of pure code in the game file, and the proof program lifts that exact block out and plays against it: 981 checks, all passing, including the win firing at exactly the last safe square and not one click earlier. The checker itself works by consistency: it asks, twice, whether a complete mine assignment exists that fits every revealed census, the total mine count, and your flags — once with the square safe, once with it a mine. Both stories possible: a forced guess. Only the mine story: convicted. It searches the frontier of covered squares with a fixed budget of steps, and if the budget runs out it says nothing at all rather than guess about your guess.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the first click protects only the square you clicked — some modern versions also guarantee its whole neighbourhood opens, and this one does not, so your first number can be a working census rather than a free flood. Flags are unlimited and the mine counter can go negative if you plant more flags than there are mines; that is the classic game's own behaviour, kept. The fields are honestly random: there is no "no-guess" board generation quietly removing the hard positions, so forced guesses genuinely occur, most often on Epic — the game names them instead of designing them away. The forced-guess checker trusts your flags as asserted facts; a wrong flag makes the position inconsistent and the checker then makes no claim, which is the honest response to a poisoned ledger. And the checker has a step budget — on a very large, very open frontier it may decline to rule, and the lose screen falls back to a plain "Boom" rather than a verdict it did not earn.
