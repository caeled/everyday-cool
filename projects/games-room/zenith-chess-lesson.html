Zenith Chess — the lesson

Zenith Chess is the sequel: the same kitchen-table idea as Zenith Othello, moved to the bigger board. A chess program that starts — verifiably, and the verification is below — with every piece worth exactly the same as every other: a queen is a pawn is a knight, 1.0 apiece, and every square on every piece’s map worth exactly nothing. Then it plays, and corrects its numbers a little after every game, and after a few thousand games of playing itself it has discovered the order of chess value on its own — pawn below knight below bishop below rook and queen — though not, even after 5,000 games, the textbook distances between them. The recreation was built by Gemini Neon (Google Antigravity) at Steven Powell’s direction and was integrated into this room unchanged — with one adjustment since, directed by Steven on 21 November 2026 and recorded in the honest box below: the watched self-play speeds were slowed to 1–2.5 seconds a move, because at the delivered speeds (down to 45 ms) a game flew by too fast to watch. Nothing else differs from the delivered file. This page is the collection’s half of the bargain: an audit. The rules engine was run against the standard perft test positions and matched the known counts exactly; the learning functions were extracted verbatim from the shipped file and run headlessly through 5,000 complete self-play games against a fixed-seed random generator so the numbers reproduce; and where the tribute’s own words say more than its code does, the last section of this page says exactly that, in the open.

The history — the sequel, briefly

The full telling lives with the Othello lesson: in the early 1980s Steven and his father Henry built a Heathkit Zenith computer on the family kitchen table; Henry’s Othello program kept a table that let it improve the more it was played; teenage Steven proposed letting it play itself to learn faster — an idea dismissed at the time, which worked, and which Steven counts in his research lineage (Paper 023) to this day. When Steven commissioned this chess version, his reasoning was the modern proof of that same proposal: in 2017 DeepMind’s AlphaZero learned chess from a blank slate by self-play alone and beat the strongest conventional engine on Earth. If the idea scales to that, it should also fit back onto the kitchen table. This file is the attempt — Henry’s learning table, rebuilt for the game his son’s generation watched a machine master from scratch.

The game — how a chess position gets a number

Every chess engine, from this tribute to the world-class ones, answers one question thousands of times: how good is this board, and for whom? The classical answer has three parts. Material: add up your pieces at their agreed prices — the textbook table, pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9, memorized by every beginner. Placement: a piece’s worth depends on where it stands, so each piece type also carries a piece-square table, a bonus or penalty for each of the 64 squares — knights are paid extra in the centre, kings are paid to hide in the corner until the endgame. Search: judgement is checked against the future — look a few moves ahead, assume your opponent answers as unkindly as possible (that is minimax), and let the positions at the horizon be graded by the first two parts. What makes Zenith Chess an experiment rather than just an engine is that it is handed the shape of that answer — values exist, tables exist, search exists — and none of the numbers. The prices, and every square’s bonus, are what it has to learn.

The one idea — 197 numbers and a book

The program’s entire opinion of chess is 197 learned numbers. Five are material values — pawn, knight, bishop, rook, queen (the king is priceless and priced at 0; he is never traded). The other 192 are piece-square tables: six tables of 32 weights, one per piece type including the king — the king has no price, but he is allowed to learn where he likes to stand. Thirty-two weights cover 64 squares because the board’s left and right halves are mirrored (the a-file and h-file share a weight, b with g, c with f, d with e) and one table serves both colours, flipped top-to-bottom for Black. To grade a board, the program adds up, for every piece, its value plus its square’s bonus, White minus Black, and squashes the sum into a verdict between −1 and +1:

verdict = tanh( 0.12 × Σ side × ( value[piece] + table[piece][square] ) ) At the start of the program’s life every value is 1.0 and every table entry is 0, so a queen, a pawn, and a knight contribute identically, and the verdict on the starting position — and on any position with equal armies — is exactly 0. Note what is not in the sum: there is no mobility term, no count of moves or attacked squares. Unlike its Othello sibling, this judge sees only wood and placement. (Mobility matters enormously in this program — but as a referee, not a judge. That comes below.)

Beside the numbers sits the family heirloom: an opening book. Positions from the first 14 plies of every learned game are stored under a key (the board, the side to move, and the castling rights, written as a string), with a visit count and the running total of how those games ended. When the program grades an opening position it has seen at least 3 times, in the first 8 moves of the game, it blends the book’s track record into the verdict — by a sixth after 3 visits, rising to a cap of 42% for a position seen 8 or more times. The numbers are its judgement; the book is its memory of specific places it has actually been.

How it learns — one game, one small correction, and a referee with a whistle

When a game ends, the program replays it backwards, exactly as its sibling does. The result becomes the target — +1 if White won, −1 if Black won — and each position on the path is corrected toward a blend of that outcome and the next position’s verdict (0.78 of the carried target, 0.22 of the position’s own grade). Every piece standing on a visited board nudges its value, and its square’s table entry, by a small step:

weight += learning-rate × error × (1 − verdict²) × 0.12 × scale The learning rate starts at 0.11 per game, stepping down to 0.075 after 100 games learned and 0.045 after 350. The scale compensates for headcount — a queen appears once per side, pawns eight times, so per-occurrence steps are weighted pawn ×0.45, knight ×0.95, bishop ×1.0, rook ×1.05, queen ×1.55, so rare pieces learn about as fast as common ones; table entries step at ×0.55. Values are clamped to [0.2, 12], table entries to [−2.5, +2.5]. The target ignores the margin, as in the sibling: mate in 12 and mate in 60 are the same +1.

Chess, though, refuses to end on schedule — so who declares the results of the training games? The program plays its silent self-play games under a 100-ply cap (50 moves per side), and if nobody has been checkmated by then, a referee adjudicates by mobility dominance: count each side’s legal moves, and if one side has at least 7 more than the other, that side is declared the winner — by a fraction, (the gap ÷ 20), capped at 0.88, so a dominance verdict teaches more gently than a mate. If the gap is smaller, the game is a draw — and here this program differs from its sibling in a way the audit wants on the record: a drawn game teaches nothing at all. No correction, and not even book entries; the game counter simply ticks up by one. The same goes for draws in your games. Roughly a third of everything this brain ever plays is a draw — a third of its life is experience it is built to throw away.

Who teaches it is your choice, as in the sibling. The trainer buttons (+25 / +100 / +250) and the watched Play Itself arena run self-play: both sides driven by the current numbers at 1-ply, with deliberate random exploration mixed in — about half its moves are coin flips at the very start, decaying toward a floor of 14% as it gains experience. In vs Computer and 2-Player modes, the Learn from Us toggle (on by default) makes every finished game a lesson through the identical learning function into the identical store — “us” is literal here too, two humans at one keyboard included. And it persists: the brain — values, tables, book, counters, curve — is saved to the browser’s local storage under the key zenith_chess_brain_v1, exportable and importable as JSON, with “Wipe to 1.0” as the honest reset. When the book passes 2,200 positions, saving trims it to the 1,600 most-visited: the brain forgets rare openings on purpose, so it stays small enough to keep.

The receipts — what the audit ran

The rules are exact — perft does not negotiate

The standard way to prove a chess move generator is perft: count every leaf of the move tree to a fixed depth from a test position, and compare against the counts the chess-programming world has verified to the last node. The engine was extracted verbatim (the script passes node --check) and run against the four canonical positions. Every count matched exactly — 53 audit checks in all, none failing:

The named cases behind those numbers were also checked one by one. Castling is refused through an attacked square and onto an attacked square, on both wings; it lands the king and rook correctly and revokes rights properly — a rook captured on its home square strips that side’s right, as does any king move. The en-passant capture that would expose the capturing side’s king to a rook along the rank is correctly illegal (the generator applies each move and tests its own king, so the rarest pin in chess falls out of the general rule), and the same capture is legal in the control position without the rook. Fool’s mate is mate; a crafted stalemate is a stalemate. Promotions are generated in all four flavours, underpromotions included. In played games all three draw rules are present: threefold repetition, insufficient material, and the fifty-move rule, which fires at 100 halfmoves — with checkmate on the move still winning, because mate is checked first.

The learning is real — and its teacher is mostly a referee

  • Tabula rasa, verified. A fresh brain’s values are exactly [pawn 1.0, knight 1.0, bishop 1.0, rook 1.0, queen 1.0] and all 224 table entries (six tables of 32, plus an unused seventh) are exactly 0. On test positions with bare kings and one extra piece, a queen, a knight, and a pawn each grade at exactly 1.0 — the fresh brain literally cannot tell them apart.
  • One game teaches a little, measurably. A single won king-and-queen position, learned once, raises the queen’s value 1.0 → 1.0178 and her table entry on the queen’s square, and touches nothing else. A perfectly symmetric position taught with a “White won” target moves no parameter at all (largest change: 0.0) — every White update is cancelled by its Black mirror twin in the shared tables, so this brain learns only from asymmetry, from the ways games stop being mirror images.
  • The book keeps its rules. After one learned 20-ply game the book holds exactly 14 positions — the first-14-plies rule, to the visit. It is not consulted at all before 3 visits; at 3 visits the blend toward the recorded outcome matches the code’s formula to nine decimal places.
  • 600 games: the order arrives. After 600 seeded self-play games (about two seconds of compute), normalized the way the game’s own display does it, pawn = 1.00: knight 1.62, bishop 1.87, rook 3.41, queen 3.50. The full conventional ordering — every piece in its textbook place — from all-ones and self-play alone. Of those 600 games, 14 ended in checkmate, 411 were decided by the mobility referee, and 175 were draws that taught nothing.
  • 5,000 games: the order holds, the distances don’t. After 5,000 games (about ten seconds), the table reads:

Bishop clearly above knight, rook and queen clearly above the minors — and the queen essentially tied with the rook, the two swapping places between snapshots (at 4,000 games queen 4.64, rook 4.29; at 5,000, rook 4.24, queen 4.11). Only 59 of the 5,000 games — 1.2% — ended in checkmate; 3,366 were mobility verdicts and 1,575 were untaught draws. The signal that most separates a queen from a rook in real chess is mating power, and in this classroom mates almost never happen, so the distinction is barely taught. These are not converged constants, either: they are a running average at a still-warm learning rate, and the knight’s value wandered between 1.43 and 2.26 across the snapshots on the way.

  • The trained numbers play differently. On a test position where the white queen can take a pawn on c5 or a knight on e5, the untrained brain grades both captures’ outcomes at exactly 0.000 — a coin flip between gold and bait. The 5,000-game brain grades taking the pawn +2.043 and taking the knight +2.374, and its playing self takes the knight, at both 1-ply and 2-ply. It also finds a mate in one (rook to the back rank) at its shallowest setting, as it should: the 1-ply player checks for immediate mate explicitly.
  • The tables learn geography — some of it. The pawn table at 5,000 games shows the advancement gradient the tribute promises: a pawn on the seventh rank carries +0.28 of table, on the sixth +0.13, at home −0.25 — pawns really do gain value advancing (at 600 games this was still noise). Two of the pawn table’s eight rows are exactly 0 and always will be: pawns never sit on the promotion rank or their own back rank, so those rows are unteachable by construction. The knight’s promised centre preference has not landed — centre +0.03 vs rim −0.01 at 600 games, centre −0.00 vs rim +0.01 at 5,000: a coin still spinning, two orders of magnitude below the material values. And the queen’s table has learned something true about its classroom and false about chess: her largest bonus, +0.39, is for staying home — in games where both sides grab greedily, queens that ventured out got traded, so survival correlates with staying put, and the table learned the correlation.
  • The benchmark saturates. Against the baseline bot, in 40-game batches under one fixed-seed protocol: untrained 79%, 300 games 99%, 600 games 95%, 5,000 games 100%. Read that honestly: the floor is high because the opponent is weak and even an all-ones table counts material, the ceiling is 100, and 40-game batches wobble by several points — the 300-game brain “beating” the 600-game brain is noise, not regression. The values table above is the real learning curve; the benchmark mostly proves the graduate can beat the playground’s designated loser, which — to be fair — the newborn nearly can too.
  • Persistence. A save/load round-trip through local storage returns learned values intact, and the audit watched a stuffed 2,300-entry book save down to exactly the 1,600 most-visited, as coded.

One honest boundary on method: the self-play proof ran the shipped runSilentSelfPlayGame(), learnFromTrajectory(), and runBenchmark() themselves — rules, learning, and measurement are function-proven end to end. The wiring that calls them from buttons, toggles, and game-end events (the trainer chunks, Learn from Us, the watched arena, the human-game draw checks) was read in the shipped source, not clicked headlessly; there is no browser in the audit bench. What the page claims the buttons do matches the functions they call — and the audit verified there are exactly two callers of the learning function: silent self-play, and the game-ended handler that serves every other mode.

The three players — one judge, three depths of thought

The depth selector changes how far ahead the computer looks, never what it values. 1-Ply (Pure Table) tries each legal move, grades the resulting board with the numbers and the book, and takes the best — with one piece of explicit craft: it checks whether any move is immediate checkmate and scores it above everything. 2-Ply (Tactical) and 3-Ply (Tournament) run a real alpha-beta minimax: my move, your best reply, (at 3-ply) my best answer — each side choosing against the other, the learned numbers grading the positions where the search runs out of depth. A mate found inside the search is worth 1.8, minus a whisker per ply, so faster mates are preferred; stalemate and the draw rules score 0. Moves are tried in an order set by the brain’s own current values — captures of the (currently) most valuable victim first, the classic MVV-LVA ordering, with promotions given a fixed head start — which is what makes the pruning bite, and means the search itself sharpens as the values learn. Inside the search the opening book is switched off; it speaks only at the shallowest level and in the eval readout. The benchmark always fields the 1-ply player, so the curve measures the raw numbers undistracted by search — the right thing to measure if the question is “are the numbers learning?”

How it’s built — the architecture as found

Zenith Chess is a single HTML file — 75,201 bytes, one script block of 52,597 characters, no libraries, and no external references of any kind: the only link in the file points back to this room’s index page. The sound effects are synthesized live with Web Audio oscillators, so there are no audio files either. It would run off a USB stick in a Faraday cage — or, in the spirit of the tribute, off a cassette in a farmhouse.

One closure, three layers

The script is one strict-mode closure with clean seams. The rules engine is pure functions over a 64-cell board — generateLegalMoves, applyMove (which returns a new position rather than mutating), isSquareAttacked, inCheck — and knows nothing about learning or drawing; it is fast enough that the audit’s 197,281-leaf perft took about a tenth of a second. The brain — evalRaw, evalTanh, alphaBeta, chooseAIMove, learnFromTrajectory, the silent self-play driver and the benchmark — knows nothing about the screen; that separation is precisely what let this audit lift the whole mind out of the file and run 5,000 games through it headlessly. The stage — a 560×560 canvas board, three themes (Modern Tournament, Heathkit H-89 green phosphor, Zenith Z-100 amber), the piece-value bars normalized to pawn = 1.00 exactly as this lesson reports them, the 8×8 table heatmap (each cell the mirrored table’s real value, ×100), and the strength chart — only renders what the other two layers decide. The “Show PST on Board” overlay paints the same numbers onto the board itself. The stage badge names the brain’s seniority by games learned — Novice Explorer at 5, Developing Sense at 40, Tactical Learner at 150, Master Value Table at 400.

What the training buttons actually do

The +25 / +100 / +250 buttons run silent self-play in chunks (5, 10, or 15 games at a time, yielding to the browser between chunks so the page never freezes), slipping in a quick 14-game benchmark roughly every 50 games so the curve grows while you watch. The Benchmark button runs 16 games. The watched Play Itself mode plays at the speed you pick, at 1-ply with its own slightly gentler exploration rate, learns at each game’s end, and deals the next game automatically — with one difference from the silent trainer: a mobility verdict there counts as a full ±1 win, not the silent referee’s capped fraction. It is the slowest teacher and the best show.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: this game was built by Gemini Neon (Google Antigravity) at Steven Powell’s direction — the chess sequel to Henry Powell’s learning-table Othello program — and was integrated unchanged; the single later adjustment is Steven’s own (21 November 2026): the self-play speed options, originally 520/180/45 ms a move, are now 2,500/1,500/1,000 ms (Slow/Steady/Brisk), so a watched game can actually be followed. The change touches only those three option values — no rules, search, or learning code differs from the delivered file, and every measurement on this page still describes the shipped engine. Its in-page tribute text is its own voice — warm, and mostly accurate. This box is where the collection’s audit differs from that voice, precisely and without mockery: Gemini built a real thing, and the job here is to say exactly what it is.

The values start at 1.0 — the architecture doesn’t start at zero. The boldest claim — all pieces equal, all tables zero, “zero hardcoded chess values” — is, for the learned numbers themselves, literally true, and the audit verified it to the digit. But a blank table is not a blank mind. A human chose that squares come in mirrored families of 32 (so the machine can never believe the a-file differs from the h-file), chose the tanh squash and its 0.12 scale, chose the per-piece learning-rate scales, chose that captures are ordered by victim value in the search with promotions given a hardcoded +5 head start, and chose that a mate is worth 1.8. The program is told what kinds of things might matter; it is not told which way they point. A tabula rasa with good bones — the same honest shape as its sibling’s.

The “Random Bot” is not random, and its judge knows the textbook. The stat pill says “vs Random Bot”; the chart and log say “Random-Capturer Bot,” and the code sides with the chart: the baseline prefers a random capture 60% of the time and a random move otherwise — a meaningfully stronger opponent than pure chance. But when a benchmark game hits the 110-ply cap unfinished, it is adjudicated by conventional material — hardcoded 1/3/3/5/9 values with a 4-point threshold. The same textbook numbers drive the captured-pieces display beside the board. They never touch the brain; they live in the measuring instrument and the furniture. The student is discovering values that its examiner already knows.

Most of its education is refereeing, not chess. In 5,000 self-play games: 59 checkmates, 3,366 mobility verdicts, 1,575 draws that taught nothing. The 100-ply cap means most training games are opening-and-middlegame sketches that never reach an endgame, and their “results” are mostly a count of who had more legal moves when the whistle blew — a fractional, capped signal at that. This is the main reason the learned values are compressed (queen ≈ rook ≈ 4 pawns, not 9): the experiences that would teach the queen’s true distance from the rook are precisely the ones this classroom rarely has. It also means the values are best read as what wins mobility-capped games between two greedy 1-ply players — a real dialect of chess, honestly learned, and not the whole language.

Draws teach nothing — a regression from the sibling. Zenith Othello’s drawn games pull every visited position toward 0; they are lessons in what equality looks like. Here a draw — a third of all games — only increments the counter. Its opening positions don’t even reach the book. Whatever this brain believes about drawn positions, it believes by accident of the wins and losses around them.

The AI never underpromotes. The rules engine generates all four promotions — perft position 4, thick with them, matches to the node — and a human at the board gets the full four-way choice dialog. But the computer’s move generation, in play, search, self-play, and benchmark alike, only ever promotes to a queen. The rare position where a knight promotion wins and a queen promotion stalemates is one this AI will never find, because it never looks.

Three small rules tightenings. The fifty-move rule ends the game automatically at 100 halfmoves; the laws of chess make it claimable there and automatic only at 150 — mate on the move still wins, since mate is checked first. Threefold repetition is counted on a position key that omits the en-passant square, so two positions differing only in whether an en-passant capture exists count as the same position. And the insufficient-material test — no pawns, rooks, or queens, and at most one minor piece on the whole board — misses exactly one dead position: king and bishop against king and bishop with both bishops on the same colour, which the function plays on (the audit built it: bishops on c8 and b1, both light-squared, and the game continues). Everything else the audit tested — and it tested the catalogue — is exact.

“TD(λ)” names the family, not the textbook algorithm. As with the sibling, the update is genuinely temporal-difference in spirit, but the mechanism is a single backward sweep carrying one blended target (0.78 carried, 0.22 the position’s own verdict) — not per-weight eligibility traces. Simpler, cheaper, and it demonstrably learns.

The book is an opening book only — and a forgetful one. Only the first 14 plies are recorded; it is consulted only in the first 8 fullmoves, only after 3 visits, capped at a 42% blend, and switched off entirely inside the 2-ply and 3-ply search. In memory it grows without bound during play (23,210 states after the audit’s 5,000 games); only saving prunes it, to the 1,600 most-visited. Small print on the curve, too: batches are small (16 by the button, 14 inside the trainer), the chart keeps the last 26 points and its horizontal axis is benchmark count, not games — and the Hint button always searches at least 2-ply, so your advisor is a little stronger than the opponent you configured. One kindness to record: the stage badge in the page’s static HTML reads “Tabula Rasa (All Pieces = 1.0),” and the script’s own first-stage label says exactly the same — the audit checked, because the sibling’s badge didn’t match. This one does.
