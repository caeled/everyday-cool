Stack-a-Bot — the lesson

Stack-a-Bot is the smallest complete game in this collection, and that is exactly why it has a lesson. Crates of robot parts fall down a 10 × 20 well; you steer and turn them; filled rows clear; and every two cleared rows lights another part of the robot waiting on the bench — antenna, head, eyes, body, arms, legs — until twelve rows finish the robot and a fresh one rolls in. It was built by Muse in the main chat, on 15 November 2026, at Steven Powell’s request, as this room’s own teaching example: an original Everyday Cool workshop with no upstream copy. The room page already shows its skeleton in a little table — five ingredients, each with the function it lives in. This page is the audit of that table and of everything under it. The rules file is only 3,564 bytes, small enough to check exhaustively, and the audit did: the pure logic was run through 43 checks, the shipped screen-and-keys driver was run headlessly through 17 more, and the game’s own test suite passes on top. Where the gentle version differs from the tournament game it descends from, the last section says so.

The one idea — a game is a world remembered as data

Strip any game down and three things remain. A world, remembered as data. A set of rules, written as functions that answer questions about that world. And a loop that keeps asking: what did the player do, what do the rules allow, what does the world look like now — many times a second. Stack-a-Bot’s whole world is one value: a list of 20 rows, each a list of 10 squares. A square is either null (empty) or a letter — 'I', 'O', 'T' and so on — naming the crate settled there. That is the entire memory of the game. The falling crate is four more squares held to one side; the score, the row count, and the robot’s lit parts are three numbers. Everything you see on the canvas is drawn fresh from those values, every frame. When a game is built this way — world as data, drawing as a readout — the rules can be lifted out and tested without a screen at all, which is precisely how this audit was able to run them.

The split is deliberate and it is the first thing worth stealing. The rules live in stackabot.js, which never touches the page: no canvas, no keys, no clock. The screen, keys, and clock live in app.js, which contains no rules at all — it asks the rules questions and draws the answers. Two files, one seam, and each side can be checked without the other.

The pieces and the bag — fairness, dealt seven at a time

How a falling-block game deals its pieces decides whether it feels fair. Pure random dealing has no memory: it can deal you four S-crates in a row and then starve you of the long I-crate for thirty pieces, and it will, eventually. Stack-a-Bot does what the modern tournament game does — it deals from a 7-bag. All seven types go into a bag, the bag is shuffled (a Fisher–Yates shuffle, driven by an injected random source), and the pieces are dealt out until the bag is empty; then a fresh bag is shuffled. The audit verified 7,000 consecutive bags: every one a true permutation of the seven types, every type dealt exactly 7,000 times. The consequence is a promise the player can lean on: no drought can exceed 12 pieces. The worst case is a type dealt first from one bag and last from the next — and over a 49,000-piece dealt stream, the audit measured the maximum gap at exactly 12, the theoretical bound, occurring in practice. The opposite mercy exists too: a type can arrive twice in a row across the seam between bags. Tensor-Tris, across the room, runs the same 7-bag — its lesson names it there as well; here it is not a claim, it is a counted fact.

One honest split in how the bag gets its randomness. The logic accepts any random source: the file ships mulberry32, a small seeded generator, and with a fixed seed the entire dealing sequence reproduces exactly — which is how the game’s own tests, and this audit, can prove statements about it. The page you play, though, shuffles with the browser’s Math.random, unseeded. So the rules are provable and replayable; a particular game you played last night is not. It cannot be re dealt, only remembered.

Rotation — arithmetic, with a small set of elbows

There are no stored rotation states and no rotation tables. A piece’s shape at any turning is computed on demand: start from the base list of four squares and apply rotateCells() once per quarter-turn, inside the piece’s own bounding box — 4 × 4 for the I, 2 × 2 for the O, 3 × 3 for the rest. Two consequences fall out of that choice, and the audit measured both. First, the O crate’s rotation is a genuine no-op: its four squares map onto themselves at every turning, so turning it changes nothing but the counter. Second, the I crate’s horizontal form sits on row 1 of its box in one orientation and row 2 in the opposite one — the long crate does not spin around its centre, it tumbles inside its box.

When a rotation would collide, the driver does not give up immediately. It tries the turned piece at five horizontal offsets, in this order: 0, −1, +1, −2, +2 — a small set of elbows, all sideways. There are no vertical kicks: nothing ever nudges a piece up or down to make a turn fit. The audit drove the shipped driver headlessly through the three walls of the world:

  • Left wall. A vertical I standing in column 0 turns successfully: the un-kicked rotation would poke two squares through the wall, kicks −1 and +1 still clip, and the +2 kick lands it legally at x = 0, fully turned. The elbows earn their keep.
  • Right wall. A T at the right edge turned and ended at rot 1, x = 7, with no square outside the well and none overlapping — the first working offset wins and the piece stays in bounds.
  • The floor. An I lying flat on the floor cannot turn at all. Its vertical form needs rows the floor occupies, and every sideways elbow keeps the same rows — so the rotation is refused, cleanly: the piece stays exactly as it was, unturned, unharmed, still playable. No clip through the floor, no overlap, no half-turned state.
  • Boxed in. Surrounded by settled crates with no elbow room, rotation is likewise refused and the piece is left byte-for-byte unchanged.

That refusal behaviour is the design’s character: a move in this game is all-or-nothing. tryMove() computes the turned shape, asks collides(), and only then commits — so an illegal position is never drawn, not even for one frame.

Gravity, locking, and the top of the stack

The clock pulls the falling crate down one row every dropInterval(level) milliseconds — 800 at level 1, shrinking by 70 per level to a floor of 80 ms, reached at level 12 and never faster. Your level is one per ten rows cleared: level = ⌊rows / 10⌋ + 1. Pressing ↓ moves the crate down a row immediately (and pays a point, below), but it does not lock it — locking belongs to the clock. The piece locks at the exact moment a gravity step tries to move it down and collides() says no. There is no lock delay: no grace period to slide or turn a landed crate along the floor, as tournament rules allow. When the clock says land, it lands — the shipped driver was run to confirm a crate resting on the floor merges into the board on its next step and the next crate spawns. Space, the hard drop, is the one exception to patience: it falls the crate straight to its resting row and locks it in the same instant.

Locking writes the crate’s letter into the board — only the squares inside the well; a crate that locks partly above the top contributes only its in-bounds squares — and then the board is checked for full rows. And the game’s end is narrower than most players assume: the stack reaching the top does not, by itself, end anything. The game ends at one moment only — when a newly spawned crate already collides at its spawn position, in the centre columns at the very top (the I spawns one row higher, at y = −1, since its shape lives on the second row of its box). Fill the sides to the ceiling and play continues; block the spawn and it’s over. The audit blocked the spawn zone with settled crates, called the shipped spawn, and watched the state flip to game-over.

Rows, collapse, and the robot — the arithmetic of the bench

A row clears when all ten squares hold letters. clearLines() works by subtraction: it keeps every row that still has an empty square, counts what vanished, and pads the top with fresh empty rows. Nothing is pushed or shifted cell by cell — the rows above a clear fall by exactly the number of cleared rows because they are simply re-stacked from the bottom. The audit marked single cells above a full row and watched each land precisely one row lower after a single clear.

Scripted constructions reconciled to the crate:

  • Five O crates side by side fill the bottom two rows exactly → 2 rows cleared, board empty.
  • Ten vertical I crates fill the bottom four rows → 4 rows cleared, board empty.
  • Through the real driver: an O crate dropped into the last gap of a prepared row → rows = 1, score = 100; a second → rows = 2, and robotProgress() reports the antenna lit.

The robot is pure arithmetic on the row count. LINES_PER_PART = 2; six parts — Antenna, Head, Eyes, Body, Arms, Legs, always in that order — make LINES_PER_ROBOT = 12. At 11 rows: five parts lit, the legs next. At 12: one whole robot, parts reset to zero, and the count toward the next robot begins. At 25 rows the function reports 2 robots built and none lit on the third — the panel on the page is a straight readout of this function, not a separate tally that could drift from it.

The score — points exist, and the robot is the real one

There is a score, and it is the classic table, multiplied by your level:

Rows cleared at once1234
Points × level100300500800

Verified cell by cell: scoreFor(4, 3) = 2,400, and clearing four rows is worth more than four singles — the room page’s advice to “leave a well open for the long crate” is backed by the numbers. Two smaller payments ride along: each row of soft drop (holding ↓) pays 1 point, each row of hard drop pays 2 — a hard drop of an S crate on an empty well paid exactly 36 points for 18 rows of fall, counted by the driver itself. And one quirk, found by playing the boundary: the level used to price a clear is computed after the new rows are added. Your tenth row is therefore paid at the level-2 rate — the audit’s tenth row scored 200, not 100. A generous rounding, consistently applied: the game congratulates you at the new rate the moment you earn it.

But the points are the small score. The score the room is named for is the robot: parts lighting on the bench every two rows, a whole machine every twelve, the count of finished robots read out at game over. Points evaporate when you close the tab; the bench is what you were building.

The receipts — what the audit ran

  • 43 logic checks, all passing, against stackabot.js itself: the piece set enumerated; rotation cycles (four turns = identity, for every piece; the O invariant); collides() proven pure and correct at the floor, both walls, settled crates, and above the top (squares above the well are legal — that is what lets crates spawn); merge() proven to return a new board without touching the old; clears of 1, 2, and 4 rows with collapse distances exact; the full score, level, and interval tables; the robot function at 0, 1, 2, 11, 12, and 25 rows; 7,000 bags verified as permutations with the drought bound measured at 12.
  • 17 driver checks, all passing, running the shipped app.js headlessly with a stub screen: spawn centring; hard-drop distance and payment; rotation refused at the floor; rotation saved by the +2 kick at the left wall; rotation refused when boxed in; locking on the gravity step; line clears, part lighting, and the level-boundary payment through real play functions; soft-drop payment; and game over on a blocked spawn.
  • The game’s own suite — node test.cjs, shipped in the room folder — passes: “pieces, rotation, collision, merging, line clears, scoring, levels, the 7-bag, and the bench robot.” The workshop page tells visitors to run it after bending the code; the invitation is honest, the tests are real.

One boundary on method, stated the way this collection states it: the driver audit ran app.js’s own functions in a harness with a fake canvas and a seeded Math.random — the logic executed is the shipped logic, keystroke for keystroke, but no real browser rendered a frame. What the player sees is a straight drawing of the state these functions produce; the drawing code was read, and it holds no rules of its own.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: Stack-a-Bot was built by Muse at Steven Powell’s direction as this room’s teaching example — the collection’s own work, not a guest build, and this lesson is its audit. It is a gentle falling-block game, and its gentleness is a list of deliberate omissions from the tournament form. Here is the list, so nobody mistakes the teaching model for the full machine:

No lock delay. Tournament rules give a landed piece a short grace window to slide and turn before it sets. Here, the gravity step that fails is the lock — a crate that touches down is committed. High-level floor play (sliding a crate under an overhang at the last instant) is therefore impossible; the well is more forgiving in every other way, and this is the price.

Sideways elbows only. The rotation kicks are five horizontal offsets, with no floor or wall kicks in the vertical direction and nothing like the tournament game’s full kick tables. The measured consequence stands above: a long crate lying on the floor cannot stand up. Plan the I’s landing before it lands.

Rotation inside a box. Pieces tumble within their bounding squares rather than around a centre pivot, which is why the I’s horizontal form changes row between its two flat orientations, and why the O’s “rotation” is pure ceremony. Simpler arithmetic, slightly different feel — named here because a player moving between this and Tensor-Tris or the tournament game will feel it in the first minute.

The clock discards time. At most one gravity step per animation frame, with the remainder thrown away. On a smooth screen this is invisible; on a struggling one the game politely slows instead of lurching. Speed is therefore “80 ms per row at the floor of the table” only on hardware that can hold the frame rate.

The played game cannot be replayed. The logic is seedable and its proofs are seeded, but the page deals from Math.random. There is no seed on the screen, no replay, no undo — and no hold queue, no second preview beyond the one next crate, and no T-spin or combo bonuses. The score table is four numbers and two drop payments; that is the whole economy.

Above the top, squares quietly vanish. A crate that locks with squares still above the well loses those squares — merge() writes only in-bounds cells. This is standard in spirit (the well is the world), but combined with spawn-only game over it means a stack can poke through the ceiling and play on, as long as the centre stays clear. The end, when it comes, comes at the spawn — exactly as the receipts section describes.
