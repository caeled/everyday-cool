Games are
little worlds.

Every game is a small world with rules you can learn by playing. This room has three things: a shelf of honest places to play online, a game that runs right here on the page, and a short workshop on how games are built, using that very game as the example.

Nothing here needs a download or an account. The shelves below leave this site; the game in the middle never does.

01 / THE GAME SHELVES

Legit places to play online.

“Legit” here means the games are put online by the people who own them or have the rights to share them: libraries, the game companies themselves, and long-running game shelves. No downloads, no installers. Some shelves carry ads, and a few keep their best games behind a paid shelf; each card says so plainly, so there are no surprises.

The Internet Arcade

Run by the Internet Archive, a nonprofit library

Classic coin-op arcade cabinets from the 1970s to the 1990s, running in your browser exactly as the library preserved them. Press 5 to feed the machine a virtual coin, 1 to start.

Free · No account · No ads

MS-DOS Games Library

Run by the Internet Archive, a nonprofit library

Thousands of classic PC games from the floppy-disk years, playable in the browser. Oregon Trail included, dysentery and all.

Free · No account · No ads

AARP Games

Run by AARP

Crosswords, solitaire, mahjong, word games, and daily puzzles, made to be picked up for ten quiet minutes. You do not need to be a member to play most of them.

Free · No account for most games · Has ads

Lichess

Run by a nonprofit, built by volunteers

Chess against people around the world or against the computer, plus puzzles and lessons. Completely free, with no ads at all, forever; that is the project's promise. An account is optional and only saves your games.

Free · No ads · Account optional

Pogo

Run by Electronic Arts (EA)

The long-running home of casual classics: bingo, word whomp, card games, and puzzle rooms people have played in for over twenty years.

Free with ads · Some extras need a paid Club Pogo account

MSN Play

Run by Microsoft

Solitaire, mahjong, bubble shooters, and word games in the browser, the modern home of the old MSN Games shelf.

Free · Has ads

New York Times Games

Run by The New York Times

Wordle, Connections, the Mini crossword, and Strands. A few daily plays are free; the full shelf, including the big crossword, is a subscription.

Some free daily · Rest by subscription

Poki

A game shelf company in Amsterdam

A big, bright shelf of modern browser games: racing, puzzles, two-player games, and quick five-minute diversions. Everything runs in the page.

Free · Has ads · No account

CrazyGames

A game shelf company in Belgium

Another big browser shelf, strong on action, driving, and .io games you play live against other people.

Free · Has ads · Account optional

Kongregate

A long-running game shelf, home of indie classics

Where a generation of small-studio and hobbyist games first found players. Many of the shelf's classics still run in the browser.

Free · Has ads · Free account saves progress in some games

itch.io: games in the browser

A marketplace run for independent game makers

Small, strange, heartfelt games by independent makers, filtered to the ones that play right in your browser. It is also a shop, so look at each page: free games say so, and makers sometimes ask a few dollars for a download.

Many free · Each page states its price · Account only to buy or comment

A word on “free emulator” sites you may find on your own: many of them offer games nobody gave them permission to share. The two Internet Archive shelves above are the honest version of the same idea: a library doing preservation, in the open.

02 / PLAY IT RIGHT HERE

Stack-a-Bot.

Crates of robot parts fall from the top of the bench. Steer and turn each crate as it falls. Fill a whole row and the row clears. Every two rows you clear lights another part of the robot waiting on the bench: antenna, head, eyes, body, arms, legs. Twelve rows finishes the robot, and a fresh one rolls in. The stack reaching the top ends the game.

02B / ANOTHER FALLING-BLOCK BENCH

Tensor-Tris.

The falling blocks are back, wearing a different job. In Tensor-Tris the pieces are AI tokens and the well is a context window: pack the tokens in tight, fill a whole row to clear it, and mind what the window can hold. Same falling-block bones as Stack-a-Bot, a different lesson on top — this one is about fitting a lot of pieces into a limited space without wasting any of it. It plays on its own page, so the bench here stays clear.

02C / WORK BESIDE YOURSELF

Echo Shift.

Program a robot's shift — a short list of moves — and run it. Then rewind: your program becomes an echo, a recording of yourself that replays from the start while you program a new shift beside it. One of you holds the switch so the door stays open; the other pushes the cargo home. The echo never thinks — it repeats, exactly — which is the whole lesson: the same list from the same start gives the same world, every time. Two stations ship, both solvable within their beat and echo limits; the stored solutions were verified by simulation in the audit. Built by ChatGPT at Steven Powell's direction.

Play Echo Shift →

02C / THE LEARNING BENCH

Zenith Othello.

In the early 1980s, Steven's father Henry wrote an Othello program for the Heathkit Zenith computer father and son built together on the kitchen table. Henry's program kept a table that let it get better the more it was played — and teenage Steven proposed it play itself to learn faster. This is that machine, rebuilt: a Reversi board whose brain starts as eleven exact zeros and learns from every game — yours, a friend's, or its own — with the learning drawn on the board as a living heatmap. Play it, teach it, or set it playing itself and watch the corners catch fire. Gemini Neon built the recreation at Steven's direction, from his description of his father's program; the lesson beside it audits the learning and tells the story whole.

02D / THE LEARNING BENCH, BIGGER BOARD

Zenith Chess.

The Othello machine's younger, larger sibling — commissioned on a thought Steven couldn't shake: DeepMind's AlphaZero learned chess from nothing, by self-play, and it worked at planetary scale. This one starts smaller and honester: every piece worth exactly 1.0, every square table flat zero, and nothing to learn from but the outcomes of games — yours, a friend's, or its own. Its rules are exact (the audit counted 197,281 positions to depth four and matched the world's verified numbers to the node), and its values genuinely move: after 5,000 self-played games the full textbook ordering — pawn, knight, bishop, rook, queen — had risen out of the ones, though its queen still sits near its rook, because so few of its games end in mate that mating power is barely taught. Gemini Neon built it at Steven's direction; the lesson beside it prints the learned numbers and names what its classroom never teaches.

03 / THE BUILD-A-GAME WORKSHOP

Every game is a loop.

Strip any game down and you find the same heartbeat, beating many times a second:

  1. Listen. What did the player just do? A key, a tap, a click.
  2. Update. Move the pieces by the rules. Gravity pulls. Walls say no.
  3. Draw. Show the world as it now stands, then start again.

Stack-a-Bot is small enough to hold in your head, so here is its whole skeleton. Five ingredients, and where each one lives in the code on this page:

The five ingredients of Stack-a-Bot
IngredientWhat it isWhere it lives
The worldA grid, 10 squares wide and 20 tall, remembered as a list of rows. Empty squares are blank; settled crates carry their color.emptyBoard() in stackabot.js
The piecesSeven shapes, each just a list of four squares. Turning a piece is arithmetic on that list, not a new drawing.PIECES and rotateCells()
The rulesOne question, asked before every move: would these squares land inside the world, on empty ground? If not, the move does not happen.collides()
The clockA timer that pulls the piece down one square at a time, faster as your level rises. Games call this the game loop.tick() in app.js
The scoreNumbers the game keeps: score, rows, level, and the robot's lit parts. Keeping score is just remembering, honestly.scoreFor() and robotProgress()

The loop itself, in the same shape as the real code:

function tick(now) {
  listen();              // keys and buttons set the player's wish
  if (timeToFall(now))   // the clock says: gravity's turn
    moveDownOrLock();    // update the world by the rules
  draw();                // show the world as it now stands
  requestAnimationFrame(tick);  // and beat again
}

Change one thing

The fastest way to learn a game is to bend it. The code for this page is in the workshop ZIP below, and each of these is a one-line change:

  • Find 800 in dropInterval() and make it 1200. The whole game breathes slower.
  • In SCORES, change the 800 to 2000. Now a four-row clear is a treasure, and players will play differently. That is game design: the score teaches people what to want.
  • Change one piece's color in PIECES. Small, but it is yours now.

Then run node test.cjs in the folder. The tests check the rules still hold; when a change breaks one, the test tells you which rule and roughly where. That back-and-forth, change and check, is most of how games (and everything else in this collection) get built.

04 / KEEP GOING

Build your own little world.

  • MDN: 2D breakout game in pure JavaScript — Mozilla's step-by-step tutorial builds a whole paddle-and-bricks game, one idea at a time. The best next step after this room.
  • p5.js — a friendly drawing-first way to code games and animations in the browser, with a big library of examples to remix.
  • freeCodeCamp: game development articles — free, long, patient walkthroughs that build real games in JavaScript and Python.
  • Java for Young Robot Builders — when you want your code to move a robot and not just a crate (included in the full collection).
  • Secret Squares — the codes and QR room, for when the puzzle itch points the other way (included in the full collection).
  • Numbers in the Wild — the math room; scores, grids, and patterns live there (included in the full collection).
