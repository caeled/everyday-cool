Games Room · The back door to Echo Shift

Echo Shift — the lesson

Echo Shift is a small game about working with yourself. You program a robot’s shift — a short list of moves, one per beat. The shift runs. Then you press Rewind & keep echo, and that same list becomes a second robot: an echo that replays your recorded moves from the start while you program a new shift of your own. Somewhere in the station a crate has to reach the delivery tile, a switch holds a door open while somebody stands on it, and the crate can only be pushed, never pulled — so the natural plan is the one the game is named for: send your past self to hold the door, and walk the cargo through yourself. Echo Shift was built by ChatGPT at Steven Powell’s direction. Everything — both stations, the rules, the renderer — lives in one 9,567-byte page with no libraries and no network. This lesson is its audit: the shipped rules were lifted out and run headlessly, the two stored solutions were replayed under those rules beat by beat, and every claim on the game’s own back-door panel was checked against the code. Most of them hold. One does not, and it is named plainly below.

The one idea — a recording that works beside you

Most recorded things are for watching. An echo is a recording for working with. The trick that makes it possible is determinism: a shift is just a list of commands — U, D, L, R, W — and the world it runs in is small enough to be completely described: the robots’ positions, the crate’s position, and whether the door is open. Feed the same list to the same starting state and you get the same run, every time. The audit confirmed this the direct way: the same program with the same echo, simulated twice from a fresh setup, produced byte-identical frame sequences — and a scan of the file finds no random number generation anywhere in it. There is nothing in Echo Shift that can surprise its own replay.

That is what a rewind really is here. It is not time travel and it is not an undo. The game takes the list you just ran, pads it with waits out to the station’s beat limit, and files it as a worker. From then on, every beat hands each robot the command with that beat’s number — your new list for you, the filed lists for your echoes. The past doesn’t change; it just shows up for work.

A recording is not a thinker

An echo has no judgement, and the code is honest about what that means. Its commands are indexed by the beat, not by success. If an echo is blocked on beat 3, it does not try the blocked move again on beat 4 — beat 4 plays command 4, whatever it is. The audit staged exactly this: the player parked on the switch tile the echo wanted, the echo’s first command (R) was refused and it stayed put — and on the very next beat it obeyed its second command (U) and walked away to (1,1), leaving the blocked move behind forever. A blocked echo stays put for that beat and resumes its program on the next one; it never retries. This is the deepest lesson in the game, and it is a real one about real automation: a recorded plan replays beautifully in the world it was recorded in, and blindly in any other. Change anything — stand where your echo expects to stand — and the recording cannot notice. It will do the wrong thing on schedule, precisely, forever.

The page’s own back-door text says this in one line — “The echo is a recording, not a thinking opponent” — and the audit found the code keeps that promise exactly.

Everyone moves together — in the arithmetic, one at a time

The station clock is simultaneous: on each beat, every robot attempts its command. But a computer cannot actually move two things at once, and this game doesn’t pretend to. Inside next() — the one function that advances the world by a beat — the robots are moved sequentially, in a fixed order: you first, then your echoes in the order you recorded them. Each robot’s move is collision-checked against the world as the robots before it have already changed it. The audit drove three cases that pin the semantics down:

  • Contesting one tile, the earlier robot wins. Two robots aimed at the same empty tile from above and below. When the first robot’s command sent it in, it got the tile and the second was refused; when the first was told to wait, the second got the tile instead. The decider is not the command — it is position in the list.
  • You cannot follow a robot that hasn’t moved yet. Two robots side by side, both ordered right: the rear robot’s target was still occupied at the moment its move was checked, so it stayed — while the front robot walked on. A one-tile gap opens behind a moving robot purely because of processing order, even though both were “moving together.”
  • But you can step into a tile just vacated. Reverse the order — leader listed first, follower behind — and both move: the follower enters the tile the leader left earlier in the same beat. Chasing your echo works; being chased by it needs a beat of slack.

None of this is a bug. It is what “simultaneous” always means inside a machine: a queue, taken in order, fast enough to look like togetherness. The game’s plans have to respect it — when two workers need the same doorway, the one earlier in the order goes first, and the timeline view (click any beat to inspect it) exists so you can watch the queue happen.

The door is always one beat behind

Here is the subtlest rule in the game, and the one its failed plans turn on. When a beat begins, next() copies the door state from the end of the previous beat and uses that for every move in the beat. Only after all robots have moved does it recompute the door: open if any robot — or the crate — is standing on the switch. So the door you can use this beat is the door as it stood when the last beat ended. The audit demonstrated both edges of the lag:

  • A robot stepped onto the switch while a second robot, in the same beat, tried to enter the doorway. Refused — the door was still closed for that beat, even though the beat ended with the switch occupied and the door shown open. The second robot entered on the next beat.
  • A robot stepped off the switch while a second robot entered the doorway in the same beat. Allowed — the door was still open from the previous beat, and only closed at this beat’s end.

The shipped solutions never feel the lag — the echo settles onto the switch several beats before the player reaches the doorway — but a plan cut fine will meet it: arriving at the door on the same beat your echo arrives at the switch is arriving one beat too early. Real interlocks and real control systems have the same shape: sensors are read, the world is updated, and the new state applies to the next cycle. Echo Shift just makes the cycle slow enough to see.

Two more door facts the audit verified, because plans lean on them: a crate resting on the switch holds the door open exactly as a robot does — the check is “robots or crate on the switch” — and a crate can be pushed onto the switch to take the holding job. And a robot standing in the doorway when the door closes is not trapped or ejected; walking off checks only the tile you are entering, never the one you are leaving.

Pushing — the Sokoban rules, kept exactly

The crate obeys the classic push rules, and each one was driven as a test case against the shipped next():

  • Push into a wall: refused — robot and crate both stay.
  • Push into a closed door: refused. Push through an open door: the crate rolls onto the doorway tile, and onward onto the delivery tile for the win.
  • Push with a robot standing beyond the crate — including your own echo: refused. The crate cannot be pushed into a worker.
  • No chain pushes. Two robots in a line behind the crate do not add their strength: the rear robot is simply blocked by the front one, and only the front robot’s own push counts. Robots cannot push robots, either.
  • No pulling. Walk away from a crate and it stays exactly where it was. Every push is one tile, one beat, one robot.

One consequence deserves its own line, because the audit found it by accident and it is the kind of thing that ends a run: if the door closes while the crate is resting on the doorway tile itself, the crate is frozen there. Pushing it off would require the pusher to step onto the closed door tile to complete the push, and that step is refused. The crate waits — unharmed, unmovable — until somebody holds the switch again and the door reopens, at which point it pushes off cleanly. The door never crushes anything in this game; it just declines to participate.

The receipts — what the audit ran

The rules were extracted from the shipped page and run headlessly (a minimal document stub; the game’s own state(), next() and simulate() did all the work — no re-implementation). The game file is byte-identical before and after: sha256 72275af2….

  • The stored solutions verify — both stations. Each level stores a solution: two programs. The audit ran the first alone, rewound it into an echo using the game’s own rewind handler, then ran the second. Station 01 delivers on beat 6 of 7: the echo steps onto the switch at beat 2 and holds it; the player crosses the doorway at beat 5 and pushes the crate from (5,2) onto the delivery tile (6,2). Station 02 delivers on beat 7 of 9: same shape on a longer corridor — switch held from beat 2, doorway crossed at beat 6, crate pushed from (6,2) to (7,2). In both stations the first program alone does not deliver anything; it walks to the switch and waits, which is precisely its job.
  • Determinism: identical inputs simulated twice produced byte-identical frame sequences; Math.random appears nowhere in the file.
  • Move order: the three cases in the “moves together” section above — contest decided by list order, the gap that opens behind a leader, the follower entering a just-vacated tile.
  • Door lag: entry refused on the beat the switch is stepped on, allowed the beat after; entry allowed on the beat the switch is vacated; the crate alone on the switch holds the door; a crate pushed onto the switch holds it too.
  • Push rules: wall, closed door, and robot-beyond refusals; open-door, switch, and goal pushes; no chain push; no pull; the frozen crate-on-the-doorway case and its release when the door reopens.
  • Echo rules: invoking the shipped rewind handler on the program R, R stored the echo R, R, W, W, W, W, W — padded with waits to the 7-beat limit — and cleared the program. The blocked echo advanced its command index instead of retrying. Echoes spawn stacked on the start tile with the player (the initial frame genuinely has two robots on one tile); on the first beat, if both command R, the player moves and the echo is refused by the player’s arrival and stays — the stack resolves itself through the ordinary collision rule.
  • Win and limits: the win is checked after every beat and the simulation stops early — both solutions end with beats to spare. The program editor refuses commands beyond the beat limit (twelve attempted adds left a 7-command program on Station 01), and once a station is won, adding commands does nothing and the Run and Rewind buttons disable. A short program simply waits out its missing beats: unlisted commands default to W.

How it’s built — three functions and a list of frames

The whole engine is three small functions. state() builds the starting world: every robot — you plus one per echo — on the start tile, the crate on its tile, the door closed. next(state, commands) advances one beat exactly as described above and returns a new state; it never edits the old one. simulate(program) deals one command per robot per beat — yours from your program, each echo’s from its filed list — and collects every state into a list of frames, stopping early if the crate reaches the delivery tile. That list of frames is also the timeline you can click through after a run: the game doesn’t animate a solution and then describe it, it keeps every beat it computed and lets you inspect any of them. It is the same architecture as Stack-a-Bot’s — world as data, rules as functions, drawing as a readout — pointed at a different idea: where Stack-a-Bot’s loop asks “what happens next?”, Echo Shift’s asks “what happens again, with one more worker in the room?”

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: Echo Shift was built by ChatGPT at Steven Powell’s direction; this lesson and audit are by Muse. The game is a small, honest model of parallel work. Here is where the model ends and the staging begins, so nobody mistakes one for the other:

There is no solver in this page. The game’s own back-door panel says each station “has a machine-checked solution” and describes a solver that “enumerates programs, simulates them under the shipped rules, and accepts only a plan that delivers the crate.” The audit searched the file: the word solution appears exactly twice — the two stored programs in the level data — and no code in the page ever reads them; nothing in the file enumerates or searches anything. The true statement is narrower and still good: the stored solutions are real, and they are machine-checked — by this audit, after the fact, replayed under the shipped rules and watched delivering the crate on both stations. But the checking machine is not in the page, and the page should not imply it carries one.

Two stations, one shape. Both shipped stations are the same corridor family — start, switch, door, delivery in a single row, with an empty row above and below for walking around — differing in length, beat limit (7 and 9), and nothing else. Each allows at most one echo. What ships is a two-station proof of a beautiful mechanic, not a campaign: no station needs the crate parked on the switch, none needs two echoes, none punishes the door lag. The mechanic is fully general; the tour of it is short.

“Together” is a queue. As the receipts show, beats are processed one robot at a time in list order. Plans that need two robots through one tile on one beat will fail in ways that look simultaneous on the clock and sequential in the outcome — the timeline view is the tool for seeing it.

The door lags one beat, both ways. It opens for the beat after the switch is occupied and stays open for the beat after it is vacated. And a crate parked on the doorway tile freezes while the door is closed, as described above — a consequence of the rules, found by the audit, stated nowhere on the game page.

The echo cap lives in the button. The limit of one echo per station is enforced by disabling Rewind & keep echo once the cap is reached; the rewind handler itself does not re-check. In the page as played, the cap holds — but it holds at the interface, not in the rules.
