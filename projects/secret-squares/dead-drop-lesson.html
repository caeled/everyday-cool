Dead Drop — the lesson

A dead drop is how spies exchange messages without ever meeting: leave the package somewhere ordinary, walk away, and the other side picks it up later. Dead Drop the workbench is that idea for computers that are not allowed to talk to each other. Four channels — a picture, a QR code, a Morse broadcast, a voice reading numbers — each turn a short message into something that can cross an air gap on a USB stick, a printed page, or through the air as sound. One file, offline, no network. This page is the other half: what each channel actually does, and the audit that drove all four of them.

Built by Steven Powell in his Night Project sessions — his sneakernet workbench, made because sometimes the models need to pass notes in class. Audit and this lesson by Muse; the workbench itself is Steven's, byte for byte.

Why a dead drop?

Every other page in this room moves information across a gap and asks what survives. Pass It On corrupts a message on purpose to teach error correction. Dead Drop starts from the opposite problem: the gap is not noise, it is policy — machines that must not be networked together, models that must not share a wire. Sneakernet is the oldest network of all: carry the storage medium yourself. The workbench's four channels are four different physical carriers for the same note:

ChannelThe carrierDirection
ImageA PNG picture file, carried or posted anywhere pictures goEncode + decode
QRA printed or displayed QR code, read by any phone cameraEncode (the reader is the other side's job)
MorseSound — a 700 Hz tone over a bed of shortwave staticTransmit (you copy it by ear)
NumbersA voice reading digit groups, numbers-station styleTransmit (you decode by hand)

Nothing here is encryption. Every scheme is published on this page and in the file's own comments — the header bytes are, in the author's words, not a secret. A dead drop hides a message by making it look like something else, or by putting it somewhere only the intended reader thinks to look. Anyone who knows the scheme can read every channel. That is the honest shape of steganography, and the workbench never pretends otherwise.

Channel 1 — the image: notes in the least-significant bits

Every pixel of a digital image carries three colour numbers (red, green, blue), each 0–255. Changing the last binary digit of one of those numbers moves the colour by one part in 256 — invisible. The image channel writes the message into exactly those last digits: three usable bits per pixel, walking red, green, blue in order, never touching the alpha (transparency) byte. The payload is the message's UTF-8 bytes behind a small header:

payload = "NP1\0" (4 bytes) · version (1 byte) · length (4 bytes, big-endian) · the message bytes The marker lets the decoder tell "this picture carries a note" from random pixel noise. Bits are written most-significant-bit first, one per colour channel.

Capacity is pure arithmetic — three bits per pixel, minus the 72 header bits:

capacity = floor((width × height × 3 − 72) / 8) bytes A 64×64 test image holds 1,527 bytes; the workbench's generated 600×600 carrier holds 134,991. The audit checked this formula against the page's own capacityBytes at six sizes — exact at every one, and a message of exactly capacity length fits while one byte more is refused.

The panel also builds the classic captioned image — top and bottom meme text over your photo or over a plain dark field — and hides the note in the captioned picture, so the carrier looks like the most ordinary artefact on the internet. The catch is the file format: the bits survive only if the picture is stored losslessly. PNG keeps every bit; JPEG would repaint the pixels and shred the note. The workbench downloads PNG for exactly that reason, and the audit proved the full journey — build a captioned image in the panel, take the downloaded PNG bytes, read them back with an independent PNG decoder, and the message comes out exact.

Channel 2 — QR: the note as a picture of itself

The QR channel is a complete QR Code (Model 2) generator written into the file: byte mode, error-correction level M, versions 1–10 — Reed–Solomon error correction over GF(256), block interleaving, all eight mask patterns scored by penalty and the best one kept. The core follows Project Nayuki's well-known generator design, reimplemented compactly so the file stays self-contained; the file's comments say so. Capacity is the standard level-M table, and the audit confirmed the page's numbers against it exactly:

Version12345678910
Bytes at level M1426426284106122152180213

There is deliberately no QR decoder in the file — the header comment calls decoding a stretch goal and leaves it to "a real QR reader / phone camera." So the audit supplied one: the independent jsQR decoder read the page's raw module matrices back at version 1, 3, 4, 8 and 10 — including a 130-byte message that forces version 8 (four interleaved blocks, version information blocks) and the full 213-byte maximum at version 10 — and recovered every message exactly, UTF-8 text included. One byte over capacity and the generator refuses, as it should. The page's own self-test only checks the v1 structure (finder patterns, timing) and says plainly that a real reader is the round-trip audit. It was right, and now it has had one.

Channel 3 — Morse: the note as sound

The Morse broadcaster turns text into the standard code — the audit compared all 52 letters, digits and punctuation marks the page knows against the ITU table; every one matches, and the page adds two conventional extras (_ and $). Timing is the standard rhythm: dit 1 unit, dah 3, one unit between symbols, three between letters, seven between words. Measured on the page's own event builder, the word PARIS totals exactly 43 units — the same 43 the collection's Skip audit used as its reference — and "SOS SOS" totals 61, word gap included. Broadcast plays a 700 Hz sine keyed by those events, over a looped band of filtered noise that sounds like a quiet shortwave band, with a lamp on the panel that lights while the key is down. The speed slider runs 40–160 ms per unit; the 80 ms default is a 15-words-per-minute hand.

The channel is transmit-only. There is no Morse decoder anywhere in the file — receiving means a person (or another program) listening and copying, the way it has worked since 1844. The audit verified the decode direction the only honest way available: it decoded the page's output with an independent copy of the standard table and recovered a full pangram plus all ten digits exactly.

Channel 4 — numbers: the note as a voice

Real numbers stations broadcast groups of digits to listeners who decode them by hand against a key. This channel borrows the theatre and simplifies the cipher to a plain encoding anyone can undo: every UTF-8 byte of the message becomes three digits (A = 065), and the digit stream is displayed — and spoken, by the browser's speech synthesis, over the same shortwave static — in groups of five:

"A" → byte 65 → "065" · the panel's own decode rule: write the digits, split into 3s, each triple is one UTF-8 byte Groups of five are display rhythm only; the information is in the triples. The audit round-tripped ASCII, Greek, a snowman and an emoji (a surrogate pair — four UTF-8 bytes, twelve digits) through the page's textToGroups/digitsToText pair: exact every time, and the digit stream matched an independent UTF-8 encoding digit for digit.

The decode helper exists in the code and is exercised by the self-test, but the panel itself offers no decode box — on the air, you copy the groups down and work them out on paper. Where no speech voice exists, the panel says so and falls back to beeps and the printed groups, which the audit drove end-to-end: attention tone, one group, "Transmission ends."

The audit — driven, not read

The workbench ships with its own self-test panel, and its footer is the collection's rule in the author's own words: breakage is the only honest audit — run the self-test. So the audit started there, clicking the page's own button in a headless DOM. Full output, verbatim:

PASS ChanImage: round-trip exact (46 bytes, capacity 1527 bytes) PASS ChanQR: v1 structure valid (finders/timing), capacity guard fires; scan with a real reader to confirm round-trip PASS ChanMorse: SOS/word-gap/timing exact PASS ChanNumbers: A=065, mixed-charset round-trip exact 4/4 passed

Then each channel was driven independently — the page's own exposed functions, its buttons, its preview pixels — 50 harness checks plus a follow-up pass on the PNG question, against hand computations, the standard QR capacity table, the ITU Morse table, an independent UTF-8 encoder, an independent QR decoder (jsQR) and an independent PNG codec. All four panels mount — the page builds the grid from four slots and substitutes a "module not present" placeholder if a channel is missing; in the shipped file every slot carries its real panel, and no control on any panel was found dead.

Honest limits

Translucent carriers can silently lose the note. The image channel's one bend: canvas and PNG pipelines store colour premultiplied by transparency, so pixels whose alpha is below 255 can have their colour bytes rounded when the file is written and re-read — and the LSBs are the first casualty. The audit's test carrier with varying alpha came back with 5,821 of 16,000 bytes altered and the payload destroyed. An opaque carrier — any ordinary photo, and every carrier the workbench itself generates — made the same journey with zero bytes changed and the message exact. Rule of the channel: hide notes in opaque pictures. The page does not warn about this; consider yourself warned here.

No checksum in the image channel. Flip one payload bit and the message decodes with one character silently garbled (the audit flipped one and got "�lip test message"). Flip a bit in the length field and the decoder does catch it — it refuses with "Payload length exceeds image capacity." Damage the marker and it reports "No valid payload." But quiet, single-character corruption passes unnoticed. The QR channel is the armoured one; the image channel is the quiet one.

Small print, all verified: the header's version byte is written (1) but not checked on decode — a header claiming version 0x63 still decoded in the audit. The QR mask is chosen with penalty rule 3 omitted (the code's own comment admits it); that can pick a different mask than the full rules would, never an invalid one — jsQR read every test code. The numbers decoder is lenient with hand-copied input in both directions: spaces are ignored and a trailing partial triple is silently dropped, but a mistyped group like 999 becomes byte 231 and decodes to the replacement character � — garbage in, visibly garbage out. Unknown characters in Morse (a tilde, an emoji) are silently dropped from the broadcast.

What the audit could not touch: the file-picker paths (choosing a carrier or a picture to read) run on URL.createObjectURL, which the headless DOM does not implement — the decode logic behind them was driven directly, and the download side was verified over real PNG bytes, but no auditor clicked an actual file dialog. Sound was driven through a stub Web Audio clock (the headless DOM has no audio hardware), so the Morse and numbers transmissions were verified as schedules and state machines, not heard; the speech-synthesis voice likewise could not be present headlessly, so the numbers station was audited on its documented beeps-and-readout fallback. Your own open of the page is the listening receipt.
