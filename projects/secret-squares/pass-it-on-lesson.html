Pass It On — the lesson

Pass It On is a game about sending a message down a wire that cannot be trusted. The channel changes digits; the only question is what you added before sending — because what you added decides whether the receiver can notice the damage, locate it, or repair it. This page is the other half of the game: the ideas it runs on, worked examples taken from the game's own proof runs, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

Redundancy is the price of trust

Every protection in the game is the same move wearing different clothes: send more than the message. A naked message carries no spare information, so a changed digit is indistinguishable from an intended one — the receiver in Level 1 writes down 4 5 1 5 1 6 when you sent 4 8 1 5 1 6, and nothing about the arrival looks wrong. You cannot detect what you did not duplicate, in some form. A check digit duplicates the message's total. Parity duplicates every row and column sum. Repetition duplicates everything. The shields differ only in what they duplicate — and that decides what they can promise, and what they cost.

The check digit — detection for the price of one symbol

The game's check digit is the rule from this room's own Bar Shelf, where it guards every barcode: weight the digits 3, 1, 3, 1 … from the right (the digit just before the check digit carries the 3), add them up, and choose the check digit that makes the total end in 0.

message 3 1 4 1 5 9 → from the right: 9×3 + 5×1 + 1×3 + 4×1 + 1×3 + 3×1 = 45 → check digit 5 The code that travels is 3 1 4 1 5 9 5, and its full weighted total is 50 — it ends in 0.

Now change any one digit — say the payload's 5 becomes a 2. The total drops by 3, to 47, and 47 does not end in 0: caught. It always works, and the reason is arithmetic, not luck: each digit is weighted 1 or 3, both of which are coprime to 10, so no single-digit change (a shift of 1–9) can move the weighted total by a multiple of 10. The game's proof changes every digit of 3 1 4 1 5 9 5 to every wrong value — 7 positions × 9 wrong digits = 63 corruptions, and all 63 are caught. In the game itself, the channel is ruder: it hits the check digit itself, 5 → 4, and the total still fails. The guard guards itself.

But notice what the receiver learns: something changed. Not which digit, not what it should be. Detection is a smoke alarm, not a map — the only remedy is to send the whole message again and hope the wire behaves, which is why Level 2's delivery costs two sends.

Parity — and what a second dimension buys

A parity guard is a sum kept as a receipt. Give each row of digits one guard digit — the row added up, mod 10 — and a changed digit breaks its row's receipt. That alone is detection again. Add a guard for every column too, and the geometry changes everything: one changed cell breaks exactly one row check and one column check, and the crossing of the two complaints is the cell. Better still, the row guard says by how much the cell drifted, so the receiver can put the original digit back.

Here is the game's own first grid, guards included (the last column and last row are the guards; the corner guards the guards):

2 7 1 8 │ 8 3 1 4 1 │ 9 5 9 2 6 │ 2 ───────┼── 0 7 7 5 │ 9

The channel changes the very first cell: 2 arrives as 3. Row 1's digits now add to 19 → 9, but its guard says 8 — the row complains, and the size of the disagreement (8 − 9 ≡ −1) is the drift. Column 1 adds to 11 → 1 against a guard of 0 — it complains too. Crossing: row 1, column 1. Repair: 3 + (−1) = 2, the digit that was sent. The game's proof flips every cell of this grid, one at a time, to every wrong value — all 20 cells, guards and corner included, 180 corruptions — and the receiver repairs every single one exactly. A guard digit can take the hit and the grid still lands whole, because a lone complaining guard with silent neighbours can only be the guard itself.

Detection and correction are different promises

Triple-sendEverything, three timesCorrects anything confined to one copy18 symbols

Neither promise is "better" in the abstract — they are priced differently, and the right buy depends on the weather and on whether the receiver can answer back. The game's finale is built on exactly that: a detected message with a re-send available costs 7 + 7 = 14 symbols, which beats triple-sending's 18 — until the one message that can never be re-sent, where forward correction is the only shield that lands it at all.

Why two errors beat the grid

Hit two cells in different rows and different columns. Now two row guards and two column guards complain, and their crossings make four suspect cells. The two real culprits sit on one diagonal of that rectangle; two innocent cells sit on the other — and every guard reading is perfectly consistent with either diagonal being the guilty one. There is no arithmetic way to choose, and "repairing" the wrong diagonal would create errors in clean cells. So an honest receiver refuses: it reports the break and stops. The game's proof checks all 66 pairs of data cells in the grid: all 66 are detected, and none is miscorrected. Two-dimensional parity detects any two errors; it corrects one. That gap — between what a scheme can catch and what it can fix — is where all real error-control design lives.

Repetition and voting — the expensive brute

The oldest shield is to say it three times and let the copies vote, position by position. It asks nothing clever of anyone, and its guarantee is easy to state exactly: if the damage is confined to one copy, then at every position two copies agree on the true digit, and the majority is always right. The proof throws everything of that shape at the game's message 9 1 7 3 3 0 — every single-digit corruption of every copy (162 patterns) plus each copy mangled end to end (3 more): 165 of 165 patterns vote back to the original. Its limits are just as plain: it costs three times the wire for every message, forever, and if two copies are hit at the same position with the same wrong digit, the vote confidently restores the wrong digit. Brute force is a real strategy; it is simply the most expensive one on the shelf.

The honest ceiling: accidents, not enemies

Everything on this page assumes the channel is careless, not hostile. A check digit stops a typo; it does not stop a person — anyone who knows the rule can change a digit and fix the guard to match, and the receiver will accept the forgery with a smile. This room says it on the Transfer Station bench and it applies to every shield in the game: a checksum is not authentication. Real systems that face enemies use cryptographic signatures, a different tool for a different job (the Secret-Message Bench, one section over). And real systems that face merely rough channels go further than parity: the QR codes all over this room carry Reed–Solomon error correction, which treats the message as polynomial arithmetic and can rebuild whole missing chunks — the Damage Lab lets you cover a code and watch a real decoder do it. Parity is the honest ancestor of that machinery: same idea, smaller numbers.

How it's built — the coding choices

A seeded channel, so the noise can be proven

The channel's flips are drawn from a seeded random generator (mulberry32): random-looking, but identical on every run. That is what makes the game testable at all — a trip can be rehearsed by a script and land the same way for the player. When Level 5's forecast says a burst is coming, the burst is a fixed, examinable fact of the level, not a dice roll the designer hides behind.

The rules are a pure-function core, so a script can play the game

Everything that decides an outcome — the check digit, the grid, the vote, the channel, the price of each shield, the resolution of a whole send — lives in one sealed block of code fenced between two marker comments in the game file. It knows nothing about the page. The proof program lifts that exact block out of the shipped file and plays against it: it corrupts the check code all 63 ways, flips all 180 grid cells, votes through all 165 single-copy patterns, and then plays every level with the obvious strategy — naked into calm skies, parity against a stray hit, triple-send for the one-way beacon, detect-and-resend where resends are cheap. The scripted operator finishes the finale's noisy day for 56 symbols of the 66 budget. The levels were proven fair — and the traps proven real — by the same code you play, not a copy of it.

Digits and mod-10, so the arithmetic stays visible

Real parity works on bits, mod 2. The game works on digits, mod 10, because then every guard can be checked with school arithmetic, by hand, on the page — the same reason this room's barcode bench works its check digit by hand. Nothing about the locating trick depends on the base: a sum, a receipt, a crossing.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the guards are mod-10 sums of decimal digits; real parity is mod-2 on bits, and real check systems (Luhn, ISBN, IBAN) vary the weights and the modulus. The channel flips symbols independently and never erases one outright; real noise also arrives in bursts that straddle copies and grids, and in erasures where a symbol is missing entirely — both are harder than anything modelled here, and burst damage is exactly what can defeat the vote. Re-sends in the game always travel clean; a real channel makes no such promise, which is why real protocols number their packets and retry with back-off (the Transfer Station's numbered packets are the same instinct). The finale's prices are the game's own symbol counts, not a real tariff. And no adversary is modelled anywhere: every shield here guards against accidents only, by design and by the room's own rule — a checksum is not authentication.
