Mastermind — the lesson

Mastermind is a duel fought entirely with questions. The codemaker hides four pegs; the codebreaker may only ask, ten times, "how about this?" — and the answer is never a name, only a count: so many pegs exactly right, so many right but displaced. Everything the codebreaker ever learns arrives in that trickle. This page is the other half of the game: the arithmetic underneath it, worked examples taken from the game's own proof runs, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The game — a codebreaker's duel from 1970

Mastermind was invented by Mordecai Meirowitz, an Israeli postmaster and telecommunications expert, and launched in 1970/71 — a plastic-and-pegs descendant of the old pencil game Bulls and Cows, which students had been playing in margins for decades. The roles are the whole design. The codemaker's power is total and silent: one hidden pattern. The codebreaker's power is only interrogative: propose a complete code, receive a scored answer, propose again. No peeking, no partial questions, no asking about one peg at a time. Every guess must be a whole theory of the secret, and every answer grades the theory without ever saying where it went wrong. That shape — whole guesses, graded answers, a ledger that only grows — is why the game rewards bookkeeping more than inspiration, and why it belongs in this room, next to the checksums and the parity grids: it is error-correction pointed the other way. Pass It On asks "what survived the wire?"; Mastermind asks "what is still possible, given everything the wire has told me?"

The country of codes: 64 = 1,296

Four pegs, six colours, repeats allowed. The count is a multiplication, not a mystery:

6 choices × 6 choices × 6 choices × 6 choices = 64 = 1,296 possible codes Repeats allowed is what makes it 64. Ban repeats and the country shrinks to 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 360 — a smaller, easier duel, and not the classic game.

Before the first guess, the secret is one of 1,296 equally possible codes, and the counter beside the board says so. That number is the game's true scoreboard. You do not win by finding the code; you win by eliminating 1,295 of its rivals, one answer at a time, until the code is the only citizen left — and then naming it. The counter is not decoration: the game recomputes it honestly after every answer, by testing all 1,296 codes against your whole ledger and counting the survivors.

What one answer tells you

An answer is a pair of counts — blacks and whites — and only 14 pairs can ever occur. Blacks run 0–4 and whites run 0–(4 − blacks), which gives 15 pairs; one of them, 3 black and 1 white, is impossible: if three pegs are exactly right, the fourth peg's colour either matches the one remaining secret peg (a fourth black) or it doesn't match it at all — there is no "wrong place" left for it to be right in. The game's proof enumerated the answers across probe guesses and found exactly the 14.

So each answer sorts the surviving codes into at most 14 classes and tells you which class the secret sits in. The classes are wildly uneven, and the unevenness is the strategy. Take the classic opening guess of two pegs of one colour and two of another — say Red Red Blue Blue. The proof deals that guess against all 1,296 secrets and counts the classes:

Answer to Red Red Blue BlueCodes still possibleWhat it means
0 black · 0 white256No red, no blue anywhere — the secret uses only the other four colours: 44 = 256.
1 black · 0 white256The largest class — one peg home, nothing else even present.
4 black · 0 white1The guess was the code. The duel ends on the first question, once in 1,296 duels.

One answer, and the country is at most a fifth of its former size — often far less. A good guess is one whose classes are all small; a bad guess is one that leaves a fat class you might land in. Choosing guesses by that standard — how evenly does this question split the country? — is the entire skill of the game, and it is a skill about the questions, not about the secret.

Corrected by halves — the counter is the lesson

Nobody halves 1,296 cleanly; the answers cut where they cut. But the rhythm is the rhythm of every halving search in this collection: hold the whole space, let each answer strike the codes it forbids, and watch the field fall. Here is a complete duel from the game's own proof runs — the house solver against the secret Purple Green Orange Blue. (Pegs are written by their colour initials: R B G Y O P.)

Guess 1: R R B G → 0 black · 2 white field: 1,296 → 222 Guess 2: Y O G B → 1 black · 2 white field: 222 → 38 Guess 3: B P G O → 0 black · 4 white field: 38 → 2 Guess 4: O G P B → 2 black · 2 white field: 2 → 1 ← the code is forced Guess 5: P G O B → 4 black · 0 white broken

Read guess 3's answer again: zero black, four white — every colour right, every peg displaced. That single answer is a straitjacket: it demands a complete reshuffle of four known colours, and only 2 of the 38 survivors qualify. One more answer and the field is 1 — at which point the duel is already over in every way that matters. Guess 5 is a formality: the code was forced, deduced before it was named. The board says so out loud when your field reaches 1, because that moment — the space closing to a single point — is the lesson the whole room is built around. Knuth's five — the guarantee, and what it costs

In 1977, Donald Knuth published the result that made Mastermind a mathematics exhibit ("The Computer as Master Mind", Journal of Recreational Mathematics): a strategy that is guaranteed to break any code in at most five guesses. His method is minimax — for each candidate guess, look at its largest answer class (the worst the house can do to you), and play the guess whose worst class is smallest. Two things about it surprise everyone. First, the optimal opening is two pairs — Red Red Blue Blue and its kin. Second, the minimax player sometimes guesses a code that the answers have already ruled out: a dead code can still be the best question, because splitting the field matters more than being a plausible answer. Knuth's five is a published worst-case guarantee, proven by exhaustive computer analysis of the strategy — stated here as his result, and it is the yardstick the game's own solver is honestly measured against below.

How it's built — the coding choices

Two-pass feedback, so no peg is counted twice

The scoring rule sounds simple and hides the game's one real trap: a secret peg must never be matched twice. Guess Red Red Red Red against a secret holding a single red, and a careless scorer awards four whites. The game's feedback counts in two passes — blacks first, with those pegs set aside on both sides; then, among the leftovers, each colour scores the smaller of its two counts. The proof holds that code to account with an independent scorer built on a different mechanism (peg-by-peg matching against a used-peg ledger) across 300,000 seeded secret-and-guess pairs — half of them deliberately duplicate-heavy — with zero disagreements.

The counter is a full-space filter, not an estimate

After every answer, the game walks all 1,296 codes and keeps exactly those that would have produced every answer on the ledger. There is no formula and no shortcut — the count is a census. The proof replays scripted duels step by step and compares the game's surviving set against brute-force filtering with the independent scorer: 938 steps across 300 seeded secrets, the sets identical every time, and the true secret never once filtered out. A filter that could lose the truth would make the counter a liar; this one provably cannot, as long as the house answers honestly — and the house's answers come from the same proven feedback code.

The demo solver — a consistent-candidate picker, named as such

The "Watch the house think" demo plays with a solver that works the way a strong human does: it only ever guesses codes still consistent with the ledger, and among those it picks the one minimising the expected field left after the answer (the sum of the squared class sizes, divided by the field). Its report card, from the proof's 250 seeded duels: average 4.41 guesses, worst 6, 96.4% of codes broken within five, and all 250 inside the game's ten. Good numbers — and still not Knuth's method. It never asks a dead code a question, and it optimises the average case, not the worst; Knuth's minimax does both differently and owns the five-guess guarantee. The gap between "wins in 4.41 on average" and "can never need a sixth" is exactly the gap between a heuristic and a proof, and the game would rather show you both than blur them.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the built-in solver is a consistent-candidate picker minimising the expected remaining field — it is not Knuth's minimax, it makes no worst-case promise, and its worst observed run in the proof was 6 guesses (a minimax player would have needed at most 5; whether this picker can ever be pushed past 6 is unproven either way — its 250-duel report is evidence, not a guarantee). The house sets its secret uniformly at random from all 1,296 codes, repeats included; a human codemaker leans on favourite patterns, and a solver that modelled human habits would beat this one against humans. The feedback pegs are shown as counts with little black and white dots; as in the real game, they never say which pegs scored — any display that pointed at the scoring pegs would be a different, easier game. Pegs here carry colour initials as well as colours, a kindness the 1970 plastic original doesn't offer colour-blind players. And the counter's census is exact for this game's 1,296-code space; the same trick at a million codes would need the smarter bookkeeping the lesson describes rather than a full walk.
