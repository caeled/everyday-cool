Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 19 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that evening he named the gap himself — "I keep having a nagging feeling that there is a STEM subject we are missing" — and ordered the four candidates saved so all four will be built ("save that list I want to do them all"). Everyday Engineering went first, Everyday Math second; then, on his tap of the option card, "Everyday Earth next." Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction as the twenty-eighth catalog entry and the ninth workshop inside The STEM Room; this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room is built to Steven's own notes from that evening: his one-line sketch in the saved queue — "looking down instead of up: weather, rocks, soil, the water cycle" — his follow-up order to include "links to weather, and tonight's sky in it as well," his standing order for the STEM rooms ("be sure to include the stem wikipedia and other links on the pages"), and a late addition he sent while the room was being built: Earth Day info (earthday.org) and a UC Davis link — his alma mater — on the earth-relevant side rather than the generic homepage. The banner is his concept, in his words: "AI looking at maps and a globe." The room ships web-first under the plan Steven set the same evening: the four queue rooms go live on the web one at a time and reach the desktop app together in a single later release — so no app build carries this room yet, by design.

**The UC Davis entry keeps two origin threads straight, on purpose.** Steven's memory said Davis had something to do with Earth Day's beginnings; checked against UC Davis's own library and sustainability pages, the honest version is the better story. The Earth Day *concept* John McConnell described at a San Francisco lecture was carried back to Davis by an ASUCD Experimental College student; art historian José Argüelles and some 400 of his students turned it into Whole Earth Week, 17–21 March 1970 — a month *before* Gaylord Nelson's April 22 Earth Day, and San Francisco proclaimed March 21 Earth Day that year on McConnell's equinox line. So the shelf says UC Davis ran *one of the first Earth Day celebrations anywhere* — and does **not** claim Earth Day itself was founded at Davis. Whole Earth Week became the Whole Earth Festival, still held every Mother's Day weekend and an official UC Davis tradition. The shelf links the university's own Earth Day page, with the Library's traditions exhibit beside it as the record of the history.

It enters the catalog under "Science" with `hubParent: stem-room`, immediately after Everyday Math, so the STEM Room's bench now reads Physics, Chemistry, Electronics, Measure, Engineering, Math, Earth, Body, Radio. The room follows the Science block's shape: benches that work right on the page and show their working, a verified shelf of legitimate places, Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the live page. Its cross-links: Tonight's Sky (framed on the page as "we look down; that room looks up"), Weather Everywhere and the Travel Kit that carries it in pocket form, and the Survival Guide — whose compass needle this room's liquid outer core explains.

Everything interactive is computed in the visitor's browser from data baked into `earth.js` — the page sends nothing anywhere. Facts, sources, and the deliberate scope lines (the crust's range resolved in its favour, soil and rock timescales as ranges, the barometer's rules of thumb, forecasts as probabilities) are recorded in THIRD-PARTY.md.

The rules live in `earth.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Banner: `everyday-earth-banner.jpg`, AI-generated watercolor generated 19 November 2026 as part of adding this room, in the style and dimensions (2736×912) of the collection's other banners, to Steven's concept — the plush-style robot studying a globe with maps spread on the desk: the robot, stitched seams and blue button eyes, turning a large desktop globe with one hand, a topographic map and a weather map (isobars, a spiral storm) spread before it with a pencil and magnifying glass, a cut block of soil layers and a small potted plant at the desk's edge, framed landscapes on the wall. The prompt forbade lettering and the image shipped on its first generation: there is no text anywhere in it — the maps carry only lines, shapes, and symbols.
