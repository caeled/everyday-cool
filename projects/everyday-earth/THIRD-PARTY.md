# Sources & limitations — Everyday Earth

Everything interactive in this room is computed in the visitor's browser from data baked into `earth.js`. The page sends nothing anywhere. This file records where the room's facts come from and where its methods stop.

## The water cycle

- **The four stages and their shares:** the cycle as taught by the USGS Water Science School: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection/runoff (with infiltration and groundwater inside the last). The page's "about 90%" figure — the share of atmospheric moisture coming from oceans, seas, and lakes, the rest mostly transpiration from plants — is the USGS's, rounded the way they state it.
- **"Older than the dinosaurs":** the first dinosaurs appear ~240 million years ago; Earth's water stock is billions of years old and has cycled continuously. The honesty card on the page states the limit: individual molecules are split and rebuilt by chemistry, so what is ancient is the water itself, not each molecule's unbroken biography.

## Rocks and soil

- **The three families and the nine rocks:** the standard classification (igneous from melt, sedimentary from layers, metamorphic from other rocks changed by heat and pressure), as in Wikipedia's *Rock (geology)* and *Rock cycle*. Formation notes are the textbook ones: granite intrusive and slow-cooled, basalt extrusive and fast, pumice vesicular; sandstone cemented sand, limestone biogenic (shells and skeletons), shale compacted mud; marble from limestone, slate from shale, gneiss banded from granite-like parents. Everyday sightings (granite countertops, limestone as cement's raw material and cave host, marble in statuary, slate roofs and blackboards) are the commonly documented uses.
- **Soil horizons:** the O, A, B, C, R sequence is the standard simplified profile (real soils add transitional horizons, and not every soil has all five — the page's diagram says it is drawn to be read, not measured).
- **Topsoil formation time:** stated as a range — an inch in a few hundred to a thousand years — because the honest figure is a range; the USDA/NRCS's commonly quoted ~500 years per inch sits inside it and is named as a quoted figure, not a constant.

## The deep Earth

- **Layer boundaries:** crust base variable (5–10 km oceanic, 30–50 km continental, ~70 km under great ranges), mantle to 2,900 km, outer core 2,900–5,150 km, inner core 5,150 km to the centre at 6,371 km — the standard model, as in Wikipedia's *Internal structure of Earth*. The bench uses 70 km for the crust's base, the top of the range, and says so: the crust is given its best case before being called a hairline.
- **Inner core temperature** ~5,200 °C and radius ~1,220 km are the commonly cited estimates. The knowledge source is seismology (wave speeds, bending, and the liquid core's shadow), as the page's earthquake card states.
- **The digging records:** deepest mine — Mponeng gold mine, South Africa, about 3.9 km; deepest hole — the Kola Superdeep Borehole, 12,262 m (~12.3 km); deepest ocean point — Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, about 10.9 km. All three are within the crust at any of its thicknesses.

## Weather

- **Fronts:** cold fronts lift warm air abruptly (brief, sometimes violent precipitation, then cooler drier air); warm fronts overrun gradually (long steady precipitation, then warmer air). Map symbols (triangles for cold, semicircles for warm, pointing in the direction of travel) are the standard synoptic convention, as in Wikipedia's *Weather front*.
- **Clouds:** the four families and their heights (cirrus above ~6 km, stratus below ~2 km, cumulonimbus tops at 12 km and beyond) follow the standard classification in Wikipedia's *Cloud*. Fore readings are the traditional, generally reliable associations — cirrus as an early warm-front signal, growing cumulus as a warning — stated as tendencies, not rules.
- **The barometer:** mean sea-level pressure 1013.25 hPa = 29.92 inHg; the conversion uses 1 inHg = 33.8639 hPa. The trend thresholds (±0.6 hPa and ±2 hPa over three hours) are the traditional rules of thumb carried by generations of barometer dials, and the bench prints its own limit: a trend at one place is a witness, not a forecast. The lightning rule — "when thunder roars, go indoors" — is the National Weather Service's own wording.
- **Forecasts as probabilities:** a "30% chance of rain" is a point probability from past-like setups; the page's phrasing ("in thirty out of a hundred past days set up like this one, it rained") is the standard plain-language explanation of probability of precipitation.

## Scope lines, stated rather than silent

- The crust is drawn and computed at **70 km**, the deepest end of its range, and the page says so — the hairline conclusion survives the generosity.
- The soil diagram is **not to scale**, and says so; horizon order is the lesson, thickness varies by place.
- Soil and rock times are **ranges and orders of magnitude**, never single dates.
- The barometer's thresholds are **rules of thumb**, and the weather section's standing line is that a forecast is a probability, not a promise.
- The rock sorter holds **nine rocks** chosen for everyday encounters, not the hundreds a geology course would name; the field method (crystals, layers, bands) is the transferable part.
