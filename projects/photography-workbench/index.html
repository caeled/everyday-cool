Bench 01 — The Three Bargains

The exposure bench.

The scene is bright daylight — the light the old sunny 16 rule was built for. Set the aperture (the size of the hole), the shutter (how long it stays open), and the ISO (how sensitive the film or sensor pretends to be). Land on the right exposure, then trade the dials against each other: many combinations gather the same light, and no two of them make the same photograph. Watch the background soften as you open up, the bird smear as the shutter slows, and the grain rise as the ISO climbs.

The stop is the currency. Each full stop doubles or halves the light. f/5.6 at 1/125, f/8 at 1/60, and f/4 at 1/250 are the same exposure — reciprocity. Photographers think in stops the way carpenters think in inches. Start at the sunny-16 answer — f/16 at 1/100 at ISO 100 — and walk the dials from there.

Bench 03 — Sharpness Has Edges

Depth of field.

A lens focuses at exactly one distance; everything else is a blur small enough to forgive. That forgiven zone is the depth of field, and it is pure geometry — focal length, aperture, the circle of confusion your sensor size allows, and where you pointed. Set the bench and read the zone. Focus at the hyperfocal distance and the zone runs from half of it to infinity: the landscape photographer's whole trick, in one setting.

Why portraits melt and landscapes don't: a long lens, a wide aperture, and a close subject shrink the zone to centimetres — one eye sharp, the world gone. Stop down to f/11 at a wide angle and the zone swallows the scene. Same camera. Different geometry.

Bench 04 — The Oldest Law on the Bench

Light falls off.

Light spreads as it travels, so the same lamp paints a wall at two metres with a quarter of what it gave at one. The inverse-square law is why flash photographs fall off into darkness behind the subject, why moving a lamp a little matters more than buying a brighter one, and why the sun — far enough away that its distance barely changes across a scene — lights a landscape evenly. Drag the subject away from the lamp and watch the meter.

The same law runs the radio benches and the astronomy shelf — anything that spreads from a point pays it. Everyday Physics' LuxLab measures light for real; this bench shows the geometry underneath.

Bench 05 — Where Things Go

The frame.

Composition guides are not laws — they are where eyes tend to land. Lay the grids over the scene and feel how each one argues for a different photograph, then crop the frame to a different shape and watch the picture change its mind. The thirds put the horizon on a line and the subject off-centre; the golden ratio (1.618…) nudges everything a breath further; the diagonals hunt for tension.

The honest line: every master breaks these on purpose. The grid is a starting argument, not a verdict — but you have to know where the lines are before you can break them well.

Help

How this bench works.

Five benches, all live. The exposure bench animates: the bird crosses on its own, and your shutter setting decides whether it arrives sharp or smeared. Every readout is computed from the standard formulas — EV = log2(N²/t), the thin-lens depth-of-field equations, the angle-of-view arctangents, and the inverse-square law — the same arithmetic a light meter and a lens chart use. Nothing here phones home; the page runs whole offline.

On a touch screen: every bench works by tapping the select menus and dragging the sliders; the canvases are displays, not controls, so nothing needs a hover or a right-click.

The banner is AI-generated, in the collection's watercolor family: the bench's keeper putting a camera together from its parts, with the photograph it is dreaming of projected on the wall.
