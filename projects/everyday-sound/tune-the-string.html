Tune the String

A target note plays. Your job: tune a string to match it — with the real physics on the table, not a fake dial. A string's pitch follows f = (1 / 2L) · √(T / μ): shorten the length L or raise the tension T and the pitch climbs; a heavier string (bigger μ, mass per metre) sings lower.

How tuners really do it

Play the target and your string together. When two notes are close, you hear a slow wobble. Tune until the wobble dies — that's unison, and it's how tuners really do it. The wobble is called beating: it pulses at exactly the difference between the two frequencies, so a wobble of 3 pulses a second means you're 3 Hz away. Slower is closer. Silence — the steady, wobble-free tone — is the win.

Buttons: Play target, Play string, Play both. When you think you've got it, press Hold it — check my tuning. Early rounds give you a tuner needle; later rounds take it away and your ears do the work, the way they do on a real stage.

Honest limits

This string is the ideal kind from the physics book: perfectly flexible, no stiffness, so its overtones are the tidy family 2×, 3×, 4× the fundamental. Real strings are a little stiff, which stretches their overtones slightly sharp — piano tuners compensate for it, and it's called inharmonicity. Here μ is fixed within each round (a real player changes strings between songs, not during one), the target notes are equal-tempered, and the tones are synthesised stand-ins: a clean triangle-ish string voice and a sine for the target, so the beating between them is easy to hear. The numbers, though, are never faked — every frequency on this page comes out of the formula above, computed live from your slider positions.
