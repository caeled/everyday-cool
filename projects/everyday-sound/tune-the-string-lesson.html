Tune the String, explained

The game is the front door: you play it, and your ears learn something before your head does. This page is the back door — the physics underneath the game, the mathematics written out in full, the game's own numbers worked by hand, and the coding choices that built it, including the places where the game honestly simplifies. Read it before you play, after you play, or — from the 📖 Lesson button inside the game — while you play.

The game in one paragraph

Each round, the game plays a target note and hands you a string that starts out of tune with it. You get exactly two controls — the string's length and its tension — and one formula, computed live and never faked, turns those two numbers into a pitch. Early rounds show you a tuner needle. Middle rounds let you peek at the needle only a few times. The last three rounds take the needle away entirely, and the only instrument left is the oldest one in music: play both notes together, listen to the slow wobble between them, and tune until the wobble dies. Land inside the round's window — as tight as ±15 cents — and hold it.

The physics

Three things set a string's pitch

A stretched string is one of the oldest instruments in science — Pythagoras studied it on a one-stringed board called a monochord, and this game is a monochord with a score attached. Its pitch is set by exactly three things:

  • Length. A longer string vibrates slower and sings lower. Halve the length and the pitch exactly doubles — that's the octave, and it's the first musical ratio anyone ever measured.
  • Tension. A tighter string snaps back harder, vibrates faster, and sings higher — but only by the square root of the tension. Double the tension and you do not double the pitch; you multiply it by √2 ≈ 1.41, about a tritone. To double the pitch you must quadruple the tension. This surprises nearly everyone the first time, and it is why guitar pegs feel so touchy near the top of a string's range and why strings break: the last few semitones cost enormously more pull than the first few.
  • Mass per metre (μ). A heavier string is lazier — more mass to haul back and forth — and sings lower, again by a square root. This is why the fat strings on a guitar are the low ones, and why bass strings are wound with extra wire: winding adds mass without adding much stiffness.

Underneath all three sits one picture: the string's basic note is a standing wave with still points at the two fixed ends and a single hump in the middle. That hump is half a wavelength fitted onto the string, and everything in the formula below is bookkeeping for that one picture.

Beating: the wobble that tells the truth

Play two tones that are close but not equal and you don't hear two notes — you hear one note that swells and fades, swells and fades. That pulsing is beating. It happens because the two waves drift in and out of step: when their peaks line up they add and the sound is loud; half a cycle of drift later a peak meets a trough, they cancel, and the sound dips. The pulses come at exactly the difference of the two frequencies. Three hertz apart means three swells a second. A tenth of a hertz apart means one slow swell every ten seconds — and zero difference means the wobble is gone entirely.

That is why beating is the tuner's real method, older than any electronic tuner and still used by piano tuners today: it turns a tiny difference you cannot judge directly into a slow rhythm you can count. Your ear is poor at saying "that note is 4 cents sharp" and superb at saying "the wobble is slowing down." The game's final rounds take the needle away because, past a certain closeness, the ear listening to beating is the more honest instrument of the two.

The math

f = (1 / 2L) · √(T / μ)

Taken apart, symbol by symbol:

  • f — the frequency of the string's basic note (its fundamental), in hertz: vibrations per second.
  • L — the vibrating length of the string, in metres.
  • 2L — because the fundamental fits half a wavelength on the string, the full wavelength is λ = 2L. This factor of 2 is the standing-wave picture doing its bookkeeping.
  • T — the tension, in newtons: how hard the string is being pulled at its ends.
  • μ (the Greek letter mu) — the string's mass per metre, in kilograms per metre.
  • √(T / μ) — this is the speed of a wave travelling along the string, in metres per second. Tighter (bigger T) means a faster wave; heavier (bigger μ) means a slower one. The whole formula is then just the oldest wave relation there is: frequency = wave speed ÷ wavelength, f = √(T/μ) / 2L.

These are Mersenne's laws, written down in the 1630s — and the game computes with them on every movement of either slider. Nothing on the game's screen is looked up from a table.

Cents: measuring pitch the way ears hear it

cents = 1200 · log₂(f₂ / f₁)

Ears hear pitch as ratios, not differences: the jump from 110 Hz to 220 Hz sounds the same size as 220 to 440, because both are the ratio 2 — an octave. Cents turn ratios into a ruler. An octave is divided into 1200 cents, so one equal-tempered semitone is exactly 100 cents and a cent is a hundredth of that — small enough that a difference of a few cents is a fine tuner's working unit. Because the ruler is logarithmic, cents add where frequencies multiply: go up 100 cents twice and you have risen 200 cents, a whole tone, whatever note you started from.

The beat rate

beat rate = |f₁ − f₂|

Add two sine waves of slightly different frequencies and the sum can be rewritten as a single wave at the average frequency, wrapped in a slow envelope that rises and falls at half the difference — and since the ear hears the envelope's loudness, which peaks twice per envelope cycle, the audible pulsing lands at exactly the full difference, |f₁ − f₂| pulses per second. Beating is not a metaphor layered on the physics; it is the arithmetic of the two waves, audible.

Worked examples — the game's own numbers

Every number below comes from the game's actual proof runs — the same calculations its automated tests verified — redone here by hand so you can watch them work.

Example 1 — How hard do you pull for A2?

Round 1's string has μ = 0.006 kg/m. Take it at L = 0.65 m and ask: what tension makes it sing A2, 110 Hz? Rearrange the formula — square both sides and solve for T:

T = μ · (2Lf)² = 0.006 × (2 × 0.65 × 110)² = 0.006 × 143² = 0.006 × 20,449 = 122.694 N

About 122.7 newtons — roughly the weight of a 12.5 kg suitcase, hanging off one string. Real guitar strings live in this neighbourhood, which is why their pegs are geared.

Example 2 — A winning position in Round 1

The game's proof run found this exact Round 1 solution, using only values the sliders can actually land on: L = 0.263 m, T = 20.1 N, μ = 0.006 kg/m.

f = (1 / (2 × 0.263)) · √(20.1 / 0.006) = 1.901 × √3350 = 1.901 × 57.88 ≈ 110.04 Hz

Against the 110 Hz target, that's cents = 1200 · log₂(110.04 / 110) ≈ +0.57 cents — a whisker sharp, and far inside Round 1's ±25-cent window. Note what the solution did: it reached A2 at a short length and a gentle tension instead of the long, hard-pulled string of Example 1. Same note, different string — the formula has a whole curve of right answers, and part of the game is discovering that your ears, not the sliders, get the final say about which one you use.

Example 3 — Counting the wobble

Your string sits at 110 Hz; the target is 113 Hz. Play both:

beat rate = |113 − 110| = 3 pulses per second

Three swells a second, countable against a clock. Now tune upward slowly: the swells spread apart — two a second, one a second, one every few seconds — and each spread is the sound of the gap closing. At the top of the game's range, around A4 = 440 Hz, a wobble of one pulse every four seconds is a gap of about a single cent. That is the precision the final rounds are asking your ears for, and it is genuinely achievable — it is how tuners earned a living for centuries before batteries.

Why the needle goes away. In the last three rounds the game hides the needle and, if you miss, tells you only sharp or flat — never how much. That's not cruelty; it's the honest order of the instruments. The needle reports a number the game computed. The beating reports the thing itself, happening in the air between the two tones, and at close range it resolves differences finer than the needle's whole-cent readout. A player who finishes round 7 has done the real task with the real tool.

How it's built — the coding choices

Every game in this collection carries a builder's lesson too, because how a simulation is made decides whether it teaches the truth or a picture of the truth. Four choices matter in this one:

The beating is real — it is never drawn

The game makes its sounds with the Web Audio API: two separate oscillators, one sine for the target and one triangle wave (with a whisper of its octave partial) for the string, both sent into the same output when you press Play both. The browser's audio engine adds the two waveforms together sample by sample — the same addition the formula describes — so the wobble you hear is the arithmetic happening in the sound itself. Nobody animated a volume knob to look like beating. If the two frequencies are 3 Hz apart, the summed wave genuinely swells three times a second, and your ear finds it there. When a simulation can afford to run the real process instead of a picture of the process, it should.

Nothing is looked up

Every slider movement fires an input event, and the handler recomputes the frequency from f = (1 / 2L) · √(T / μ) using the slider values at that instant, then redraws every readout — including the formula line, which substitutes your actual numbers into the equation so you can watch the arithmetic track your fingers. There is no table of precomputed answers anywhere in the file. The string you hear is pitched by that fresh computation, so the game cannot drift away from its own physics.

Slider steps were chosen in cents, not in newtons

A slider step is a promise: you can always nudge finer than you need to. But a fixed step in newtons is not a fixed step in pitch, because pitch follows the square root of tension — a 0.1 N step is worth about 4.3 cents down at T = 20 N, about 0.7 cents at the 122.7 N of Example 1, and only about 0.17 cents up at 500 N. The length slider behaves the same way: its 0.001 m step is about 6.9 cents at the shortest length and 1.4 cents at the longest. The design rule that falls out: the coarsest single step anywhere on either slider is roughly 7 cents — less than half the tightest win window in the game (±15 cents). So a player can always creep up on the target step by step and can never jump clean over it. The steps look arbitrary in their own units; in cents — the units ears hear — they were budgeted.

The needle admits when its number is old

In the middle rounds, pressing Check the needle freezes the cents value at that moment and shows it. If you then move a slider, the game does not quietly update the reading — it dims the marker, marks it stale, and changes the caption to "last check — the string has moved since." A measurement display that silently refreshes an old reading would be lying about when its knowledge dates from. Freezing the value and confessing its age is a small honesty choice in interface design, and it doubles as game design: a check is a snapshot you spend, not a live feed you lean on.

One more, smaller: the drawn string on the game page vibrates at a deliberately slowed, scaled-down speed (its phase advances at a small fraction of the true frequency) so the eye can follow the hump. It is a suggestion of the motion, not a measurement of it — and the code says so in a comment, because a visualisation that hides its scaling is a claim it can't back up.

Simplifications, named

  • The string is ideal. Perfectly flexible, no stiffness. Real strings resist bending a little, which stretches their overtones slightly sharp of the tidy 2×, 3×, 4× family — the effect is called inharmonicity, and piano tuners compensate for it by stretching octaves. The game's overtones are the tidy family.
  • μ is fixed within a round. Real players change strings between songs, not during one — but a real string also stretches a hair as you tighten it, thinning μ as T rises. The game holds μ constant and says so on the round briefing.
  • Targets are equal-tempered. A2 is exactly 110.00 Hz, A4 exactly 440. A string quartet tuning by ear drifts toward just intonation instead; the game's targets are the tempered grid, the same one its cents ruler is built from.
  • The voices are stand-ins. A sine for the target and a triangle-plus-octave for the string, chosen so the beating is easy to hear. A real string's crowd of partials beats in several places at once, which is richer and messier than the game's clean single wobble.
  • Length changes are the whole string. Sliding L is a monochord's moving bridge, not a finger on a fretboard — no finger pressure, no fret height, no string bending sideways.
