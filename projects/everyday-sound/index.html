Sound.

Sound is the one piece of physics you carry a laboratory for: two ears, always on. This room makes tones you can slide from a rumble to a whistle, weighs loudness honestly in decibels, times echoes off walls, and then crosses the bridge this bench was built for — music, which is ratios, fractions, and geometry that you can hear. There is a piano at the end. It keeps what you play, if you ask it to.

01 / THE TONE BENCH

Pitch is a number.

Every tone is air shaking a certain number of times per second — its frequency, counted in hertz (Hz). Low numbers rumble; high numbers whistle. Slide and listen: the bench names the nearest musical note and measures the wave's length in air as you go.

Safety: keep the volume modest, especially on headphones — a pure tone at full blast is harder on ears than music at the same setting, and there is no warning moment before it is too much.

Honest limit: small speakers cannot move enough air to reproduce the lowest tones. Down at the bottom of the slider, what you actually hear is the tone's harmonics — its higher companions — while the fundamental itself is mostly missing. The number is real; your laptop's version of it is a sketch.

02 / THE DECIBEL LADDER

Loudness, weighed honestly.

Decibels are a logarithmic ruler: every +10 dB is ten times the sound energy, and every +3 dB is about double. That is why the ladder below looks so calm while describing a jet. Pick any two everyday sounds and compare them.

The hearing line

NIOSH — the US institute that studies workplace safety — recommends no more than 85 dB for 8 hours, and the safe time halves for every +3 dB: 88 dB gets 4 hours, 91 dB gets 2, and a 100 dB tool gets about 15 minutes. Hearing damage is cumulative and painless while it happens; the ringing afterwards is the receipt.

Honest limit: the ladder's numbers are typical values, not promises. A real chainsaw's level depends on the saw, the load, and above all your distance — sound falls off fast as you back away, which is the cheapest hearing protection there is.

03 / STRINGS & SKINS

Why a guitar sings and a drum thuds.

A stretched string obeys two kind rules: shorter is higher (halve the length, double the pitch — an octave), and tighter is higher (four times the tension, double the pitch). Every fretted, pegged, and capo'd instrument on Earth is those two rules wearing a costume. This bench starts from an open A string, 110 Hz.

Skins are different

WHY DRUMS SOUND UNPITCHED

A string's overtones are a tidy family — 2×, 3×, 4× the fundamental — and tidy overtones are what let your ear name a pitch. A drum skin vibrates in two-dimensional patterns whose overtones fall at untidy ratios (about 1.59×, 2.14×, 2.30×…). With no tidy family to lock onto, the ear hears a thud with a character instead of a note — which is why drums carry rhythm and strings carry melody, and why the bell on the piano below sounds like a bell: its partials are untidy in the same way.

Strings: harmonic overtones, a nameable pitch. Skins & bells: inharmonic overtones, a voice instead.

Play it: Tune the String → — this bench's two rules as a game with a win condition. A target note plays; you match it with the length and tension sliders, working the real string physics f = (1/2L)·√(T/μ). The win is the tuner's real method: play both notes together and tune until the beating wobble between them dies.

04 / ECHOES & THUNDER

Measuring distance with your ears.

Sound is fast but not instant — about 343 metres per second in 20 °C air — and that delay is a measuring instrument. An echo is a round trip: time it, halve it, and you have the wall. Thunder is the same trick at sky scale: light arrives instantly, sound ambles, and the gap between them is the storm's distance.

Time an echo

Count the thunder

The bench uses 20 °C air. If you can hear thunder at all, the storm is close enough to respect — counting distance is for curiosity, not for deciding whether to stay outside.

Honest limit: 343 m/s is the speed at 20 °C. Sound gains roughly 0.6 m/s for every degree warmer and loses it for every degree colder — about 2% across a hot afternoon, which the echo bench above lets you fold in.

05 / SOUND TRAVELS

Faster through solid ground.

Sound is a squeeze travelling through stuff, and stiff stuff passes the squeeze along faster. Air is spongy; water is stiffer; steel is stiffest of the three — so a sound can arrive through a railway line long before it arrives through the air above it.

Honest limit: the steel figure is for a long rail or rod. Through a thick block, sound takes slightly different paths at slightly different speeds — the ranking, though, never changes: solid beats liquid beats gas, every time.

06 / THE VOICE YOU HEAR

Why your recorded voice sounds like a stranger.

Two roads to your own ears

BONE CONDUCTION

When you speak, your voice reaches your ears by two roads at once: through the air, like everyone else hears it — and through the bones of your skull, which carry the low frequencies especially well. The voice in your head is that blend, richer and deeper than the air-only version. A recording captures only the air road. Playback sounds thin and strange because it is genuinely a voice you have never heard — the one everyone else knows you by.

Open Everyday Body → — where the ears themselves get their room.

The same trick, as a tool

BONE-CONDUCTION HEADPHONES

Headphones that rest on your cheekbones instead of in your ears use the bone road on purpose: music arrives through the skull while your ears stay open to traffic, conversation, and the oven timer. It is the recorded-voice effect run backwards — proof that the second road is a real channel, not a curiosity.

Two channels in; every recording keeps only one.

07 / INTERVALS — MUSIC IS RATIOS

Math geometry with sound.

Steven's framing for this whole wing of the room: music is basically math geometry with sound. Start here. When two notes sound together, what your ear judges is the ratio between their frequencies. Small whole numbers — 2:1, 3:2, 4:3 — mean the two waves' peaks keep meeting in a tidy pattern, and the ear calls that smooth. Awkward ratios rub. Press each interval and listen for the difference.

Honest limit: the piano in the next section does not use these pure ratios. It uses equal temperament — the octave sliced into twelve identical steps — so that music can change key without retuning. The price is that every interval except the octave is slightly off pure: the fifth is 1.498 instead of 1.500, the major third about 14 cents sharp. Three centuries of ears have voted that the compromise is worth it; this bench lets you hear what was traded away.

08 / THE LITTLE PIANO

Two octaves, five voices, no samples.

A real little keyboard: C4 to C6, playable with a mouse, a finger, your computer keys — or a MIDI keyboard, if you have one. Every sound is synthesized live in your browser from the recipe of the instrument you pick; nothing is a recording. The major scale it is tuned around climbs whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half — the pattern spelled out under the keys.

Press a key — the bench will name the note and count its hertz.

C — whole →D — whole →E — half →F — whole →G — whole →A — whole →B — half →C

Computer keys: A W S E D F T G Y H U J K play one octave (A is C, W is C♯, and so on up to K, the C above). Pitch is logarithmic — each octave doubles the frequency — which is why the logarithms in Everyday Math fit music like a glove.

Honest limit: Web MIDI works in Chrome and Edge, not in every browser, and only with your permission. If the button above never appeared, your browser is one of the ones without it — the piano still plays by pointer and keys.

Honest limit: these five voices are synthesized stand-ins — oscillator stacks shaped like each instrument's harmonics and envelope. A real piano's sound comes from strings, a soundboard, and the room it stands in; this bench borrows the shape of that sound, not the sound itself.

10 / THE CIRCLE OF FIFTHS

Geometry you can hear.

Take the twelve notes and arrange them not in pitch order but by perfect fifths — each note a 3:2 step from the last — and they close into a perfect ring. Musicians navigate by this circle: neighbouring keys on the ring share almost all their notes, so songs move around it the way journeys move between neighbouring towns. Click a note to hear it climb its fifth.

Twelve fifths make the full turn — and in pure ratios they overshoot seven octaves by a small comma of pitch, one of the cracks equal temperament was invented to paper over.

The same twelve points, turned to colour: Everyday Light's colour wheel arranges twelve hues on a ring and strikes chords by angle — complementary straight across at 180°, triads at 120° steps. Geometry you can see, from the room beside this one — Steven's pairing: music theory here, colour theory there.

13 / HELP

Help — how Everyday Sound works.

The manual for this workshop — what each part does, the simplifications it makes, and where its information comes from. This page and the full README work offline; the GitHub links need the internet.

Full documentation (README)Source on GitHub ↗Sources & limitationsDownload this workshopAll Everyday Cool documentation

The tone bench

A WebAudio sine oscillator on a logarithmic slider, 30–4000 Hz — logarithmic because pitch is: equal slider steps feel like equal musical steps. The readout names the nearest equal-tempered note and its distance in cents, and gives the wavelength in air at 343 m/s. Keep the volume modest (the bench runs at a deliberately low level), and remember the low end is mostly harmonics on small speakers: the fundamental below ~80 Hz is more felt than heard, and laptop speakers barely attempt it.

The decibel ladder

Nine typical levels, from a whisper to a jet, with a comparison bench: pick two and it shows the difference, the intensity multiple (10Δ/10), the rule-of-thumb loudness multiple (doubling per 10 dB), and NIOSH's safe-exposure time for the louder one — 85 dB for 8 hours, halving per +3 dB. The levels are typical published values; distance and the individual machine move real readings a lot, and the section says so.

Strings & skins

The string bench varies length (25–100%) and tension (50–400%) from an open A string at 110 Hz, computing frequency as base ÷ length × √tension, with presets for the octave (½ length), fifth (⅔), fourth (¾), and the tension octave (×4). The waveform drawing counts half-wavelengths so you can watch the standing wave tighten. The companion card explains drum skins: inharmonic overtones, hence no nameable pitch — the same reason the piano's bell voice sounds like a bell.

Echoes, thunder & travel

The echo bench halves the round trip at a temperature-adjusted speed of sound (343 m/s at 20 °C, ±0.6 per degree). The thunder bench sets the ÷3 rule of thumb beside the computed distance so you can see the rule's small, safe error. Sound Travels times a given distance through air, water, and steel and states the railway-listening trick plainly: through the rail wins by seconds per kilometre.

The voice you hear

Two cards on bone conduction: you hear your own voice through air and skull together, recordings keep only the air path, and bone-conduction headphones exploit the skull path on purpose. The fuller anatomy — the ear itself — lives in Everyday Body, linked from the cards and the sister rooms.

Intervals & temperament

Six intervals played as pairs of sine tones from a root you choose, each labelled with its pure ratio (2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 5:4, 45:32 for the tritone), its pure frequency, and its equal-tempered frequency with the piano's error in cents. A toggle switches playback between pure ratios and piano tuning so the compromise is audible, not just tabulated. The fifth's figures are the canonical example: pure 1.500 against tempered 1.498, about 2 cents.

The little piano

Two octaves, C4–C6, drawn as real keys and played by pointer, touch, computer keys (A W S E D… with Z/X shifting octave), or a connected MIDI keyboard. Five voices — Piano, Organ, Plucked string, Bell, Synth — are synthesized live from harmonic recipes and envelopes; there are no samples. The readout names each note, counts its hertz, and gives its C-major scale degree; the step chips under the keys spell the major scale's whole-whole-half pattern. Web MIDI appears only where the browser offers it (Chrome/Edge, with permission); where it doesn't, the bench simply doesn't mention it beyond one honest line.

Take a song with you

A Standard MIDI File reader and writer, written for this room in plain JavaScript: the reader handles formats 0 and 1, running status, and tempo changes, merges tracks, and plays the result through the chosen voice with the keys lighting as notes sound; files are parsed locally and never uploaded. The demo is Beethoven's Ode to Joy, public domain. The recorder captures every note from every input with its timing, plays it back, and writes a real format-0 .mid (480 ticks per quarter, tempo meta event) you can download and open anywhere MIDI plays. It keeps notes and timing only — no pedal, and dynamics only where the input reports them.

Rhythm & the circle

The rhythm bench fills a 4/4 measure with whole, half, quarter, and eighth notes and keeps the fraction sum in eighths so the verdict — exact, under, or over — is arithmetic, not opinion; it is the fractions bench of Everyday Math wearing a conductor's hat. The circle of fifths is an SVG ring of the twelve pitch classes, each a 3:2 step from its neighbour; clicking a note sounds it and its fifth, and the readout explains the near-miss (twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by a comma) that temperament exists to absorb.

Honest limits

Every bench carries its limits on its face: speaker physics at the tone bench's low end; typical-not-promised decibel values and the distance effect; the speed of sound's temperature drift; temperament's cents-level compromises, tabulated rather than hidden; synthesized voices that borrow the shape of instruments, not their sound; a MIDI player that is a player piano, not a studio; and a recorder that keeps notes and timing, not performance nuance. Where this room simplifies, it says what it simplified.

Works offline?

Yes — with one natural exception. The benches, the piano, the MIDI player and recorder, and this Help all run from the files in this folder, including inside the desktop app; a MIDI file you load is read from your own disk. Only the Links Out shelf needs the internet, because those pages live on other people's sites.
