02Power & Radio-Control Studio

Ready to get fancy? Bolt on real components — a small electric motor, a battery, a radio receiver and servos that move actual flaps, plus three ways to claw energy back: sun, motion, and heat. Start at Simple: one motor, one switch. Go Studio for batteries, propellers and the harvesters — including a radiator belly that turns waste heat into lift — then Pro for servo throws, control mixing, radiator finish and live in-flight trimming. Everything you fit changes the weight, the balance, and the flight — the simulator flies the plane you actually built.

Fit components

Tap a component to fit it or remove it. Weight is the tax on every fancy idea — watch the parts list and the aero readout argue about it.

Bigger battery = longer powered flight, more nose-to-tail weight to balance.

Big props bite more air at low speed; small props spin faster and suit darts.

How hard the motor pushes once you're airborne. Full throttle on a floaty glider mostly buys a loop.

How much of the underside radiates. A small panel under the motor and battery catches the hottest spots for the fewest grams. Half belly spreads it out. Whole bottom radiates EM along the full fuselage and wings' underside — most heat returned as lift, most weight and drag paid for it.

Permanent up/down flap angle. Up trim trades speed for climb before you ever throw.

How far the flaps can deflect. More throw = sharper turns and a much better chance of tearing a paper hinge.

Softens flap response near center stick — small corrections stay small. Twitchy plane? Add expo before you add blame.

How much sun the solar film can drink. Overcast still generates — just at a sip instead of a gulp.

How hard the generator brakes to make power. Max grab recovers most of what the motor spent — like an EV hauling its battery back up on every stop — but every watt grabbed is a watt of glide given up. Captured energy is never free; it's traded, not lost to heat.

How well the belly actually radiates. Shiny foil reflects and holds heat in; a matte black, high-emissivity finish throws heat away as infrared EM fastest — and downward radiation is the only kind that pushes you up.

Pro parts fitted automatically with RC: elevon mixing is ON — one servo pair drives both pitch and roll from the same rear flaps, exactly like a real flying-wing setup.

Lift is energy too: every meter of altitude is stored energy your folds bought. Glide down and that height is spent as distance — or, with the kinetic harvester fitted, partly captured back as charge on the way down. Climb, bank the height, spend it slow. That trade is the whole game of soaring.

And the heat of that fall is energy too: descending, braking and running the motor all end as heat if you let them. The radiator belly is the last capture in the chain — it takes heat that would just warm the air and radiates it downward as EM, so the fall itself pushes back up a little. In real physics radiation pressure is tiny; in this studio it's scaled up so you can see the trade and feel what covering the whole bottom buys — and what its grams cost.

    05What the flight shows you

    Every crash in the simulator is one of these four ideas, wearing a paper costume.

    Lift is a speed tax you pay gladly

    Wings make lift by pushing air down — and the faster you go, the more they make. That's why a gliding plane that slows down doesn't float to a stop; it sinks, dives to buy speed back, and porpoises along. Watch for that wave in your flight path.

    Lift (force) on Wikipedia →

    Center of gravity runs the show

    A paper plane rotates around its balance point. Weight ahead of the wing's lift center and it flies straight but dives. Weight behind it and the nose pitches up, stalls, and falls — the classic loop-and-crash. Paper clips on the nose are how real folders tune this.

    Center of mass on Wikipedia →

    Dihedral is self-correction, built in

    Wings in a shallow V catch more air on whichever side dips, rolling the plane level again — no pilot required. It's the same trick real aircraft use, and it's why the flattest, coolest-looking planes are often the worst fliers.

    Dihedral on Wikipedia →

    Drag is the bill for everything

    Every fold, edge, and square centimeter of wing charges you speed. Distance records go to designs that balance just enough lift against the least drag — a trade-off called glide ratio, and the real score of every flight you logged above.

    Glide ratio on Wikipedia →  ·  Paper planes on Wikipedia →

    Two ways to make power out of thin air

    Solar: cells on the wing turn sunlight into charge the whole time you're up — area and sunshine are the budget. Kinetic: a windmilling prop or ram-air turbine turns airflow back into electricity, the same trick airliners keep for emergencies and EVs use when braking. Fit both in the Studio and watch the battery gauge fight the motor to a draw.

    Solar cells on Wikipedia →  ·  Ram-air turbine on Wikipedia →  ·  Regenerative braking on Wikipedia →

    Regenerative braking: don't pay for heat

    An electric motor and a generator are the same machine facing opposite directions. Spin the wheels — or the prop — and the rotor's magnets sweep past the coils, breaking magnetic lines of force; that snap induces an electric current. Friction brakes take the energy you paid to create and throw it away as heat. A regenerative system grabs most of it back and puts it in the battery. That's the kinetic harvester in the Studio: on the glide, the windmilling prop drives the motor as a generator, and the plane slows because it's charging — the braking and the generating are one and the same act.

    Regenerative braking on Wikipedia →  ·  Electromagnetic induction on Wikipedia →

    Lift is energy — if you capture it

    A climbing plane is a battery being charged with height. Lift does the lifting, your throw (or motor) pays for it, and the glide spends it back as distance. Albatrosses cash this in for thousands of kilometers by dynamic soaring — harvesting energy from wind gradients instead of flapping. Your kinetic harvester plays the same game at paper scale: climb, store height, and capture some of it on the way down instead of letting drag keep all of it.

    Dynamic soaring on Wikipedia →  ·  Potential energy on Wikipedia →

    Even the heat of the fall can be used

    Follow the energy to the end of the chain and it always lands in the same place: heat. The motor warms, the battery warms, drag warms the skin, and a descending plane turns stored height into warm, stirred air. A radiator on the underside gives that heat one useful exit — radiating it downward as electromagnetic radiation, mostly infrared. Light carries momentum, so every bit of EM thrown downward pushes the plane up a little: radiation pressure, the same force that pushes solar sails. A small panel under the hot parts is the cheap version; making the whole bottom radiate is the full version. Real radiation pressure is tiny — the studio scales it up so the idea is visible — but the direction of the trade is honest: heat you radiate downward is heat that returns as lift, instead of heat that just leaves.
