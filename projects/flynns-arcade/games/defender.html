What the planet shows you

Defender (Williams Electronics, 1981) was the first famous game that was bigger than its screen. The world scrolls a full planet wide and wraps around, so the action is never all in front of you. Eugene Jarvis built in the answer: the long-range scanner across the top — an early video-game minimap — compresses the whole world into one strip. You don't watch the planet. You watch the picture of the planet, and dive where the picture says you're needed. That's the same move as every radar scope, network monitor, and dashboard since: radar thinking — a small true map beats a big partial view.

Rescue is a physics problem

A lander grabs a humanoid and climbs. Shoot the lander high up and the humanoid falls — and falling, past a certain height, is fatal. So the real play is interception: kill the carrier, then be underneath the falling person to catch them, then descend gently and set them down. Catch, carry, deliver. It's the same sequence as a helicopter rescue or a drone recovering a payload — the cargo can't take the drop, so your trajectory has to absorb it.

Triage, all the time

Ten humanoids, threats appearing on both sides of a world you can't fully see. You cannot be everywhere, so Defender is a game of choosing: which abduction do you answer, which do you let go? If a lander reaches the top with its captive, it comes back down as a mutant — faster, angrier, aimed at you. A problem ignored doesn't stay the same problem; it upgrades. And if all ten humanoids are lost, the planet explodes and you fight the next wave in empty space, with nothing left to protect and everything shooting at you.
