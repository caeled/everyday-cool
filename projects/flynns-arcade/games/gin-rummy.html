THE GAME, THE LESSON

Solitaire taught the deck against itself — pattern, sequence, patience. Gin Rummy is the same 52 cards with a second mind at the table. Nothing here is hidden except the other hand, and even that leaks: every card your opponent takes or throws is public information, written in the open. The player who reads the table best usually wins it.

The game

Gin Rummy appeared in 1909, sharpened from plain rummy by Elwood T. Baker and his son C. Graham Baker, and it became the card game of Broadway dressing rooms and Hollywood sets. Two players, ten cards each, one deck — the same deck Solitaire uses, pointed at a person instead of a puzzle.

The name is a pun: gin to match the older game of rum, a pair of drinks. At this table it's just points — a family card table, first to 100.

The lesson — sets & sequences

Your hand wants to become melds: sets of three or four of a kind, and runs of three or more in one suit. Everything that isn't in a meld is deadwood, and deadwood is weight you're carrying. The whole game is sorting — by kind, by order — under pressure, against someone doing the same sort faster.

The lesson — the public ledger

The discard pile is a public ledger. When the House picks up the 7♠, that's a receipt: it wants sevens, or spades, or both — and now you know not to feed it either. When the House throws a king, that's a receipt too: kings are safe to want. Nothing about the pile is secret. The only question is whether you were keeping the books.

The Ledger panel beside the table keeps them for you, out loud, as they happen — so you can learn to keep them yourself.

The lesson — when to knock

Knocking is an expected-value decision, the same shape as half the decisions in life. Knock at 10 or under and you take the sure, small win right now. Hold out for gin — zero deadwood — and the win is bigger: their full count plus 25. But every extra turn you wait, they might knock first, or knock so low you get undercut: they score the difference plus 25, and your patience becomes their points.

Sure thing now, or bigger thing later at a price. The game won't tell you which is right. It will keep the receipts either way.

How to play

  1. Ten cards each. One upcard starts the discard pile. The non-dealer may take it first; if both pass, the non-dealer draws from the stock to open.
  2. On your turn: draw the top of the stock or the top discard, then throw one card. You can't throw the card you just took from the pile on that same turn.
  3. Knock when your deadwood — after your throw — is 10 or less. Gin is knocking with zero.
  4. Against a knock, the defender lays off deadwood that extends the knocker's melds, and only the rest counts. Against gin, nothing lays off.
  5. Scoring: the knocker scores the deadwood difference. If the defender's count ties or beats the knocker's, the defender undercuts and scores the difference + 25. Gin scores the full count + 25.
  6. Stock down to 2 cards with no knock? The hand is a draw — same dealer redeals. First to 100 points takes the match.

Reading the House

The House plays a clean, readable game: it keeps its melds, holds its near-melds, throws its most useless high cards, and knocks when the count is low. It reads the same ledger you do — it notices what you pick up and stops feeding it, and it notices what you throw and relaxes about those ranks.

Which means your pickups are tells, too. Everything at this table is. That's the game.
