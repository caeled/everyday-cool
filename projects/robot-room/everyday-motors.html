Bench 1

What Makes It Spin?

Inside a brushed DC motor there are only three characters: a coil of wire, permanent magnets, and a clever little switch called a commutator that flips the current every half-turn. Push and pull between magnets = rotation. Power it on and watch — then slow it way down to catch the flip.

Watch the commutator: every time the coil passes vertical, the split ring swaps which brush touches which half — and the current arrows flip. That tiny flip is what keeps the spin going round and round.

🔧Takeaway: Electricity pushes the coil, magnets deflect it, the commutator flips the push — round and round.

Curious? Why does current spike when the motor stalls?

A spinning motor generates its own little voltage pushing back against the battery (engineers call it back-EMF) — that's what limits current while it spins happily. Stall the shaft and that push-back vanishes, so current jumps to the maximum the wire allows. More current = more heat = the smell of a cooked motor. The simulator shows this: watch the current gauge when you drag load to 100%.

🔧Takeaway: Voltage buys speed, but load eats it. Every motor has a sweet spot — and a stall point where it trades motion for heat.
