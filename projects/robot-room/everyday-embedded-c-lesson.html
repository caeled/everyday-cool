Everyday Embedded C — the lesson

The Java Young Robot Builders room teaches robots in a friendly language, with the machine politely hidden. This workshop is the door underneath it. A real robot brain — here, a model of the ATmega328P, the chip inside an Arduino Uno — has no digitalWrite underneath. It has registers: bytes in the silicon whose bits are switches wired straight to the pins and timers. C programs for small chips are mostly the art of flipping those switches in the right order. The bench walks the canonical first week of that art: blink an LED, discover why the obvious way of timing it is a trap, and hand the timing to hardware. This page is the other half: what the registers are, the formulas the bench is checked against, and the receipts from driving it before it shipped.

A register is a row of switches the code can flip

Inside the chip, certain memory addresses are not storage — they are control panels. Write a byte to the address named DDRD and you have not stored a number; you have set eight switches, one per pin of Port D, each deciding whether its pin is an output (the chip drives it) or an input (the pin listens). Write to PORTD and, for every pin that is an output, you have set its voltage: bit = 1 means 5 V on the pin, bit = 0 means 0 V. That is the whole secret of "embedded C" at this level — the program is ordinary C, but its variables are wired to the physical world, one bit at a time.

Two consequences run through everything else in this lesson. First, direction comes before data: a pin set as an input drives nothing, whatever its PORT bit says — the bench proves this (proof 8 below), and it is the cause of a large fraction of all real-world "my LED won't light" evenings. Second, the idioms look strange until you see the switches: DDRD |= (1 << PD7) means "set switch 7, leave the other seven alone," and PORTD ^= (1 << PD7) means "flip switch 7." Half of embedded C is those two moves.

Stage 1 — blink is the "hello world," and it earns the title

Every programming tradition has a first program that proves the whole toolchain works. In embedded C it is not printing text — there may be no screen — it is blinking an LED. If the LED blinks, then the compiler works, the code reached the chip, the clock runs, and you understand pins. The bench's Stage 1 is the canonical form: set PD7's direction bit once, then loop — flip the port bit, wait, flip it back.

The wait is _delay_ms(), and it deserves a hard look, because it is not a pause button. The chip has nothing to "sleep" with in this program, so the delay is a busy loop: the CPU counts clock cycles to itself until the time has passed. At the Uno's 16 MHz, the arithmetic is unforgiving:

cycles burned = F_CPU × delay = 16,000,000 × 0.5 s = 8,000,000 cycles One half-second delay. The blink it produces has period 2 × 500 ms = 1000 ms — a stately 1 Hz — and the CPU spent eight million cycles producing the "off" half of it. Measured on the bench's model in proof 7, exactly.

Stage 2 — why delay loops waste the chip

Shrink the delay to 1 ms and the blink runs at 500 Hz — far past the ~30 Hz where your eye stops seeing flicker — so the LED simply looks steadily half-lit. Notice what just happened: blink fast enough and a digital pin pretends to be an analog one. That is PWM, discovered by hand. But the price is total: during every one of those delays the CPU can do nothing else. A robot that times its LED this way cannot watch its sensors while it waits.

The bench's polling lab prices that blindness. A sensor blips five times in five seconds, each blip 250 ms long. A polling program sees a blip only if one of its looks lands inside the blip. Driven on the bench (proof 9):

Stage 3 — the timer: a counter that works for free

Timer2 is a small counter built into the silicon, wired to pins D3 and D11. It counts clock ticks — divided down by a prescaler you choose with three bits — and when its count reaches a compare register you set, it can flip its pin automatically. The CPU writes four registers once, at startup, and never thinks about timing again. Timer2's prescaler table has a quirk worth meeting early, because datasheets are full of them: its dividers are ÷1, ÷8, ÷32, ÷64, ÷128, ÷256, ÷1024 — the ÷32 and ÷128 exist on Timer2 and not on the chip's other timers (proof 1 decodes the whole table).

fFast PWM = F_CPU ÷ (N × 256)  ·  duty = (OCR2B + 1) ÷ 256 In Fast PWM the counter runs 0→255 and repeats; the pin turns on at the bottom and off at the compare match. So the frequency is set by the prescaler alone, and the duty by OCR2B alone — two knobs that do not interfere, which is why this mode runs the world's motors. At ÷64: 16,000,000 ÷ (64 × 256) = 976.5625 Hz, and one timer tick is 64 ÷ 16 MHz = 4 µs.

And the duty ladder (proof 4) holds one beginner-trapping subtlety: OCR2B = 0 is not 0%. The pin is switched on at the bottom of the count and off at the match, so it is on for OCR2B + 1 counts — at 0, one count in 256, 0.39%. Then 63 → 25%, 127 → 50%, 191 → 75%, 255 → a full 100%. The bench doesn't just assert this from the formula: proof 5 samples the pin's own waveform function 2,560 times across one period at OCR2B = 63 and measures exactly 25.00% high — the wave the page draws is the wave the formula promises.

ftoggle, CTC = F_CPU ÷ (2 × N × (1 + OCR2A)) CTC mode with the pin set to toggle is the hardware blink: the counter counts to OCR2A, flips the pin, starts over. At OCR2A = 124 and ÷1024, the pin toggles at exactly 62.5 Hz — a match every 8.00 ms (proof 6). But an 8-bit counter has a fence: even at the slowest prescaler and the largest compare value, the toggle rate bottoms out at 16,000,000 ÷ (2 × 1024 × 256) = 30.52 Hz, a 15.26 Hz blink. A hardware 1 Hz blink is impossible for this timer alone — real code counts timer overflows in an interrupt to go slower, and interrupts are one of the next doors named below.

Two more modes round out the bench's honesty. In phase-correct PWM the counter counts up and back down, so the pulse sits centred in the period and the frequency halves: f = F_CPU ÷ (N × 510), duty = OCR2B ÷ 255. At ÷64 that is 490.196 Hz — a number Arduino owners have met: it is the Uno's PWM frequency on pins 3 and 11, because the Arduino core runs Timer2 in exactly this mode. And with WGM22 set, OCR2A becomes the counter's TOP, so the frequency itself becomes programmable: proof 3 sets OCR2A = 99 at ÷8 and measures exactly 20,000 Hz with duty (49 + 1) ÷ (99 + 1) = 50%.

The Arduino contrast — the training wheels are these registers

None of this is hidden knowledge Arduino keeps from you; it is simply underneath. On an Uno, pin 3 is PD3, Timer2's OC2B output. When you call analogWrite(3, 128), the Arduino core has already (in its init(), before your code runs) set Timer2's mode and prescaler, and your call writes 128 into OCR2B. The bench's Stage 3 preset is the same act performed openly: COM2B1 set, Fast PWM chosen, prescaler ÷64, OCR2B = 127 → 50% duty at 976.5625 Hz — and the generated code pane reads the C back off whatever bits you set, so the correspondence runs both ways. Likewise delay() is Stage 1 with better manners, and pinMode(7, OUTPUT) is the DDRD bit with the register's name filed off. Elliot Williams' line — there is no Arduino "language," only C over avr-libc with the initialisation done for you — is the workshop's whole thesis, and it is in the references.

Why bother taking the wheels off? Because the wheels only reach where their maker pointed them. The day a robot needs a 20 kHz PWM the motors won't whine at, or a blink the CPU doesn't have to perform, or two timers that don't fight over a pin, the friendly functions run out and the datasheet is what's left. This bench is practice for that day, on the friendliest possible terms.

Honest notes

One chip, simplified — the simplifications, named. The bench models one port (D) and one timer (Timer2) of the ATmega328P. The real chip also has Ports B and C, Timer0, a 16-bit Timer1, a USART, an ADC, and more. Timer2 was chosen because it drives the Arduino's pin 3 and because its prescaler table is the quirky one — but nothing here should be read as the whole chip.

The next doors are named, not simulated: interrupts and analog input. A compare-match or overflow interrupt lets the timer tap the CPU on the shoulder — that is how real code blinks at 1 Hz (the slowest timer event here fires about 30 times a second, so a handler that counts a few dozen of them owns a second), watches buttons without polling, and still runs PWM untouched. The ADC — reading a sensor's voltage as a number — is the other missing half of every real robot program. Both belong to later workshops; this one says so rather than faking them.

The delay model is idealised. The bench prices _delay_ms(ms) at exactly F_CPU × ms ÷ 1000 cycles. The real avr-libc delay is a calibrated instruction loop that lands close to that — the library's own documentation calls its delays approximate, and the exact count shifts with the value requested. The lesson's point (millions of cycles, all of them wasted) survives the approximation easily; the third significant figure does not.

Small print, kept visible. COM2B = 01 in a PWM mode is a datasheet special case the waveform model doesn't draw (the bench steers you to 00, 10, 11, and to 01 in CTC where it means toggle). The FOC bits are write-only strobes on real hardware and decorative here. The drawn waveform is slowed down by roughly the ratio between 976 Hz and your screen's refresh — its shape is sampled from the same pin function the proofs measure, but no screen could show you the real pace. LED brightness in the model is linear in duty; your eye is roughly logarithmic, so a real 50% LED looks brighter than half. And the motor bar's "speed follows average voltage" is the steady-state cartoon — the Motors workshop is where back-EMF makes that honest.
