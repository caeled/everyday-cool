Everyday Control — the lesson

The workshop is a line-following robot and three knobs. Underneath it is the single most useful idea in robotics: don't compute the perfect move — measure the error, correct a fraction of it, measure again. The controller never sees the world. It sees one number — the error — and everything it does is a function of that number's present, its past, and its rate of change. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the plant, the controller, the measured behaviour of every preset, and the honest story of how this workshop reached the shelf.

Workshop designed and written by Qwen (Kimi) at Steven Powell's direction; completed by Muse under Steven's sanction — see Provenance, next.

Provenance — what arrived, and what was built here

The workshop arrived from Qwen's studio the way its sibling Motors did, only more so: the design, the copy and Benches 1–5 were all present and the teaching was sound — but the file was truncated. It contained no script at all: no simulation, no Bench 6, no closing sections, no footer. Every Run button was inert, every slider decorative, and its four tuning presets were behavioural claims about a machine that did not exist in the file. The audit is on record (audit-qwen-control.md), and its readiness call was not ready — not patch-sized.

Steven chose salvage over scrap: keep Qwen's workshop, complete it here. So Benches 1–5 stand as she wrote them — same design, same voice — with a short list of corrections where her copy disagreed with itself or with the machine that now ships (each one is listed in the corrections table below). Everything that moves is the completion: the simulation, Bench 6, the closing sections, the self-checks, and every line of script. The preset labels were then measured against the finished simulation, and two presets were re-tuned so the labels tell the truth. Nothing in this lesson is asserted: every number was measured from the shipped code, and the workshop re-measures ten of them live when you press its self-check button.

The loop in one picture

A thermostat holds a room, cruise control holds a speed, you hold a bicycle upright. Each one measures, compares with a target, and pushes back. The thermostat is the humble ancestor — full on, full off, temperature forever hunting around the setpoint (Bench 4's wobble bot is that controller with wheels). PID is what you build when hunting isn't good enough. The whole subject is one picture:

e = setpoint − measurement The only number the controller ever sees: how wrong things are, and in which direction. On this bench the setpoint is "line, centred" and the measurement is the line sensor's offset — so e is the line's displacement from centre, normalised: e = −m / 40, with m in track units.
u = Kp·e  +  Ki·∫e dt  −  Kd·(dm/dt)/40 Push for how wrong it is now (P), plus how long it's been wrong (I), plus how fast the wrongness is changing (D). Note the D term's shape: it differentiates the measurement m, not the error — the standard fix for derivative kick, because the setpoint can jump but the measurement can't. u is a steering rate, clamped to ±2.6 rad/s.

On a real controller the calculus is bookkeeping: each tick, add a little rectangle (integral += e·dt) and divide a difference by the tick ((m − m_prev)/dt). That is why dt appears in both terms — and why the loop's clock matters, below.

The plant — the robot the knobs steer

The simulation runs in the line's own frame, at a fixed step. Stated exactly, it is this:

ẏ = v·sin θ  ·  θ̇ = u_act − κ(s)·v·cos θ + w − c·θ  ·  m = y + L·sin θ y is the robot's lateral offset from the line, θ its heading relative to the line's tangent, v = 46 units/s. The line curves as drawn — a sine of amplitude 60 units and wavelength 800 — and κ(s) is that curve's curvature, so the line itself is a moving target the loop must track. The sensor sits L = 5 units ahead of the drive axle, which is what m measures. The steering reaches the wheels through a first-order lag (τ = 0.18 s — motors can't change turn rate instantly), the tyres scrub away turn rate at c = 0.4 /s, and w is an optional constant veer — the dragging wheel — of 0.3 rad/s when its box is ticked. dt = 0.05 s: the loop runs twenty times a second, on a fixed step, never on the animation clock. Proof 1 counts it: 10 s of sim time is exactly 200 steps.

P — a spring with no brakes

Proportional control is the obvious idea: steer in proportion to the error. On this robot its failure is not the textbook one — it's the wobble. There is almost no damping in the steering loop (a whisper of tyre scrub is all), so every correction overshoots: measured on a straight line, P alone (1, 0, 0) swings to −16.8 units past the line, crosses it 7 times in 20 s, and needs 12.3 s to settle. On the curving track it never settles at all — late-run RMS error 5.42 units, still hunting at ±7.6. That is why the preset is labelled "wobbles," and why Bench 5's Curious panel now says so in that order.

The textbook failure is real too — it just needs a constant push to show itself. Give the robot the dragging wheel (a constant veer w = 0.3 rad/s) and P with D parks a full 7.50 units off the line, permanently. Holding against a constant push takes a constant steering output; at zero error, P produces zero output; so the loop parks exactly where the leftover error generates the needed push:

y_offset = w / Kp × 40 = 0.3 / 1.6 × 40 = 7.50 units Theory and measurement agree to the second decimal — the sim's P+D robot sits at a mean of +7.50 units under the veer. (The famous formula ess = setpoint/(1 + Kp·K) is the same story for a type-0 plant; this robot's steering integrates, so its version of the leftover appears against disturbances instead. The audit flagged the original panel for telling the type-0 story as if it were universal; the panel now tells this robot's measured one.)

I — memory that finishes the job, and its grudge

The integral is memory: it sums the error over time, so a small error that persists grows a push until the plant moves. Against the dragging wheel, the full PID rides at a mean of 0.34 units — the 7.50-unit leftover, swept up. The cost is momentum: the sum keeps pushing after the target is reached, so PI (1.4, 0.25, 0) overshoots to −24.3 units — further than P alone — and takes 23.3 s to settle. And memory has a darker failure, the one the workshop's windup chip is named for:

Windup. Pin the robot off the line (the Shove & pin button: held at +34 units for 4 s, steering saturated, error unable to shrink) and the integral piles up credit for work the wheels cannot do. Measured: the integral reaches −3.42 (Ki·∫ = −2.05). Released, that pile dumps all at once — the robot swings to −185 units past the line. The bench's defence is the professional one, an anti-windup clamp: the integral is capped at |Ki·∫| ≤ 0.9, all the steering can use and no more. Same pin with the clamp on: integral stops at −1.50, and the release swing is −46.2 units — a 75% cut. The clamp is invisible in honest running: the sweet spot on the curve peaks at |Ki·∫| = 0.114, far under the cap. It only wakes up when the robot is pinned, which is exactly when it should.

D — brakes that watch the future

The derivative reacts to how fast the error is changing — approaching fast, it leans the other way before arrival. It is the difference between the wobble and the glide: add D to the mix (the Sweet spot: 1.6, 0.12, 1.4) and the same robot, on the same line, settles in 4.0 s with an overshoot of just −3.2 units, then holds the curving track at an RMS error of 3.21 units (worst excursion 4.8). D's known vice — amplifying sensor noise — is named in the honest box; this sim's sensor is exact, so the bench shows D's virtue undiluted and says so.

The presets, measured — including the two that had to change

Qwen shipped four preset buttons with behavioural labels and no machine behind them. The completion measured each one. Two labels were already true of the finished sim. Two were not, and those presets were re-tuned — the smallest change that makes the label honest — rather than relabelled, because the four-step arc (wobble → overshoot → sweet → sluggish) is the bench's teaching spine.

Why the two re-tunes were forced, in numbers: at Ki = 0.5 the PI preset never settles in this plant — late RMS 16.10 units, still swinging ±23.8 at 45 s; "overshoots" would have been a euphemism for "unstable." And the shipped over-damped gains (2.4, 0.2, 2.6) were, in the event, the fastest tune on the bench — settled in 2.1 s, beating the sweet spot — because heavy D on the measurement is a superb brake and the high P snapped it home. The audit had flagged exactly that preset ("the highest P of the set, labelled sluggish") as a claim the page couldn't cash. Now it can: the re-tuned set is genuinely all brake and no hurry.

Tuning by recipe — Ziegler–Nichols, and its reputation

The workshop teaches tuning by feel, which is a legitimate road. The other road is the 1942 recipe: zero I and D, raise P until the loop rings at a steady amplitude — that gain is Ku, the ring's period is Tu — and read the gains off the table:

ControllerKpTiTd
P0.5·Ku——
PI0.45·KuTu / 1.2—
PID0.6·KuTu / 2Tu / 8

with Ki = Kp/Ti and Kd = Kp·Td. Know its reputation before you use it: the table aims at quarter-amplitude decay, which is an aggressive tune — a starting point you then soften, not a verdict. On this bench you can find the ring by hand (P alone, keep raising it); the gentler road — one term at a time, watching what each buys — is the CTMS tutorial on the reference shelf, and the ritual in the workshop's closing cards.

The loop runs on a clock

The integral is a sum of error × dt; the derivative is a difference ÷ dt. Run the loop twice as fast without rescaling and you have quietly retuned it; run it on the animation clock — as many sim pages do — and the "gains" are married to the visitor's monitor. This bench steps its plant at a fixed dt = 0.05 s and accumulates real time into whole steps, so a 120 Hz screen and a 60 Hz screen run the same robot. Proof 1 is the receipt: 200 steps in 10.000000 s of sim time, counted, not assumed.

The other PID bench — a fair comparison

Everyday Physics holds Antigravity's PID Inverted Pendulum — the same subject from the other guest builder, and the comparison the audit could only half-make while this bench had no machine. Now both sides are measurable. Antigravity's plant is the realer physics: exact nonlinear Lagrangian cart–pole dynamics, RK4-integrated, with an inspectable PID, an anti-windup toggle and actuator saturation; its recorded defect is behavioural — the swing-up never catches — inside dynamics that genuinely integrate. This bench's plant is humbler (a kinematic line-follower, not a dynamics derivation), and its controller is the plainer teaching instrument: every preset's label is a measured number in the table above, and its windup is not a toggle's promise but a demonstrated 185-to-46 before-and-after. Different plants, different lessons: the pendulum teaches that control can hold an unstable thing up; this one teaches what the three knobs do to a robot that has to follow the world. The collection's own Feedback Bench in Nerd Heaven draws the same loop from the wiring side — Control's arithmetic lives inside Feedback's diagram.

Bends named honestly

The sensor is perfect. The measurement is the true offset, every tick, exactly. A real robot reads an encoder with finite counts, a camera with frame latency, a line sensor with a threshold and noise — and noise is exactly what makes D dangerous in the field. This bench filters nothing because it has nothing to filter; it says so rather than pretending the problem away.

No backlash. A real gearbox has slop: reverse the motor and nothing moves for a few degrees while the teeth cross the gap. Inside that dead zone the loop is open and doesn't know it — a classic limit cycle that beginners swear is a tuning problem. The sim's drivetrain is tight; the wobble you see here is the controller's, not the gearbox's.

Saturation is a chosen number. The ±2.6 rad/s steering limit is a line in the code. The real limit is a battery that sags and a driver chip with a current ceiling (Motors' stall beat). Windup happens at the real limit whether or not the code knows it exists — the bench's pin is a clean, visible stand-in for a messier truth.

The plant is a polite fiction. Kinematic, linearised where it matters, with lag and scrub as single constants. It teaches the shape of tuning — what each knob buys and what it costs — not a set of gains to carry to a real robot. Real tuning is iterative precisely because the model is a polite fiction: you tune, the world corrects you, you tune again.

dt in the wild. The sim steps when it steps; a real loop shares a microcontroller with sensor reads, radio traffic and someone else's blocking delay. Jitter in dt is jitter in Ki and Kd — Beauregard's sample-time discipline, on the reference shelf, is the professional answer.

Safety is part of the subject. A loop with the sign wrong is not "untuned" — it is a machine driving itself to the rail at full power. First power-up of any real loop: small gains, output limits set, wheels off the ground, stop within reach. The sim can't teach the flinch; this list is the checklist instead.
