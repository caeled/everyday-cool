Everyday Simulation — the lesson

Before a real robot is allowed to move, its software rehearses in a simulated world — Gazebo, Webots, a thousand crashed virtual robots for every one that ships. This bench is that rehearsal room at tabletop scale: a maze, a two-wheeled robot, three distance rays, and one uncomfortable measurement at its centre — the robot's own diary of where it has been is wrong, by an amount you can watch grow. This page is the other half: what the model is, what was measured, and where the seams are.

Why simulate before you build

A real robot is slow to forgive. It has mass, it falls down stairs, its battery dies mid-test, and every bug in its steering becomes a dent in something you own. A simulator inverts all of that: the world is free, the robot is weightless until you say so, a crash costs one reset button, and — the part professionals actually rely on — the experiment is repeatable. Run the same maze a hundred times and a deterministic simulator gives the same answer a hundred times, so when the answer changes, you changed something. That is why this bench's auto-driver proof runs every maze twice and passes only if the two runs agree to the step.

Simulation has a price, and this lesson pays it openly: a simulator is a model, not the world. Everything below is true of the model — a perfect 2D grid, exact sensors, one tidy slip knob. The skill being taught is not "trust the sim." It is "know exactly which promises your sim makes, and measure whether it keeps them." That is what the proofs are for.

The world and the robot, in numbers

The floor is a grid of 20 × 14 cells at 0.5 m each — a room 10 m × 7 m. The robot is a circle of radius 0.18 m with two driven wheels 0.30 m apart, each capped at 0.60 m/s. The physics advances in fixed steps of 1/120 s, whatever the screen's refresh rate does, and each step is integrated with the exact arc equations — not a straight-line nibble:

v = (vL + vR) / 2   ·   ω = (vR − vL) / 0.30 m The differential-drive model: the robot's forward speed is the average of its wheels; its turn rate is their difference over the track width. For constant wheel speeds the path is a circular arc of radius R = v/ω — the same model, with the same constants, as the Python Robotics bench, so the room agrees with itself.

Three distance rays fan out at −35°, 0°, +35° from the heading, reaching 3.0 m. Each is computed by walking the wall grid (an Amanatides & Woo traversal) to the first wall cell; the crossing distance is the measurement. Collisions resolve by pushing the robot's circle out of any wall cell along the shortest escape, so the robot slides along walls. Both claims — the rays and the sliding — are measured in the proof table, not asserted.

Odometry — the diary vs the world

Ask a simple robot where it is and it answers from odometry: it adds up its own wheel rotations and integrates the same arc equations. That diary is exact arithmetic — and it is only as true as the wheels' contact with the floor. The bench keeps both books side by side: the solid robot is integrated from the actual wheel speeds (after the slip knob takes its cut) and collides with walls; the dashed robot is the diary, integrated from the commanded speeds, and it never collides — wheels spinning against a wall still write in a real robot's diary, and they write in this one too.

The centrepiece measurement: command both wheels to 0.40 m/s for three seconds — "drive straight" — with the left wheel slipping 15%. The left wheel really turns at 0.34 m/s, so the truth arcs gently left at ω = 0.06 / 0.30 = 0.20 rad/s around a circle of radius 1.85 m, ending 34.4° off its starting heading. The diary, which heard only the command, reports a dead-straight 1.2 m. Measured gap between the two after three seconds: 0.3586 m — and the proof computes the predicted gap from the arc equations independently and gets 0.3586 m, with the simulated true pose landing within 7.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ m of the predicted endpoint. At 0% slip the same experiment leaves a gap of exactly 0. The diary is never wrong because its sums are wrong; it is wrong because the world didn't do what the wheels were told. Every real navigation stack — the sensor fusion in the ROS 2 bench, the ray geometry in Everyday Sensors — exists to keep correcting that diary against the world.

The auto-driver, and its honest record

The auto-driver is a right-hand wall follower: keep the right ray near 0.45 m, lean left as the front ray shortens, spin left when a touch is imminent, arc right when the wall is lost. It reads only the three rays — the same numbers the visitor sees in the sensor panel; there is no map and no cheating channel. Wall following is a real strategy with a theorem-shaped shadow: it reaches any goal that touches the wall system it is following, and it can never reach a goal that doesn't. The three preset mazes were chosen to show both halves of that sentence, and the record below is measured — re-run live, in your browser, every time the bench's proof table fills:

MazeWhat it testsMeasured record
First RoomOpen floor, a few blocks, goal in the far corner against the wallsSOLVED in 27.5 s
The CorridorThree full-width barriers, gaps alternating sides — the follower must traverse every bandSOLVED in 85.8 s

The Island's failure is the lesson, not an embarrassment: the follower orbits the room and the island faithfully for four minutes and never comes within reach of a goal floating in open floor, because nothing in its rule can make it leave a wall. Beating that maze honestly takes a different idea — a map, a memory, a plan — which is precisely the step from this bench to real navigation.

The headless-vs-eyes split: the core — raycasts, integration, collision, odometry, the auto-driver's full record — is proven by script, above. The page around it was driven in jsdom with the canvas stubbed: buttons, modes, the drift test and the proof table all exercised. What no script here can certify is the drawn picture itself; that receipt is the visitor's own open of the bench, as with its sibling exhibits.

The seams, stated: the grid world is perfect and the map is never wrong; the rays are exact, with no noise, blur or dropouts (real distance sensors have all three); the wheels answer instantly; slip is one steady knob on one wheel, while real slip changes by the second; and the wall follower is a teaching controller, not a planner. Inside those seams, every number on this page was measured from the shipped core.

How the exhibit is built

One self-contained HTML file, no libraries, nothing fetched — the collection runs offline. The simulation core (world, raycast, integrator, collision, odometry, follower, proofs) is a single marked block with no DOM references, which is what lets the same code be extracted and examined in Node at build time and re-run by the bench's own proof table in the browser. The page around it is only drawing and buttons: canvas rendering, a fixed-timestep loop fed by an accumulator (so the physics rate never depends on the display), keyboard and touch-pad driving, maze editing with a browser-local autosave, and the lesson overlay. Determinism is a design rule, not an accident: no random numbers anywhere in the core.
