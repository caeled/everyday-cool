Everyday ROS 2 — the lesson

The bench draws a small robot's brain the way ROS 2 organises it: five little programs — nodes — that never call each other directly, connected only by named streams of notes called topics, plus one private telephone line called a service. This page is the other half: why real robots are built as a crowd of small programs instead of one big one, the counting rules that make the bench's numbers exact, and the receipts from driving it. Readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button on the bench.

Why a robot is many small programs

The beginner's first robot program is usually one loop: read the camera, find the thing, plan a path, drive the motors, repeat. It works — until it doesn't, and then everything is broken at once, because everything is the same program. ROS 2's answer is to split the loop into its natural pieces and let each piece be its own program: a camera node that only publishes pictures, a detector node that only reads pictures and publishes what it found, a planner, a motor driver. Each can be started, stopped, restarted, replaced or written by a different person, because none of them knows the others exist. They know only the names of the topics.

This collection already has a bench about passing notes: the Secret Squares' Dead Drop, a sneakernet workbench built on the observation that sometimes the models need to pass notes in class. ROS is what happens when the note-passing grows up and gets a postal service: every note has a named slot it travels through, anyone may read a slot by subscribing to it, and the post office keeps count. The bench's topic table is that count, kept in the open.

Topics — shouting into a named room

To publish is to drop a message into a named topic and walk away. To subscribe is to have every message on that topic delivered to you. The publisher does not know who is listening — on the bench, /camera would publish exactly the same frames if the detector were deleted, and the proofs lean on that: the chain works because of the names, not because of any wiring between the programs themselves. This decoupling — in space (any machine), in time (any start order), in number (one publisher, many subscribers; the bench proves two publishers sharing /cmd_vel) — is the whole architectural idea, and it predates ROS: it is the publish–subscribe pattern, in the references.

The bench's chain is the canonical robot pipeline, four hops long:

/camera —/image→ /detector —/detections→ /planner —/cmd_vel→ /motor_driver Each arrow is a topic; each hop costs 2 steps (0.10 s) on the wire in this model, and a node reacts in the step its message arrives. The planner's orders are a velocity pair (v, ω) — the Twist idea: "forward this fast, turning this fast." The driver is a unicycle: θ grows by ω·dt, position by v·dt along the heading.

The counting rules — why the numbers are exact

A simulator that says "about ten a second" teaches nothing. This one counts, and its counting rules are printed on the bench and repeated here. The clock is fixed: dt = 0.05 s, twenty steps per sim second, whatever the screen does. A publisher at rate r keeps a fractional accumulator — every step it adds r × dt, and publishes whenever the total crosses a whole number:

published ≈ rate × elapsed  (± 1 message) The ±1 is honest: a step boundary can hold a fraction of a message in the accumulator, so over any run the count is the floor of rate × elapsed, never more than one short. Proof 1 measures it at three rates — 7 Hz for 30 s published 210, 20 Hz for 10 s published 200, 1 Hz for 30 s published 30 — dead on, all three. And the ledger always balances: at any moment, published = delivered + dropped + still on the wire, for every topic. Proof 2 checks the chain's version of it: 99 images delivered, exactly 99 detections published; 98 detections delivered, exactly 98 planner orders. Nothing is created or lost between the counters.

Stop the camera — how robot software actually fails

The bench's big red button is its main lesson. Pause the camera and nothing is told to anybody — yet the robot stops. What happens, step by step, is the standard failure shape of every real robot pipeline: the messages already on the wire still arrive (at the stop in proof 3: one image, one detection, one order in flight), each downstream node does its last piece of work (+1 detection, +2 orders, +3 deliveries — counted exactly), and then the driver's last order goes stale: it obeys a /cmd_vel for only 0.5 s after receiving it, so silence reads as "stop," which is the safe reading. The robot had driven 2.79 m; its pose then stays bit-identical for as long as you care to watch.

That 0.5 s freshness rule deserves its name: it is a watchdog, and real motor drivers ship with one for exactly this reason — a robot whose planner has crashed should coast to a stop, not drive forever on its last order. And the debugging method falls out of the same picture: when a ROS robot misbehaves, you walk the graph asking each topic are you flowing? — ros2 topic hz in real life, the counts table on the bench — and the first quiet topic is where the failure lives. Everything downstream of it is not broken. It is starved. There is a difference, and it is most of the job.

Services — the second shape, and actions, the third door

Topics are broadcasts: one-way, to whoever is listening, with no reply. Some conversations don't fit that shape. "Reset yourself, and tell me when it's done" wants exactly one answer from exactly one node. That is a service: a request and a response, between two parties, off the topics entirely. The bench's /reset proves the separation rather than asserting it — proof 4 calls it three times against a running robot and checks that every topic total and the message log are unchanged by the calls, while the pose comes back exactly (0, 0, 0). If a number had moved, the shapes would be leaking into each other, and the proof would say so.

ROS 2 has a third shape the bench names but does not simulate: actions. A service is for quick questions; an action is for jobs that take time — "drive to the kitchen" — where the caller wants progress reports along the way (feedback), the final outcome (result), and the right to cancel halfway. Under the hood an action is built from topics and services, which is why the honest order to learn them is the order on this page: broadcasts first, conversations second, jobs third. The official tutorial is in the references.

QoS — how strong a promise does a topic make?

Buried in real ROS 2 is a set of policies called Quality of Service, and the first of them is reliability. A reliable topic promises: every message arrives, or the sender keeps trying. A best-effort topic promises nothing: fire and forget, and some messages simply never land. Best effort sounds like the sloppy option until you price the alternative — for a camera feeding a detector at 20 frames a second, a guaranteed-but-late frame is worse than a dropped one, because the next frame is already coming. Real sensor drivers choose best effort on purpose; real command channels usually choose reliable. The bench models the one knob: on /image, best effort drops each frame with a 15% chance, and the topic table counts the corpses.

The drops are seeded — a mulberry32 generator with a fixed seed — so the same run always loses the same frames, and proof 5 can print an exact number instead of a shrug: at 20 Hz for 100 s, 2,000 published, 290 dropped (14.50%), 1,710 delivered, the delivered count exactly published − dropped once the wire drains, and a second run with the same seed drops the same 290. The 14.50% against the nominal 15% is what an honest dice roll looks like at n = 2,000 — close to the rate, not glued to it, and the proof reports the measured figure rather than rounding it into the claim.

Who drives? — two publishers and a freshest-wins rule

Proof 7 establishes that the planner and your joystick can publish /cmd_vel simultaneously — 298 orders in ten seconds, 98 planner + 200 joystick, the counts adding exactly. So who is driving? The bench's driver holds only the freshest order it has received and obeys that one: last message wins. Because the joystick publishes at 20 Hz — faster than orders arrive through the camera chain — its message is always the freshest while teleop is on, and proof 6 measures the consequence in the robot's own heading: source planner → joy → planner as the joystick toggles, and under joy the heading grows by exactly the commanded ω: Δθ = 0.800 rad in 1.0 s. Real robots often insert a proper arbitrator (twist_mux) with priorities and timeouts instead of raw freshness — named here because the bench's simpler rule is a choice, not the only design.

Bends named honestly

A model of the ideas, not ROS 2 itself. Nothing here speaks DDS, discovers other nodes on a network, or runs more than one process. Real ROS 2's middleware handles discovery, transport, and QoS negotiation between machines; this page keeps the shapes — nodes, topics, services, one QoS knob — and drops the plumbing. What transfers is the graph and the failure shapes, which is most of what a beginner debugs.

Nodes react in the step their message arrives. The bench's detector is instantaneous: a frame delivered at step s produces its detections at step s. Real vision takes milliseconds — often longer than the frame interval — so real pipelines queue, lag, or shed frames. The bench's chain therefore has less latency than any real one: two steps per hop, always, where a real hop is a distribution, not a constant.

One QoS knob of a family. Real QoS also sets history (keep-last depth — a slow subscriber silently overwrites old messages, a loss this model never has), durability, and deadlines; and a reliable subscriber paired with a best-effort publisher simply never connects in real ROS 2 — a compatibility trap this single-bus model cannot express, since every subscriber here hears every non-dropped message.

Messages are summaries, not structs. Real messages are typed — an Image carries real pixels, a Twist six numbers. The bench's payloads carry the two or three fields the next node actually reads (frame number, box count, v and ω) plus a line of text for the echo. The counts are real; the cargo is a label saying what the cargo would be.

Arbitration is freshness, stated. Last-message-wins is the bench's rule, printed on the bench. It makes the 20 Hz joystick win by construction. A priority mux would make the joystick win by rank instead — a different, also legitimate design, and the difference matters on robots where a safety stop must outrank everything regardless of freshness.

The arena window follows the robot. The little top-down view is a camera centred on the robot, not a walled garden; the odometer readouts keep the true pose when the trail runs long. The robot itself is a unicycle with no wheel slip, no motor lag, and no floor — kinematics only, because this bench is about the conversation, not the driving. (The Robot Room's Motors and Control workshops are about the driving.)

Packets on the graph are slowed for the eye. The glowing dots take 0.38 s to cross an edge whatever the sim says; the sim's own wire time is 2 steps (0.10 s). Believe the counts, not the dots.
