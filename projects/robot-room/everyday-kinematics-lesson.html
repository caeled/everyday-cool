Everyday Kinematics — the lesson

The exhibit is a two-link robot arm — an upper arm of 120 units on a shoulder joint, a forearm of 100 units on an elbow joint — and it answers the two questions every robot arm lives between. Forward kinematics: given the joint angles, where is the hand? Inverse kinematics: given where the hand should go, what are the joint angles? The first is arithmetic. The second is the one robots earn their living on — and this page is its other half: the triangle that solves it, the two answers every target has, the ring the hand can live in, and what an honest bench does when you ask for a point outside it.

The bench was built by Muse for Everyday Cool's Robot Room, at Steven Powell's direction. It is one of the room's six Muse-built workshops, alongside three from a guest builder — and like everything in this collection, its claims below were driven and measured before they were printed.

Forward kinematics — the easy direction

Pose the arm with the sliders and the hand's position is already decided. Each link contributes its length pointed along its own direction: the upper arm along θ1, the forearm along θ1 + θ2 (the elbow angle is measured relative to the upper arm — the way a real elbow works, and the way servo code usually counts it). Add the two contributions, x and y separately:

elbow = (L1·cos θ1,  L1·sin θ1)
hand = elbow + (L2·cos(θ1 + θ2),  L2·sin(θ1 + θ2)) With L1 = 120 and L2 = 100. The bench opens at θ1 = 35°, θ2 = −55°: the elbow lands at (98.30, 68.83), the forearm points along −20°, and the hand reads (192.27, 34.63) — check it against the bench's first readout; it agrees, because the readout is this formula. There is exactly one answer, it is exact, and nothing about it can go wrong except typing it in wrong. FK is the direction you use to check the other direction — which is exactly what the proofs below do.

Inverse kinematics — the working question

A working robot is never handed angles. It is handed a job: put the hand there. Going from "there" back to angles is inverse kinematics, and for two links it collapses into one picture: the base, the elbow, and the target are the three corners of a triangle whose sides you know — L1, L2, and the target's distance d. A triangle with all three sides known has all three angles known, and the law of cosines is the tool that reads them off:

cos θ2 = (d² − L1² − L2²) / (2·L1·L2)
θ1 = atan2(y, x) − atan2(L2·sin θ2,  L1 + L2·cos θ2) The elbow angle falls out of the distance alone. The shoulder angle is the target's own direction, atan2(y, x), minus a correction — the angle the forearm's sideways reach steals from the shoulder. (atan2, the two-argument arctangent, is doing quiet load-bearing work here: it knows which quadrant the target is in, where a plain atan would guess.) No iteration, no initial guess, no convergence to hope for: the solver is a closed form, and it returns the same answer every time.

Worked once, with the bench's own preset target (120, 80): the distance is d = √(120² + 80²) = 144.22, so cos θ2 = (20800 − 14400 − 10000) / 24000 = −0.15, and θ2 = ±98.63°. Taking the elbow-up answer, θ2 = −98.63°: the target's direction is atan2(80, 120) = 33.69°, the correction is atan2(−98.89, 105) = −43.29°, and θ1 = 33.69° − (−43.29°) = 76.98°. Run FK on (76.98°, −98.63°) and the hand lands on (120, 80) — the audit's drive of the shipped page read θ2 = −98.63° off the bench for exactly this target, and flipping to elbow-down read +98.63° with the hand unmoved. Which brings us to the two answers.

Every reachable target has two answers

The ± in θ2 = ±98.63° is not sloppiness; it is geometry. The triangle can fold with the elbow on either side of the base-to-target line, and both folds put the hand in precisely the same place. For (120, 80): elbow-up is (θ1, θ2) = (76.98°, −98.63°), elbow parked high at (27.03, 116.92); elbow-down is (−9.60°, +98.63°), elbow slung low. Same hand, different arm. Real controllers choose a family and stick to it — the choice decides whether the elbow clears the table, the workpiece, or the robot's own cabling — and the bench's Elbow up / Elbow down buttons put that choice in your hands, where it belongs. The two families meet at the workspace rim: as the target approaches full stretch, the two elbows converge, and at d = 220 exactly there is only one answer left, θ2 = 0. That meeting point is a singularity, and it gets its own section below.

The workspace — a ring, with a hole in it

The hand cannot go just anywhere. Fully stretched, the arm spans L1 + L2 = 220 units; folded flat against itself, the forearm reaches back over the upper arm and the hand still sits |L1 − L2| = 20 units from the base. Between those radii: everything. Outside them: nothing. The reachable set is a ring — an annulus — with a dead zone around the base that beginners never expect: a two-link arm cannot touch its own shoulder, because the short link cannot fold into the long one. Proof 7 below verifies the ring by brute sampling — 14,400 poses, none escaping 20…220, with both rims attained exactly at the straight and folded poses — and the bench draws the ring on the table so the boundary is something you can see before you run into it.

When the answer doesn't exist — degrade gracefully, and say so

Ask the bench for (300, 40) — the fire-coloured preset does — and there is no answer. What happens next is a design decision, and this bench's decision is printed on its face: the target slides along its own ray to the rim at 220, the arm solves for that, and the status line reports unreachable — too far, with the hand's shortfall (82.65 units for that preset) in the readouts. Dead-zone targets get the same honesty inward: folded flat, hand on the inner ring, shortfall named. Nothing freezes, nothing returns NaN, and the arm visibly does the best that geometry allows while saying it is doing so.

That behaviour is a choice, and the collection contains the other choice, measured. Everyday Physics holds a guest-built arm bench (Kinematics — Robotic Arm Inverse Kinematics, by Antigravity) whose forward kinematics its lesson verifies exact to under a millimetre and whose iterative solvers land reachable targets at 0.0–0.3 mm — genuinely fine work. But at a fully-stretched target on the singular axis, its damped-least-squares and CCD solvers, in its lesson's own words, freeze forever: the gradient that drives them vanishes at full stretch, so they receive no signal and sit still. Neither behaviour is a moral failing — iterative solvers earn their keep on arms with many joints, where no closed form exists — but a beginner's first arm should fail the way this one does: out loud, with the shortfall printed. Breakage is the only honest audit, and a solver that tells you it fell short can be audited; one that sits still cannot.

Singularities — where the two answers become one

At full stretch (θ2 = 0) and at full fold (θ2 = ±180°) the arm is singular: the two elbow families merge, and the hand temporarily loses a direction — fully stretched, it cannot move further outward at all, and a sideways nudge of the target swings the shoulder wildly for almost no hand movement. Iterative solvers feel singularities in their bones, because the Jacobian matrix they invert goes rank-deficient there (its determinant hits zero) and the inverse explodes or vanishes. The bench's closed form feels almost nothing: acos is perfectly calm at the ends of its range, and proof 5 measures it — (220, 0) solves with θ2 = 0 from both families, hand exact; (20, 0) folds to θ2 = ±180°, hand exact. Calm is not the same as cost-free, though: near the rim the analytic answer is still exact, but a real arm's motors would have to move very fast to track a target sliding along it. The bench is kinematics — it prices positions, not speeds — and says so in the bends.

A word on the tiny numbers: 10⁻¹³ is not zero, and the table does not call it zero. It is floating-point dust — the residue of cos(90°) not being exactly 0 in binary — and it is reported at its true size rather than rounded into a cleaner claim. On a 220-unit arm it is about a billionth of a hair's width. The tolerances were set at 1×10⁻⁹ before the runs, and every measurement came in four-plus orders of magnitude inside them.

Bends named honestly

Planar. The arm lives flat on the table — two joints, one plane. Real arms add a rotating base and a wrist, and their IK stops being one triangle. The two-link planar case is the honest kernel of all of it, which is why it is taught first everywhere — including here.

No joint limits. This bench's elbow folds to a full ±180° and the shoulder spins freely. Real servos stop (often at ±150° or less), cables wrap, and links collide with themselves — each limit carves a bite out of the pretty ring. The ring drawn here is the geometric workspace; a specific real arm's is smaller, and its datasheet is where you'd read by how much.

Fixed link lengths. L1 = 120, L2 = 100, chosen unequal on purpose: equal lengths would close the dead zone to a point and hide the inner ring, which is half the lesson. The formulas in the core are general — the proofs' exact numbers belong to these lengths.

Kinematics is not dynamics. Nothing here knows about mass, torque, gravity, or speed. The bench can tell you the angles for a pose it would take a real motor real effort to hold, and near the rim it will happily demand joint speeds no servo has. Torque is the Motors workshop's subject; holding a pose against the world is Control's. This bench prices positions only.

The ±180° seam. Angles are wrapped to −180°…+180°, so dragging a target around the back can flip a slider's reading from +179° to −179° — the same physical direction, wearing the other name. The arm does not jump; the label does. Real controllers track turns continuously to avoid exactly this paperwork.

The exact centre. A target at (0, 0) has no direction, so "slide it along its ray" has no meaning; the bench's stated convention solves toward +x and flags it like any other dead-zone target. It is a convention, printed here and in the Help, not a discovery.
