What you are looking at

A two-link robot arm on a tabletop: an upper arm of length L1 = 120 driven by the shoulder angle θ1, and a forearm of length L2 = 100 driven by the elbow angle θ2 (measured relative to the upper arm). Lengths are in plain "units" — call them centimetres on a hobby arm. There is no simulation loop and no physics engine: kinematics is geometry, and every number on this bench is exact trigonometry, computed the same way every time.

Two questions, two directions

Forward kinematics (FK) asks: given the joint angles, where is the hand? x = L1·cos θ1 + L2·cos(θ1 + θ2), y = L1·sin θ1 + L2·sin(θ1 + θ2). The sliders pose the arm this way. Inverse kinematics (IK) asks the working robot's question: given where the hand should go, what angles get it there? Drag the amber target (or click anywhere on the table) and the bench solves IK and moves the arm — and the sliders follow, so you can read the answer off them.

How the solver works

Two bones and a target make a triangle: base, target, elbow. The law of cosines gives the elbow angle straight from the target's distance d: cos θ2 = (d² − L1² − L2²) / (2·L1·L2). Then the shoulder angle is the target's own direction minus a correction: θ1 = atan2(y, x) − atan2(L2·sin θ2, L1 + L2·cos θ2). That is the whole solver — no iteration, no guessing, nothing that can fail to converge.

Every reachable target has two answers

acos hands back one elbow angle, but the triangle can fold the other way: θ2 = ±acos(…) are both correct, mirror images across the base-to-target line. The Elbow up / Elbow down buttons pick which family the solver returns — "up" puts the elbow on the upper side of that line, "down" on the lower side. Real robot controllers make exactly this choice (it decides whether the elbow clears the table or the workpiece), and they make it on purpose, the way you are making it here.

Unreachable targets — stated behaviour

The hand can only live in the ring (the workspace) between radius |L1 − L2| = 20 and radius L1 + L2 = 220 from the base. Ask for a target outside the ring and this bench does not freeze and does not pretend: it slides the target along its own ray to the nearest point of the ring, solves for that, and tells you — the status line says unreachable, names the reason (too far, or inside the dead zone around the base), and reports how many units the hand falls short by. The arm reaches as far as it honestly can, in the direction you asked. Iterative solvers (the Jacobian and CCD benches in Everyday Physics) behave differently at the workspace edge — one of them freezes dead at a fully-stretched target; the lesson page compares, with receipts.

Reading the numbers

There is one source of truth on this bench: the two joint angles. The hand position is always FK computed from the angles on the sliders, so the readouts cannot disagree with the drawing or with each other. Reach error is the distance from the hand to the target you asked for: zero (to float precision) for reachable targets, and exactly the shortfall for unreachable ones. Angles are shown in degrees, wrapped to −180°…+180°.

Stated choices and honest bends

Link lengths are fixed (120 and 100) so the workspace numbers are exact and checkable. There are no joint limits: a real hobby arm cannot fold its elbow through itself or spin its shoulder forever — servos stop, cables wrap. This bench lets the geometry say everything it can say; the lesson names what a real arm would refuse. The arm is planar — flat on the table — where a real arm also lifts out of the plane. And at the fully stretched pose (θ2 = 0) the two elbow answers merge into one: that is a singularity, a place where the arm trades a choice for a direction it cannot move in. The bench stays stable there — ask the sliders — and the lesson explains why the analytic solver does not care while iterative ones do.

Everyday Kinematics — pose it by angles, or drag the hand where it should go

A robot arm answers two questions. Given the joint angles, where is the hand? That's forward kinematics — easy, exact, one answer. Given where the hand should go, what are the angles? That's inverse kinematics — the question every working robot lives on, and it usually has two answers. This bench does both, in the open, with a solver you can read in one sitting.
