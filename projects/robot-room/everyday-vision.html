What this is

A robot camera does not see balls and blocks. It sees a grid of numbers — one brightness-and-colour measurement per pixel — and everything a "vision" robot knows has to be computed from those numbers. This bench runs the classical pipeline that robots used for decades, live, on a synthetic camera feed drawn by the page itself (no camera, no photos, nothing leaves your browser):

  • Stage 1 — Threshold. Convert each pixel to grey (grey = 0.299·R + 0.587·G + 0.114·B, the BT.601 luma weights), then keep only pixels at or above the threshold slider. What survives is a binary image: 1s and 0s.
  • Stage 2 — Colour mask. Pick a target colour. A pixel survives if its colour is within the tolerance of the target, measured as a straight-line distance in RGB space: √((R−Rt)² + (G−Gt)² + (B−Bt)²) ≤ tolerance. That metric — RGB Euclidean distance — is named here because the lesson is honest about its weakness: it cannot tell "same colour, dimmer light" from "different colour." Try the lighting slider and watch.
  • Stage 3 — Blobs. The surviving pixels are grouped into blobs by connected-components labelling (two-pass union-find, 4-connectivity: pixels touching only at a corner count as separate blobs). Each blob reports its area and its centroid — the average position of its pixels, cx = Σx / area, cy = Σy / area. The biggest blob matching your colour gets the crosshair.
  • Steer. The bottom strip is the whole point: a little robot turns toward the tracked centroid using nothing but that centroid's x. The steering error is e = (cx − centre) / centre, from −1 (hard left) to +1 (hard right). Two numbers in, one number out — that is the funnel every vision robot lives on.

Every control

  • Threshold slider — the Stage 1 cut level, 0–255.
  • Target colour swatches — red, blue, green, or yellow: which object Stage 2 hunts for.
  • Colour tolerance slider — how far a pixel's colour may be from the target (RGB distance) and still count.
  • Lighting slider — a shadow gradient across the scene, up to 55% darker at the right edge. Real rooms do this to real robots constantly.
  • Noise slider — per-pixel random brightness jitter, like a cheap sensor in dim light.
  • Pause / resume — freeze the feed to inspect one frame.

Simplifications, named

  • The scene is synthetic and flat-lit by design: objects are flat colours with a small highlight spot, so the pipeline's arithmetic can be checked exactly. Real cameras add lens blur, auto-exposure, and compression mush.
  • One lighting model: a smooth left-to-right shadow. Real shadows have edges, and real colour constancy is a research problem, not a slider.
  • No neural nets anywhere. This is the classical pipeline — threshold, label, measure — the one that found the coloured ball in a thousand student robots before learned detectors existed. The lesson says why it is still the right first pipeline to learn.
