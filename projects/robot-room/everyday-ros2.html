What you are looking at

A small robot's brain, drawn the way ROS 2 organises it: not one big program, but five little ones — nodes — passing each other notes. The notes are messages, the named mail slots they travel through are topics, and the whole drawing is called the computation graph. This page is a model of ROS 2's ideas running on one page's clock — not ROS 2 itself. The lesson page says exactly what that leaves out.

The cast

/camera publishes a picture on /image at the rate you set (1–20 per second). /detector subscribes to /image and publishes what it found on /detections. /planner subscribes to /detections and publishes driving orders on /cmd_vel. /motor_driver subscribes to /cmd_vel and moves the little robot. /joy is you, on a joystick: when teleop is on it publishes its own /cmd_vel at 20 Hz. Two publishers on one topic is legal — the driver simply obeys the freshest order it has received, which is why the faster teleop wins.

The four experiments

1 · Stop the camera. The big red button pauses /camera. Watch the counts: the last pictures already on the wire still arrive, the detector publishes its last detections, the planner issues its last orders — then the driver notices its orders have gone stale (it obeys a /cmd_vel for only 0.5 s) and the robot halts. Nobody told the planner anything. The chain starves link by link, and that chain reaction is how real robot software actually fails — and how you debug it: find the first quiet topic.

2 · Teleop override. Turn /joy on and the robot starts turning in a steady circle (ω = 0.80 rad/s) — its orders now come from you, because yours arrive fresher. Turn it off and the planner quietly takes over again.

3 · QoS: reliable vs best effort. The /image topic can run reliable (every message guaranteed, the default here) or best effort (fire and forget). In best effort this model drops 15% of frames on the wire — counted, in the open, in the topic table. Sensor feeds in real ROS 2 often run best effort on purpose: a late picture is worth less than a fresh one. Notice what the drop does downstream: fewer detections, lumpier driving — but nothing crashes, because nobody was promised anything.

4 · Call a service. Topics are one-way broadcasts. A service is the other shape: one node asks, one node answers. The /reset button sends a request to /motor_driver and gets a response — the robot teleports home, and not a single topic message is sent doing it. The service log shows the exchange; the topic counts do not move. (ROS 2 has a third shape, actions — long jobs with progress reports. Named here, not simulated; the lesson explains.)

The clock and the counting rules

The simulation runs on a fixed step, dt = 0.05 s — twenty steps per sim second, whatever your screen does. Publishers keep a fractional accumulator (acc += rate × dt each step, publish when it crosses 1), so a 7 Hz camera publishes exactly 7 messages per sim second, give or take the one message a step boundary can shift. A message spends 2 steps (0.10 s) on the wire between nodes. A node that receives a message reacts in the same step it arrives. Counts in the tables are the sim's own counters — published, delivered, dropped — and the proofs at the bottom check them against the arithmetic.

Stated choices

Best-effort drops are seeded. The drop dice are a mulberry32 generator with a fixed seed, so the same run always loses the same frames — that is what lets the proofs print an exact dropped count instead of a shrug. Arbitration is last-message-wins: the driver holds only the freshest /cmd_vel and its source; real robots often add a proper multiplexer (twist_mux) for exactly this job. The robot is a unicycle: heading θ grows by ω·dt, position by v·dt along the heading. The arena window follows the robot (an odometer keeps the true pose in the readouts) — the window is a camera, not a wall.

Reading the page

Glowing packets on the graph are slowed down for your eyes — at 20 Hz the real traffic would be a blur; the counts are always the sim's true numbers. The message log is this page's ros2 topic echo: pick a topic and read what is actually flowing. The node cards show each node's inbox count and the last note it touched. If the numbers ever disagree with the pictures, believe the numbers — and read the proofs.

Everyday ROS 2 — five little programs passing notes

A robot is not one program. It is a camera that shouts pictures, a detector that shouts what it saw, a planner that shouts driving orders, and a driver that listens — strangers to each other, connected only by the notes they pass. This bench is that conversation, live: stop one voice and watch the silence travel.
