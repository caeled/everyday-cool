Everyday Control

Make the robot notice. Then make it react.

Motors move. Sensors notice. This bench is where they meet: a loop that reads, decides, and acts — twenty times a second. Nudge a slider and watch a robot overshoot, wobble, and finally settle right on the line. By the end you'll have tuned a PID controller, which is the same trick keeping drones level, kettles at temperature, and cruise control cruising.

Start here → hit "Run" on the PID Tuner and watch the robot hunt for the line. Then grab the P slider and break it on purpose.
❓ How this workshop's robot works — the honest model

Every bench that moves runs one simulation, stepped at a fixed dt = 0.05 s — the loop really does run twenty times a second, whatever your screen's refresh rate is. The robot is a differential-drive line-follower in the line's own frame: it drives at 46 units/s; its heading turns at the steering rate the controller commands. The sensor sits 5 units ahead of the drive axle and reads the line's offset m; the error is e = −m / 40. The PID is the textbook discrete form — u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e·dt − Kd·(dm/dt)/40 — with the derivative taken on the measurement (so a target jump can't kick it), the output saturated at ±2.6 rad/s, and an anti-windup clamp holding |Ki·∫| ≤ 0.9. The steering reaches the wheels through a 0.18 s lag (motors can't change turn rate instantly) and the tyres scrub away turn rate at 0.4 /s. The line curves exactly as drawn (a sine, amplitude 60 units, wavelength 800). Bench 4's wobble bot is the same plant with the PID replaced by bang-bang: full-left or full-right by the sign of m. Nothing on this page is an animation of a result — it is the result, computed live. The self-checks at the bottom re-measure ten of the page's claims from this exact code.

Bench 1

The Loop

A toaster runs for two minutes whether the bread is frozen or already on fire — that's open loop: run blind and hope. A thermostat checks the temperature and corrects — that's closed loop. Robots want to be thermostats, not toasters. The control loop in four words, forever: read → compare → correct → repeat — twenty times a second.

Bench 2

Blinking Too Fast to See (PWM)

Remember the thread Everyday Motors left hanging? A microcontroller pin is weak — it can't power a motor directly. But a motor is basically on/off anyway. So we cheat: switch full power on and off hundreds of times a second. The motor only feels the average. 25% on-time = quarter speed. That trick is PWM — pulse-width modulation — and "duty cycle" is just the fraction of time spent ON.

⚡ Blinking ≈490 times a second — a typical robot board's PWM rate. Way too fast for the motor to notice the gaps — it just feels the average.

At this speed you can see each pulse. A real motor fed 2 blinks a second would stutter and buzz — that's why real PWM runs at ≈490 blinks a second.

🌬 Coasting — no power at all. ⚡ Full power — no blinking needed.
Average power: 50%
🔧Takeaway: Full power, chopped into blinks. The motor feels the average — that's speed control without a gearbox.
Bench 3

Meet the Bodyguard (Motor Driver)

Everyday Motors promised you'd meet this one: "a motor driver is the motor's bodyguard." Why? Motor pins are weak, motors are hungry and electrically noisy — stalling spikes current, and coils kick back voltage when switched off. The driver takes the microcontroller's tiny PWM whisper and delivers the motor's full-power shout. An H-bridge lets it push current either direction — so one chip does forward, reverse, brake, and coast.

🎯 Challenge 1 of 4: Make the motor spin forward.
💡 Click the switches (S1–S4) to route current. Forward means current crosses the motor left → right. The driver chip does this automatically in real life — here you are the chip.
🔧Takeaway: Tiny signals in, big current out, either direction. The driver is the muscle; the loop is the brain.
Bench 4

The Wobble Bot — why "just correct" isn't enough

A line-following robot with one sensor and one rule: "if the line is left of center, steer left; if right, steer right." Sounds fine. Watch it drive. The bot overshoots, corrects, overshoots the other way — a zigzag wobble that never settles. That's what happens when corrections ignore how far off you are and how fast you're closing in.

line left of boterror: 0line right of bot
〰️ Zigzagging — never settles on the line

The bot is following the loop — read, compare, correct, repeat. The problem isn't the loop. It's that the correction is always full-left or full-right, no matter how tiny the error. The next bench fixes exactly that.

🔧Takeaway: A loop that only says "steer this way" overshoots forever. Corrections need to know how much.
Bench 5 · Flagship

The PID Tuner

Same robot, same line — but now the steering is smart. Three knobs, one job each: P steers in proportion to the error (big miss = big steer). I remembers stubborn leftover error and leans harder until it's gone. D watches how fast the error is shrinking and eases off before overshooting. Together they turn the zigzag into a smooth glide. Tune it badly on purpose — that's how you learn what each knob really does.

🌀 Windup!

The clamp caps the integral at what the steering can actually use (|Ki·∫| ≤ 0.9). The dragging wheel pulls the robot sideways at a constant 0.3 rad/s. Shove & pin holds the robot off the line for 4 s while the error can't shrink — the windup demonstration.

error (distance off the line) steering output
Curious? What's "steady-state error" — the thing I keeps fighting?

It's the stubborn leftover gap that P alone can't close — but watch this robot closely, because its P-only failure arrives in the other order. First comes the wobble: with nothing to brake the swing, P alone crosses the line 7 times in 20 seconds and overshoots by 17 units before the wobble dies (that's preset 1 — D is the brake it's missing). The leftover gap is real too; it just needs a constant push to show itself. Tick the Dragging wheel box under the tuner and P+D park a full 7.5 units off the line, forever — because holding against a constant push takes a constant steering output, and at zero error P has nothing to make it from. The I term solves it by adding up that small error over time until the correction is big enough to close the gap completely. That's the whole trick.

🔧Takeaway: P for now, I for the past, D for the future. Tune the mix and the wobble becomes a glide.
