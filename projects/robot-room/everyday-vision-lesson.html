Everyday Vision — the lesson

A camera doesn't see objects. It sees numbers — 19,200 of them on this bench's little 160×120 feed, three per pixel — and a robot that "follows the red ball" is really running a funnel: pixels in one end, arithmetic in the middle, and out the other end a single steering number. This page is the other half of the bench: the three stages of that funnel, the real formulas, and the proof table — measured numbers, not promises.

The funnel

Every stage of the pipeline throws information away on purpose. The camera frame holds 160 × 120 × 3 ≈ 57,600 numbers. Thresholding or colour-masking crushes that to 19,200 ones and zeros. Blob labelling crushes it further, to a short list of regions. The centroid crushes one region to two numbers — and the steering error crushes those to one. A robot doesn't need a picture of the world; it needs to know which way to turn, and how much. The whole craft of classical robot vision is choosing what to throw away, in what order, so that the one number left over is the number you needed.

Stage 1 — Grey, then threshold

First the colour goes. Each pixel becomes a single brightness by the BT.601 luma weights — the ones television settled on because human eyes are most sensitive to green:

grey = 0.299·R + 0.587·G + 0.114·B Pure green (0,255,0) lands at 150; pure red at 76; pure blue at 29. That ordering is your eyes, written as arithmetic — and it is why the blue ball nearly vanishes in the bench's threshold view while the yellow block blazes.

Then the cut: a pixel survives if grey ≥ T, where T is the slider. The result is a binary image — a mask of 1s and 0s. Thresholding is the oldest trick in machine vision and still the first one reached for, because it turns "find the bright thing" into a question arithmetic can answer. Its weakness is printed on the bench's face: it knows nothing about colour, so a bright floorboard and a bright ball are the same to it.

Stage 2 — The colour mask, and its honest weakness

Stage 2 keeps the colour and asks a different question: how far is this pixel's colour from the target colour? The bench measures that as a straight-line distance in RGB space:

distance = √((R−Rt)² + (G−Gt)² + (B−Bt)²) ≤ tolerance The boundary is exact and the proof below walks it: a pixel at distance 39 is in, at 40 is in (the rule is ≤), at 41 is out.

Now the honest part, because this metric has a real flaw and the bench lets you watch it happen. RGB distance cannot tell same colour, dimmer light from different colour. Drag the bench's lighting slider to full shadow with the red ball at the right edge and the mask keeps only 54 of the ball's 377 pixels — and the centroid of what survives sits at (131.2, 90.2) when the ball is really at (135, 94): the pipeline doesn't just lose the target, it lies about where it is, by about 5.4 pixels. Real vision systems answer this by measuring colour in a space like HSV, where hue — the colour's identity — is separated from brightness, so a shadow changes mostly the number you ignore. The references below point at that next step; this bench stays in RGB on purpose, so the failure is one you can produce with a slider instead of taking anyone's word for.

Stage 3 — Blobs: from pixels to a place

A mask says which pixels match; it doesn't say which matches belong to the same object. Connected-components labelling answers that: scan the mask, and group touching survivors into regions — blobs. The bench implements the classical two-pass union-find version, with one deliberate choice worth naming: 4-connectivity. Pixels that touch edge-to-edge are one blob; pixels touching only at a corner are two. The proof table plants two squares touching at exactly one corner and counts them: 2. (Plenty of real libraries default to 8-connectivity and would say 1. Neither is wrong — but a pipeline that can't tell you which rule it used can't tell you what its blob count means.)

Each blob then gets measured. Its area is just its pixel count. Its centroid — the average position of its pixels — is the first two image moments:

cx = Σx / area   ·   cy = Σy / area For a symmetric blob this is exact: the proof plants a rasterised disc and reads its centroid back as (20.000, 20.000), its true centre. For a half-shadowed blob it is exactly as wrong as Stage 2 made it — the funnel has no stage that can apologise for an earlier one.

And then the funnel's exit. The steering strip takes the tracked centroid and computes e = (cx − 79.5) / 79.5 — zero when the target is centred, −1 at the left edge, +1 at the right — and turns the little robot toward it at a limited rate. When the mask loses the target entirely, the robot holds its last heading and waits. That behaviour isn't a shortcut in the demo; it's what real tracking robots do, because "keep doing the last sane thing" beats "spin at random" every time.

How the core works

The bench follows the house pattern: a pure core fenced between /* === CORE START === */ and /* === CORE END === */, no DOM inside. The synthetic scene, both masks, the labeller, and the steering step all live there, so the interface and runProofs() call the same arithmetic. The scene is generated, never photographed: makeScene(t) paints a wall, a floor, three blocks and two balls whose positions are closed-form functions of t, applies the lighting gradient, then adds deterministic per-pixel noise (a hash of pixel index and frame seed — same frame, same noise, every run, which is what makes the proofs repeatable). sceneTruth(t) reports where the objects actually are, so the tracking proof grades the pipeline against reality instead of against itself.

Honest notes

The scene is synthetic. Flat colours, one highlight spot per ball, no lens blur, no auto-exposure, no compression. That is deliberate — it's what lets every number above be checked exactly — and it means the bench proves the arithmetic, not a real camera's behaviour. A real feed would bend every stage here, mostly at the mask.

One lighting model. A smooth left-to-right shadow, up to 55% at the edge. Real shadows have hard edges, real rooms mix light sources of different colours, and real colour constancy is an open problem wearing a slider's clothes here.

Occlusion is designed out of the tracking proof. The red ball is painted last, so nothing ever covers it. In the first headless run it was painted first, other objects occluded it, and the worst tracking error measured 5.578 px — the pipeline faithfully centroided the visible crescent of the ball. That failure is real and common; it was removed from the proof because the proof grades the pipeline, and named here because you deserve to know it exists.

No neural nets. Nothing on this bench learned anything. Threshold, label, measure is the classical pipeline — the one that found coloured balls, followed lines, and docked robots for decades before learned detectors arrived. It is still the right first pipeline to learn, because every one of its steps can be pointed at, computed by hand, and caught lying — as Stage 2 was caught above.
