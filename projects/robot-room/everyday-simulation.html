What you are looking at

A whole robot world in one page: a maze of walls, a small differential-drive robot (two driven wheels, like the one in Everyday Python Robotics), and three distance sensors. Real robot software gets tested in simulators like this — Gazebo, Webots — before it is allowed near real hardware, because in here you can break a thousand robots before breakfast and it costs nothing. This page is that idea at tabletop scale, with its seams showing.

The world

The floor is a grid of 20 × 14 cells, each 0.5 m square — a room 10 m × 7 m. Three preset mazes are on the buttons. In ✏️ Edit walls mode you can click or drag on the canvas to build and erase walls, and with the ◎ Set goal tool you can move the goal. Your maze is autosaved in this browser. The border is always wall.

Driving

Use the arrow keys or W A S D, or hold the on-screen pad. The keys set the two wheel speeds directly: forward drives both wheels, a turn key speeds one wheel and slows the other, and the physics turns that into motion with v = (vL + vR)/2 and ω = (vR − vL)/0.30 m — the same motion model, with the same track width, as the Python Robotics bench next door. The wheels top out at 0.60 m/s. The physics runs at a fixed 120 steps a second, however fast or slow your screen refreshes.

The sensors

Three distance rays fan out at −35°, 0°, +35° from the robot's heading, each reaching 3.0 m. They are computed by raycasting against the wall grid — the real geometry, not a script. The numbers in the sensor panel are the same numbers the auto-driver steers by. There is no second, secret channel: if a ray can't see a wall, the driver can't either.

Collisions

The robot is a circle of radius 0.18 m. When a step would push the circle into a wall cell, the step is pushed back out along the shortest escape — so the robot slides along walls instead of sticking or tunnelling. The proofs measure the clearance over a scripted drive straight into a wall: it never goes below zero.

Odometry — the robot's diary

The dashed cyan robot is where the robot believes it is. It keeps that diary the way real robots do: by adding up its own wheel motions (odometry). The diary is integrated from the commanded wheel speeds and never collides — wheels that spin against a wall still write in the diary. Turn the wheel-slip knob up and the left wheel starts lying about how far it turned: the true robot (solid) arcs away while the diary (dashed) sails straight on. The error readout is the distance between the two — measured, not scripted. In the proofs' open test room, three seconds of "straight" driving at 15% slip leaves the diary 0.36 m from the truth — and the drift test on this page runs the same experiment in the same bare room. That gap is the single most important fact about real robot navigation, and it is why robots also carry the sensors from Everyday Sensors.

The auto-driver

🤖 Auto-drive hands the wheels to a right-hand wall follower: a small controller that reads only the three rays. It hugs the wall on its right at about 0.45 m, turns left when the front ray gets short, spins left when it is about to touch, and arcs right when it loses the wall. Wall following is a real, century-old maze strategy — and it has a known failure: it can only ever reach places that touch the wall system it is following. Its measured record on the three preset mazes, re-run live in the proofs below: First Room — solved in 27.5 s. The Corridor — solved in 85.8 s. The Island — FAILED: four minutes of faithful wall-following never reaches a goal that sits in open floor touching no wall, which is exactly the failure the textbooks promise. No "solved" banner appears unless the robot's centre actually reaches the goal circle.

The drift test clears a bare test room — the same one the proofs use — puts the robot in the middle, and drives both wheels at 0.40 m/s for three seconds with the slip you set. With 0% slip the diary and the truth agree to the last decimal; with slip, watch the dashed robot insist it went straight. Your maze stays saved in this browser and comes back on reload. "Re-sync" is what a real robot does when it recognises a landmark: it throws the diary away and believes the world instead.

The honest seam: in this world, slip is a single knob on one wheel. On a real robot, slip is mud, carpet edges, a slightly soft tyre, a wheel that's 1 mm smaller than its twin — it changes by the second and nobody tells the robot. The knob exists so you can watch the shape of the failure with the cause labelled.

Simplifications, stated plainly: the world is a perfect 2D grid and the walls are exactly where the map says; the sensors are exact raycasts with no noise, no blur and no dropouts; the wheels answer commands instantly; slip is one steady knob on one wheel instead of a changing property of the floor; the auto-driver is a deliberately simple wall follower, not a mapping or planning system.
