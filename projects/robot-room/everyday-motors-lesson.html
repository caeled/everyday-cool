Everyday Motors — the lesson

The exhibit puts one small brushed DC motor on a bench — a cutaway you can power, a speed/torque/current bench you can load, a shelf of motor types to choose between, and a wiring board where the motor only spins when the loop is truly complete. This page is its other half: the one straight line every DC motor lives on, the self-made voltage that governs it, and the receipts — including the story of the constants the bench shipped with, what they implied when measured, and how they were rebuilt from one real motor's datasheet so the page's own claim about itself became true.

The workshop was built by Qwen (Kimi) for Everyday Cool's Robot Room, at Steven Powell's direction — the first of three guest workshops for the room, alongside six built by Muse. It arrived with two faults, documented in full below: a truncated function that kept the whole page from running, and sim constants that could not describe any real motor. Both were repaired by Muse under Steven's sanction on 2026-11-24, in four change sites, and the audit record says exactly what moved. Everything below is measured against the repaired page.

The motor is two machines in one

Feed a DC motor voltage and it turns. Turn its shaft by hand and it makes voltage — it is a generator. Those are not two facts; they are one machine seen from two sides, and everything surprising about DC motors follows from the second machine fighting the first. The whole lesson is one line of bookkeeping:

V = I·R + E The battery voltage splits two ways: some is lost pushing current through the winding's resistance, and the rest faces the motor's self-made voltage, E. Hold onto this line — every formula below is a rearrangement of it.

Voltage sets the speed the motor wants

With nothing attached, the motor speeds up until the voltage it generates itself — the back-EMF — nearly cancels the battery. At that resting point almost no voltage gap remains, almost no current flows, and the motor cruises. That point is the no-load speed, and it is set by voltage, not by load:

E = Ke · ω   ·   ωno-load ≈ V / Ke The faster it spins, the more voltage it makes against you. Top speed is the battery divided by the constant — minus a small friction nibble the honest box below owns up to. On the bench, the 12 V no-load reading is the first anchor of the motor's line: 5600 RPM, the datasheet's own figure for the motor the bench now models.

Load decides the current it takes

Slow the shaft down — a hill, a heavier robot, your fingers on the wheel — and the back-EMF drops with the speed. The gap between battery and back-EMF widens, and that gap, divided by the winding resistance, is the current. Current is not chosen by the battery and not "used up" by the motor; it is whatever the voltage gap demands:

I = (V − Ke·ω) / R   ·   τ = Kt · I Current is the voltage gap, divided by the resistance. And torque is current wearing a constant: double the current, double the push. This one pair explains the start-up gulp (at standstill there is no back-EMF at all, so the gap is the whole battery), the cruise sip, and the stall flood — same motor, same battery, three different gaps.

The two constants are one number

Kt = Ke   (in SI units: N·m/A = V·s/rad) The torque constant and the back-EMF constant are the same number — they have to be, or the motor would create energy from nothing. Datasheets hide this by quoting Ke as RPM per volt and Kt as g·cm per amp, in units that never meet. Convert both to SI and they must agree exactly. Keep this in your pocket: it is the test the bench's original constants fail, below, and the rule its repaired constants were rebuilt by.

The one straight line

Torque falls in a straight line from its maximum at stall to zero at no-load speed. Any brushed DC motor's whole personality is that line, and the line is fixed by two anchors: stall torque (at zero speed) and no-load speed (at zero torque). Find the two anchors on a datasheet, draw the line, and every operating point the motor will ever visit lives on it. The scary end of the line comes from the two easiest divisions in the lesson:

Istall = V / R   ·   τstall = Kt · V / R At stall the current is the full battery voltage across a small resistance — the largest current the motor will ever draw — and none of it becomes motion; all of it becomes heat in the windings. Stall torque is a capability number, not a working number: size your driver and battery for stall current, and design the robot to live nowhere near it.

Power peaks in the middle

Pmech = τ · ω   ·   Pmax = τstall · ωno-load / 4, at ω = ωno-load / 2 Power is torque times speed — the rectangle under your point on the line. Both ends give zero (no motion at stall, no torque at no-load), so the biggest rectangle fits exactly in the middle: half stall torque at half no-load speed. The audit verified the repaired bench does this precisely — mechanical power peaks at exactly 50% load at every voltage, as theory requires.
η = Pmech / (V · I) Efficiency is what fraction of the electrical power shows up as motion instead of heat. It is zero at both ends of the line and best at a modest load in between — which is why "most powerful point" and "best working point" are different places on the same line. On the repaired bench the model's worst — that is, highest — efficiency anywhere is 60.77%, at about 22% load. Under 100% everywhere, as physics insists. It was not always so on this page; see the constants story below.

PWM — lying to the motor, honestly

Vavg = duty × Vsupply Chop the supply on and off fast enough and the motor responds to the average: half the time on is half the voltage, as far as the motor can tell — and unlike a resistor or a linear controller, the switch doesn't waste the difference as heat. The bench's own footnote points onward to the Everyday Control bench for how a microcontroller does the chopping; the Electronics room's Power & PWM bench (linked below) shows duty cycle as something you can scope, not just slide.

Gearing — trading speed for torque

τout ≈ N · τ   ·   ωout = ω / N Bare motors spin fast and weak; robots want slow and strong. An N:1 gearbox divides speed by N and multiplies torque by about N — both anchors move, and the line redraws. This is why the motors in real robots are gear motors, and why a cheap gearbox's friction fee matters; the Physics room's Gears & Linkages Lab carries the ratio arithmetic.

Choosing a motor, in one pass: start from the robot — wheel size and weight give the torque needed at the wheel; work backwards through the gearbox to the motor's line; check the working point sits in the efficient middle of that line, and the stall current fits the driver and battery you actually have. The Adafruit and SparkFun guides on the reference shelf walk the same pass with real parts.

The constants story — measured, then repaired

The bench's framing sentence reads: "Everything you see here is how a real brushed DC motor behaves." The audit tested that sentence the way this collection tests everything: it extracted the page's own simulation code and ran it. The torque–speed shape was right — the correct straight line, power peaking at 50% load, a back-EMF panel that states the real mechanism. The numbers could not coexist in any motor that obeys physics. As shipped, the three dials were RPM_PER_V = 900, TORQUE_PER_V = 150 g·cm, CUR_PER_V = 150 mA. Converted to SI they imply Ke = 0.01061 V·s/rad and Kt = 0.0981 N·m/A — Kt/Ke = 9.24, where the law above requires exactly 1. The measured consequence, from the page's own outputs:

A motor that returns four watts for every watt it is fed is not a motor; it is a perpetual-motion claim, and it falsified the page's framing sentence. The repair, sanctioned by Steven on 2026-11-24, followed the audit's prescribed direction: stop picking dials independently and derive all three from one real motor — the Mabuchi RF-370CA-15370, a small brushed DC hobby motor whose 3–12 V range matches the bench's slider. Its datasheet anchors at 12 V:

One honest wrinkle in the derivation, printed rather than smoothed over: the model's stall torque at 12 V comes out at 221 g·cm, above the datasheet's measured 187 g·cm. That gap is the expected one — the datasheet number is measured on a real motor with brush drop and friction, while the bench models the ideal motor anchored to the datasheet's own speed and current figures. The ideal model is now internally consistent, which is what the framing sentence claims — so the sentence was kept verbatim rather than softened.

Provenance — who built what, and what was repaired

The workshop was designed and written by Qwen (Kimi) in Qwen AI Studio, at Steven Powell's direction, as pilot 1 of 3 for this room. As delivered, it had two faults. First, Bench 4's evaluate() function was truncated — its signature and first half missing — and a single syntax error (Unexpected token 'else') prevented the page's entire script from parsing: gauges frozen, picker cards never rendered, quiz never built, the page a static poster of itself. Second, the three sim constants above, which implied a 428%-efficient motor. The pedagogy underneath — the torque–speed shape, the back-EMF explanation, the quiz, the tour scripts — audited sound, and the page is fully self-contained: zero external resources, all art inline, its "runs fully offline" footer claim true.

Under Steven's sanction (2026-11-24, following the collection's Cardio and Orbital Pinball precedent — a guest file may be edited when the records say so plainly), Muse repaired the delivered file in four change sites, and nothing else:

  1. The constants line — the three dials re-derived from the Mabuchi RF-370CA-15370 datasheet as above, with the comment now naming the motor.
  2. One bench-intro sentence — Bench 2 now names the motor it models ("A virtual Mabuchi RF-370CA-15370 — a real 3–12 V hobby motor — on a breadboard."), which is what makes the framing sentence true.
  3. The restored head of evaluate() — reconstructed from the surviving groups() union-find helper and the shipped fragment's own variable names: loop topology judged with the switch's terminals bridged, switchInLoop tested by whether the loop survives removing that bridge, and a short judged on the live circuit — a battery wired to itself through an open switch is not flagged until the switch closes and the short becomes real.
  4. The two restored branch conditions around the orphaned hint statements, so the shipped hints — their texts byte-for-byte Qwen's own — fire on the wiring states they describe.

The uploaded original was not modified; the repaired build is the page in this room, and the full audit — findings, sweep tables, repair addendum with old → new values and file hashes — is the record.

Bends named honestly — what still isn't fixed

The stall snap. At 98–99% load the bench snaps to a full stall — 0 RPM, full stall torque, full stall current — while its own linear model says the shaft should still be turning at 108–216 RPM at 6 V. Named in the audit, left as shipped; the gauges and the model disagree in that last two percent of the slider, and now you know where.

Chart and gauge disagree at the snap. The chart's marker keeps riding the un-stalled speed line at high load, so at 98% the marker and the speed gauge tell different stories on the same screen.

The chart at V = 0. The torque/speed curves are hard-coded straight lines, drawn at full height whatever the voltage — at 0 V the chart shows full curves with the marker parked top-left while every gauge reads zero. Read the chart as a normalized schematic of the line's shape, not as a plot of the current settings; it is not labelled as one on the page, so it is labelled here.

The no-load current is real, and unexplained on the page. At zero load the model still draws current — 8% of stall, 42 mA displayed at 6 V — while showing 0 g·cm delivered. That is the friction current, and it is physically right; the page never says where those milliamps go. They go to heat, fighting brush and bearing friction.

Friction is otherwise absent. Beyond that fixed sip, the sim's line is the ideal one. A real motor's no-load current bends the line's ends and pulls true no-load speed below V/Ke.

Resistance is a constant here; on a real motor it's a weather report. Copper's resistance climbs about 0.4%/°C as the windings heat, so a hot motor is a different motor from the cold one on the datasheet — and a stalled motor's current sags as it cooks.

No inductance, no brush drop, no battery sag. Real windings resist changes in current (why PWM frequency matters and drivers need flyback paths); brushes lose a roughly fixed 0.5–2 V; and a real battery's voltage droops exactly when current peaks. The bench's battery is infinitely stiff and its motor responds instantly.

Datasheets are measurements, not laws. Kt = Ke is exact; the printed constants carry tolerances (±10% is normal), so two "identical" motors differ — which is why real robots close the loop with sensors and control. Those are this room's next two workshops, linked below.
