Everyday Sensors — the lesson

A sensor is a question you ask the world: how far? how bright? which way am I facing? how much have I turned? The world never answers in words. It answers in a number — a time, a current, a count — and the robot's whole picture of reality is built out of those numbers and nothing else. So this lesson keeps two columns side by side everywhere: what the world is doing and what the sensor says. Where they agree, we say so, with the measurement. Where they part — an angled wall, a magnet in the room, a gyro left running — that gap is the lesson.

The arena — one rover, a whole suite of senses

The workshop is one robot in one room, wearing every sensor at once: an ultrasonic sonar cone, a 360° lidar, a stereo depth camera, a 5-element IR line array on its nose, tactile bumpers, wheel encoders feeding a dead-reckoning "ghost," a gyroscope and magnetometer, and a GPS receiver whose fixes feed a Kalman fusion layer. Six preset labs rearrange the room to interrogate them: glass against foam, a figure-8 line race, a GPS-denied tunnel, a magnet left lying around, an icy floor. Units on the page are arena pixels labelled centimetres — a self-consistent 1 px = 1 cm scale the page itself never states (it is stated here).

Shout and time the echo — sonar and lidar

The ranger's idea is the beginner's first real formula: send something out, time its return, and take half the trip.

d = c · t / 2 Distance is half a round trip. Forget the half and every wall is twice as far away as it is — the single most-forgotten factor in beginner robotics. Sonar does this with sound; lidar does it with light. Same formula, very different c, and very different surfaces that fool it.
c ≈ 331.3 + 0.606 · T  (m/s, T in °C) The c in the sonar formula is the speed of sound — a property of today's air, not a constant of the universe. Sound speeds up about 0.6 m/s per degree, so a ranger calibrated on the bench measures a slightly different world outdoors.
echo time ≈ 58 µs per centimetre  (at c ≈ 343 m/s) The datasheet's magic number (µs ÷ 58, on the HC-SR04 every beginner owns) is just c·t/2 with the arithmetic pre-done — and now you can re-derive it at any temperature.

On this page, honesty requires a footnote that becomes a teaching point: the sim does not simulate the echo's flight. Its sonar and lidar compute distances geometrically (raycasts) and add measured noise — the geometry and the failure modes are real, the time-of-flight is the real sensors' story, told by the formulas above. What the page does model, and the audit verified both ways, is the part beginners actually get bitten by: surfaces. Sonar hears glass and is blind to foam; lidar sees foam and passes through glass. And sound reflects specularly, like light off a mirror: tilt the wall far enough and the echo bounces away entirely — measured on the page, a short wall at 50° incidence is invisible to the sonar, while at 30° it reads exactly right.

The floor answers back — the IR line array

Five infrared eyes on the rover's nose shine light down and measure what bounces back. Pale floor, loud answer; dark line, whisper. The page's model is a clean ramp, stated here because the page doesn't state it:

reading = 1 − d / 9.5   (d = distance from the line's centre, in px; 0 beyond 9.5) The audit sampled it: 1.000 dead on the line, 0.000 at 60 px off, and the symmetric five-eye pattern (0, 0.21, 1.00, 0.21, 0) straddling the line — the model in the code is the model on the page, exactly.
reflected signal ∝ albedo / d²   (the real-world law) On a physical sensor, double the distance and the answer quarters; halve the floor's paleness and it halves again. One number, two causes multiplied together — and the sensor cannot tell you which one moved. A reflectance reading is a surface and a distance, which is why line sensors are calibrated against the actual floor.
ADC step = Vref / 2N   (10-bit at 5 V: ≈ 4.9 mV) However smooth the world is, a 10-bit converter hears 1,024 loudnesses and nothing in between. Quantization is the first lie every sensor tells, and the politest.

The array's weighted centroid steers the line-follower — with, since the repair, an honest label: a proportional controller, targetW = err × 2.35, nothing more. It is a good one: driven for 120 seconds, the rover's centre stayed within 9.5 px of the figure-8 for 100.0% of steps and was never lost. When you want the other two letters — what the integral buys, what the derivative costs — the room's Control workshop next door is built around exactly that question, and its robot sees its line with this very sense.

A good memory with a slow leak — the gyroscope

The gyro reports how fast you are turning. Heading is that report added up — the first calculus a beginner meets in the wild, and its price is printed on the page if you watch the odometry ghost while the rover sits perfectly still:

θ(t) = ∫ ω dt   ·   drift = bias × t A bias of 2°/s is nothing for a second and 120° for a minute: integration adds the small steady lie up, on schedule. Measured on the page, exactly: bias 1.8 °/s gives a heading error of 18.00° after 10.0 s; bias 5.5 °/s gives the full 330° in 60 s — a robot that is sure it is turning while sitting still.

The page's IMU is a gyro plus a magnetometer — no accelerometer station (the shelf's references carry that sense; chips like the BNO055 fuse all three on-board). The accel's classic trick is worth the formula anyway, because it is the gyro's natural partner:

ax = g · sin θ Tilt an accelerometer and gravity's pull splits across its axes in proportion to the tilt — a plumb line that reports in numbers. Noisy in the moment, but it never drifts: exactly the opposite personality from the gyro. That complement is why fusion exists.

A compass in a room full of magnets — the magnetometer

The compass sense measures the local magnetic field and calls its direction "north." The page models the field honestly in structure — a base field plus an inverse-square pull toward every magnet in the room — and at arcade strength: one magnet 60 px to the side pulls 2.7× the base field, and the displayed heading swings from 57.30° to 350.04°, a 67° lie, while the distortion meter pegs at 0.933. Earth's real field is ~25–65 µT and a robot's own motors and batteries distort it by comparable amounts, so the indoor compass is a suggestion even when its numbers are gentler than this page's. Keep that swing in mind — it is the measurement the repaired fusion layer had to survive, next.

Counting is a promise the wheels can break — encoders and odometry

d = (counts / CPR) · π · Dwheel   ·   Δθ = (dR − dL) / track width Counts become revolutions, revolutions become circumferences, and the pose is the two wheels' stories differenced. Every symbol is a place error can move in: a miscount, a wheel that isn't quite D, a floor that lets the wheel spin without travelling.

Put the rover on the page's ice and the structure of the lie shows: the wheels turn, the counts are honest, and the pose is a fiction — because odometry measures the wheels and only believes in the ground. Two of this page's seams belong in the honest box rather than the formula card, and they are flagged there: on this page the encoder counters are decoration (odometry integrates velocity × slip directly — the audit set the counters to ±99,999 mid-run and the odometry output was bit-identical), and the slip percentage is not the slip delivered (at the "8%" setting the delivered factor oscillates with a mean of about +3.2% — roughly 40% of the label).

Two flawed witnesses — the fusion layer

The gyro is right for a second and wrong for a minute. GPS is noisy now but never drifts. Fusion is the discipline of blending witnesses in proportion to how much each deserves:

K = P / (P + R)   — my uncertainty / (my uncertainty + sensor uncertainty) The Kalman gain, in words. Predict from what you have, measure, and blend by relative trust — then recompute the trust and do it again. The complementary filter (θ̂ = α·(θ̂ + ω·dt) + (1−α)·θref, α ≈ 0.95–0.98) is the same handshake with the proportion chosen once; Kalman is the grown-up version with the bookkeeping done every step. This page ships the Kalman version: a scalar filter per axis, predicting from odometry and correcting from GPS fixes every 0.22 s.

The position fusion is the genuine article, and it earns its keep in the receipts below: over a 90-second Fusion patrol the fused position error runs at roughly a third to a tenth of the raw odometry error, and inside the GPS-denied tunnel the covariance honestly grows (to 64.5 of its 72 cap) and contracts again when fixes return. Uncertainty that admits itself is the whole point of the layer.

The heading side is where the repair lives. As shipped, the filter's heading correction read the rover's true heading — its "measurement" was the answer, so the fused heading could not drift and could not be fooled, and the Magnet & Gyro Drift lab's central phenomenon could never appear in the fused estimate. Repaired, the correction consumes the page's own compass, with the filter's existing judgement intact: it already trusted the compass less when the distortion meter ran high (the gain is damped by the measured distortion), the standard honest treatment. The measured result is the most instructive pair of numbers on this page. With a clean compass, the honest filter matches the old one exactly — fused error max 2.92° against the 5.5 °/s bias, because where the compass is honest the truthful update is the old update. Park the rover beside the magnet and the same filter now absorbs the compass's whole lie: fused heading error −29.98° after 1 s, −63.39° after 5 s, settled at −67.25° — the damping buys about two seconds of grace, not immunity. That is not the filter failing. That is the filter believing its compass, the way every real compass-trusting filter does, and it is why real robots cross-check their compasses instead of worshipping them.

The honest box — bends that remain, on the record

Repairs vs bends. The four fixes above were the claims that were false. What follows measured true-but-tilted, and ships documented rather than altered — the collection's usual split, and the audit's.

The σ slider is no sensor's σ (table above) — set "3.0 cm" and you get 0.6–7.4 cm depending on which readout you watch.

The scope traces are not on a common scale: in Range mode the Kalman error is plotted ×2 against the same 180 full-scale as odometry's ×1, so the two lines under one legend cannot be compared by eye (the ×2 happens to penalise the Kalman line).

Ice slips the estimate, not the rover: the true motion is identical on and off ice; only the bookkeeping corrupts. A defensible seam for a sensors workshop — but "Icy Wheel Slip" reads as though the wheels lose grip in the world.

Auto Patrol grinds: a colliding step cancels rotation as well as translation, so in contact the rover cannot turn away. Measured 60 s path lengths: Fusion 2,310 px, Tunnel 379, Slip 445, Glass vs. Foam 144; in Magnet & Gyro Drift the rover spends 88% of steps in bumper contact. The presets are drivable by hand; the patrol is the weak driver.

Small mismatches: the sonar's drawn wave arcs span ±14.9° while its sampled cone is ±9.7°; a code comment claims the specular cutoff is ">42°" where cos⁻¹(0.72) = 43.9°; the compass on this page is noise-free (real magnetometers jitter), which is part of why the repaired fusion's clean-compass numbers look so tidy.

And the standing cautions for every sensor on this page: the sim's noise is tidy Gaussian jitter — real noise spikes, drops out, and echoes off the other wall. Temperature moves everything (sound speed ~0.6 m/s per °C; gyro bias wanders with it too). Real gyro bias is not a constant but a slow random walk that won't sit still long enough to calibrate away. And every number arrives late — a sonar's echo window, a camera's exposure, a filter's lag — describing the world as it was. Sensors report numbers, not facts; this page is a safe place to learn how wide that gap can get.

Choosing and trusting a sensor, in one pass

Ask four questions of any sensor before it joins your robot. What question does my robot actually need answered? What does this sensor measure — not what is it sold as? What makes its number lie, and how big is the lie when it does? And who checks it — another sensor, a sanity limit, a human? The room is arranged around those questions: Motors move on numbers like these, Control is only as honest as the Sensors feeding it, and Simulation is where you get to lie to all of them on purpose and watch what breaks.
