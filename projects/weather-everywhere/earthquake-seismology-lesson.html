Earthquake P/S Waves, Core Shadow & Triangulation — the lesson

The exhibit has two rooms. In the whole-Earth room, a real PREM velocity model of the planet is ray-traced live: P and S rays are integrated through the mantle with a Runge–Kutta solver, refracted at the core–mantle boundary by Snell's law, blocked or bounced where physics says they should be, and timed to a draggable station anywhere from 8° to 180°. In the regional room, three stations on a 1,000 × 640 km map time P and S rings from a hidden quake so you can triangulate it. The audit drove the shipped code headlessly, called its own ray tracer and seismogram functions directly, and checked the numbers against independent seismology — including published IASP91/TauP travel times. Verdict up top: this is one of the most physically honest exhibits in the collection. Its travel times land within about 1% of IASP91, its shadow zones fall where the real ones do, its S−P triangulation recovers a hidden epicentre exactly, and its PREM layer numbers are the real ones. What it does not do is magnitude: there is no energy readout anywhere, and the seismogram's shaking grows by only ×1.42 per magnitude unit — not the ×10 amplitude (×31.6 energy) the real scale demands. And inside the famous shadow zone it shows total P silence, where real stations still record a core phase.

False claim — flagged prominently

FALSE — inside the 104°–140° “shadow,” the page shows no P arrival at all; the real Earth still delivers one. Driven at station distances of 110°, 122° and 140° (20 km focus), the exhibit's own arrival solver returns tP = null — no P phase, of any kind, only the slow surface waves — and its preset banner invites you to drag the station in “to see P & S vanish.” The shadow is real, but it is a shadow for direct P. Real seismograms inside that band record diffracted P creeping around the core (Pdiff) and, more importantly, PKIKP — the P wave that crosses the liquid outer core and pierces the inner core. A documented example: stations in Armenia at ≈ 120° from a November 2026 Peru earthquake recorded PKIKP as their first arrival, at 18 min 36 s. The exhibit itself computes PKIKP correctly once you pass ≈ 141° (at 150°–180° it returns PKIKP at 19:42–20:07) — it simply does not let that branch reach into the shadow band where, in reality, it is also the first thing a sensitive station sees. Read the shadow on the page as “no direct P or S,” which is true, not as “nothing arrives,” which is not.

What this is

  • The whole-Earth room (Mode A) is a genuine ray tracer, not an animation pretending to be one. Wave speeds come from a PCHIP interpolation of PREM (Dziewonski & Anderson, 1981) mantle knots plus analytic outer- and inner-core profiles; each ray is a Hamiltonian RK4 integration in spherical geometry, with explicit Snell refraction, total internal reflection and S-wave blocking handled at the core–mantle boundary (3,480 km radius) and inner-core boundary (1,221.5 km radius). Station arrival times are interpolated from the traced ray fan — they are computed, not scripted.
  • The regional room (Mode B) is a flat-map triangulation lab with fixed crustal speeds — vP = 6.20 km/s, vS = 3.55 km/s, surface 3.05 km/s — three draggable stations, a hidden-epicentre challenge, and a fault-rupture / liquefaction preset with two sediment basins that amplify shaking.
  • What is not in the exhibit at all: no energy or TNT readout at any magnitude, no amplitude-versus-magnitude claim in words, no Gutenberg–Richter frequency law, no Mercalli / intensity scale, and no use of the word “Richter.” The magnitude slider is labelled Mw (moment magnitude) throughout, and that label is used consistently — there is no Richter conflation to correct. The magnitude findings below are therefore about the one place magnitude does act: the seismogram's amplitude.

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out, and its own functions were called directly: premP() / premS() for the velocity model, traceSingleRay() and rebuildEarthRays() for the ray fans, updateStationArrivalsA() for station arrivals at 14 distances and 5 depths, applyPreset() for every museum preset, and evalEarthSeismogram() / evalRegSeismogram() for the waveforms. Independent checks were computed from first principles (the S−P distance factor, 101.5·M energy scaling, √3 velocity ratio) and against published IASP91/TauP travel times for a station at 75°. Nothing in the guest file was edited.

What's true — the credit side

  • The travel times are real. At the default 75° station the exhibit's ray tracer gives P at 11:34 and S at 21:16; the IASP91 model computed with TauP — the standard independent reference — gives 11:42 and 21:21 for the same geometry. Agreement within 8 and 5 seconds over a 12,000-second problem, from a browser page integrating its own rays, is a genuine pass, and the lag grows smoothly and monotonically from 1:52 at 10° to 11:39 at 100° exactly as the real travel-time curves do.
  • The shadow zones are earned, not painted. The P shadow's edges come out of the traced fan — direct P rays stop surfacing, core-refracted PKP/PKIKP rays start at ≈ 141° — and the S shadow is total above ≈ 103° because the code really does set vS = 0 in the liquid outer core and reflect S back into the mantle as ScS. The single most important discovery in seismology — that the outer core is liquid — falls out of this model the way it fell out of the real data.
  • The PREM numbers on the panel are the model's numbers. The printed “13.7 → 8.1 km/s” plunge at the core–mantle boundary, the layer table's velocity ranges, the 2,891 km boundary depth and the printed Snell invariant p = r·sin i / v(r) were all checked against the code's own velocity functions and match — the panel and the physics are the same set of books, which is rarer in this collection than it should be.
  • Triangulation is mathematically exact. The printed formula d = ΔtS−P · vPvS/(vP − vS) is the correct constant-velocity lag–distance relation; with the lab's speeds it gives 8.306 km per second of lag, and three lags alone were enough for an independent search to recover the hidden epicentre to the kilometre.
  • Depth does the right things. A 600 km focus shortens body-wave travel times, narrows the P shadow, and suppresses surface-wave amplitude to about a quarter — all three are real deep-focus signatures, and all three emerge from the code rather than from preset-specific scripting.
  • Magnitude correctly does not change arrival times. Sliding Mw from 5.0 to 9.5 leaves every arrival untouched and changes only the shaking — which is exactly right: travel time is a property of the Earth and the geometry, not of how big the quake was.

Bends named honestly

Magnitude is a volume knob, not the magnitude scale. The seismogram's amplitude grows as 1.42M − 7.5 in Mode A (1.38M − 6.8 in Mode B) and is then clamped per phase, so the full slider sweep M5 → M9.5 changes the measured peak trace only ×3.4. On the real moment-magnitude scale, amplitude grows ×10 per unit (×31,623 over that sweep) and radiated energy ×31.6 per unit (×106.75 over the sweep). Nothing on the page states an energy or amplitude number, so nothing printed is false — but a student who internalises “M9.5 shakes about three times harder than M5” has been mis-taught by the picture. The formula to carry away is the one the page never shows:

E ∝ 101.5 · M+1 magnitude = ×31.6 energy · +2 = ×1,000 energy · amplitude ×10 per unit

The seismogram waveforms are synthetic packets, not propagated waves. Name it plainly: the arrival times are computed by the ray tracer, but the trace itself is wavePacket() — a hand-built burst of three sine harmonics under a fast-rise, exponential-decay envelope, fired at each arrival time, plus a small deterministic sine “background” (not random noise). That is a reasonable, clearly synthetic seismogram; it is not a waveform solved from the wave equation, and it carries no dispersion, no phase conversions (no SKS, no pP depth phases) and no coda scattering.

The “Draw Triangulation Circles” button peeks. The challenge asks you to measure S−P lags and derive the radii — and the maths works, as the audit's independent recovery showed. But the button itself does not use any lag you measured: it reads the true station-to-epicentre distances straight out of the state and draws those circles (and reveals the epicentre). The lesson is sound; the button is the answer key, not your working.

Surface waves get one speed and no dispersion. Real Rayleigh waves arrive as a long dispersed train — at 75°, published models spread it over roughly 36–44 minutes, fast long periods first. The exhibit uses a single 3.45 km/s average, giving one arrival (40:20 at 75°, inside the real window) and one packet. Fair as an average; not how a real trace looks.

The reported shadow edge has a floor under it. The code says it extracts the shadow boundaries “from our real PREM rays,” and mostly it does — but the extraction starts from seeded values (direct P pre-set to 103°, core P to 143°) that act as floors/ceilings, and the traced fan's own deepest direct P ray actually surfaced at 99.4°. The printed 103°–141° agrees with the textbook 104°–140°, so the number you read is right; it is just not quite as purely measured as the comment claims.

“Liquefaction” is amplification, not liquefaction. The preset multiplies S and surface-wave amplitude inside two sediment basins (up to ×2.35, both values verified) and adds a second rupture pulse. Basin amplification is real physics and the contrast with the bedrock station (×1.00) is the right lesson — but no pore pressure, soil failure or ground deformation is modelled. The name promises one phenomenon more than the code delivers.

Small panel-vs-model mismatches. The layer table lists crustal vP as 6.2–6.8 km/s, while the model's own crust-to-Moho profile runs 6.20 at the surface up to 7.95 just above the Moho — the table's range describes typical crustal rock, the profile includes the Moho jump. And the regional lab's surface-wave speed (3.05 km/s) differs from the global room's (3.45 km/s) without comment; both are plausible crustal averages, for different paths.
