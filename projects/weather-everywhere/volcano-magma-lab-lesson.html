Volcano Magma Viscosity, Gas & Plumes — the lesson

The exhibit is a magma-chemistry sandbox: sliders for silica (45–76 wt%) and dissolved gas (0.3–7.5 wt%), a magma chamber that pressurises, traps or bleeds bubbles, and erupts as anything from a Hawaiian lava fountain to a collapsing caldera, with six named presets. Its core physics — the viscosity engine — is genuinely good: every direction the audit pushed it, it moved the way real magma moves, across roughly the right number of orders of magnitude. What does not survive measurement is the number it prints on the eruption afterwards. Verdict up top: the magma is honest, the VEI label is not — VEI here is a plume-height lookup with no volume anywhere in it, it grades an effusive Kīlauea fountain as VEI 3, and the preset sold as a “VEI 7 super-eruption” never once displays VEI 7.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE — the Hawaiian preset does not erupt at VEI 1; it settles at VEI 3. The telemetry's opening state promises VEI 1 for Kīlauea-style effusive basalt, and real Kīlauea eruptions are VEI 0–1 (lava fountains a few hundred metres high). Driven headlessly for 60 simulated seconds, the preset's own code held a 7.8 km plume and VEI 3 — a VEI that in the real scale means 0.01–0.1 km³ of bulk ejecta. The mechanism is visible: the code's own lava-fountain formula gives ≈ 0.9 km, but the decorative gas-plume puffs are assigned a level of neutral buoyancy of ≈ 11.2 km, the measured plume is taken from the highest puff, and the VEI table converts anything above 6 km into VEI 3. The gentlest preset in the room is graded as a respectable explosive eruption by its own instruments.

FALSE — “Yellowstone Caldera (VEI 7)” is a super-eruption that never reaches VEI 7, and VEI 7 is the wrong label anyway. Driven for 180 simulated seconds the preset peaked at plume 33.6 km, VEI 6 — never 7. Its VEI-7 branch demands plume > 36 km and vent > 68 m together; the plume never got there even after the caldera had fully collapsed (collapse progress 1.00). And the label itself understates the real volcano: Yellowstone's caldera-forming eruptions (Huckleberry Ridge ≈ 2,450 km³, Lava Creek ≈ 1,000 km³) are VEI 8, and “super-eruption” is the name for VEI 8 — the banner uses the word while the button denies it the number.

FALSE — the viscosity telemetry prints a logarithm wearing a Pa·s unit. The card is headed “Viscosity (log₁₀)” but renders the value as, for example, “1.6 Pa·s” for the basalt preset and “6.5 Pa·s” for Yellowstone. Those are log₁₀ values: the model's own viscosities are 101.6 ≈ 39 Pa·s and 106.5 ≈ 3 × 10⁶ Pa·s. Read at face value the readout is wrong by up to six orders of magnitude. (The Field Guide text gets it right — it writes 101.6 Pa·s — so the page disagrees with itself.)

FALSE BY CONSTRUCTION — a Pinatubo can never be VEI 6 here. Pinatubo 1991 was a dacite eruption, VEI 6, ≈ 10 km³, column ≈ 40 km. In this code VEI 6 is gated behind silica ≥ 72% or an already-collapsing caldera; a dacite at 63–65% silica with a 40 km column caps out at VEI 5, however long it erupts. VEI in the real scale is set primarily by erupted bulk volume; this exhibit computes no volume, no mass and no duration at all — two eruptions with the same plume height get the same VEI whether one lasts a minute or a week.

What this is

  • The chemistry core is one formula, run every frame: temperature is derived from silica, and log₁₀ viscosity is built from silica, dissolved gas and a transient “basalt pulse” heat term. Everything else — bubble rise, pressure bleed, dome growth, eruption style, edifice shape — reads from that viscosity.
  • The chamber is a pressure balance: supply pumps pressure in, an open low-viscosity conduit bleeds it off, a viscous dome traps it until the cap-rock threshold fails and the vent uncorks. Bubbles rise through chamber and conduit at a viscosity-dependent speed and swell as they climb.
  • The eruption readout — plume height and “Est. VEI” — is measured off the ash-puff particles: plume is the altitude of the highest puff, and VEI is a plume-height threshold table with two special gates (submarine ⇒ VEI ≤ 1; collapse or very silicic + tall plume ⇒ VEI 6–7).

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out and a seeded random generator. The audit called the exhibit's own updateChemistryMetrics() across its slider ranges, loaded every preset through its own loadPreset(), and drove its own step() at fixed dt = 0.05 s for 30–180 simulated seconds per preset, recording temperature, log-viscosity, style classification, plume height and VEI from the state the code itself maintained. Nothing in the guest file was edited. Independent checks are the standard reference ranges: basalt ≈ 10–100 Pa·s at eruption temperature, rhyolite up to 10⁶–10⁹ Pa·s; VEI as a logarithmic bulk-volume scale (each step ×10: VEI 4 = 0.1–1 km³, VEI 5 = 1–10 km³, VEI 8 > 1,000 km³), with column height as its secondary criterion.

log₁₀ η = 1.35 + ((SiO₂ − 45)/31)1.3 × 7.25 − 0.22 × gas − 0.55 × pulseHeat, clamped to [1.2, 8.9] The exhibit's viscosity engine, exactly as shipped. Silica is the big lever (up to +7.25 orders), dissolved gas a small linear one (−0.22 per wt%), and heat enters only through the basalt-pulse term — there is no independent temperature slider; temperature itself is derived from silica, 1,205 °C at 45% falling to 760 °C at 76%.

Eruption style — the mapping, tested on a grid

The style classifier was driven across the slider corners, not just the presets. Its skeleton is the textbook one, and it holds: basalt at any gas level (even 7.5 wt%) stays Hawaiian Effusive; 52.5–59% silica gives Strombolian Bursts; 59%+ gives a Vulcanian Dome; and Plinian requires both silica ≥ 65% and gas ≥ 4.2 wt% — a dry rhyolite (76% SiO₂, 0.3% gas) correctly refuses to go Plinian and sits as a dome, while the same silica wet (7.5% gas) goes Plinian. Edifice follows composition: Shield below 53%, Composite Cone to 62%, Stratovolcano above. Low viscosity → effusive, high viscosity + volatiles → explosive: that central claim passes, in both directions, at every corner tested.

Chamber, bubbles and buoyancy — direction checks

  • Bubbles rise slower in stickier magma — pass. Bubble rise speed is 115 px/s in basalt falling toward 7 px/s in rhyolite (×~16 range, ∝ 1/viscosity in spirit, Stokes-style), and the passive pressure bleed scales as (1 − viscosityNorm)2.2: an open basalt conduit bleeds pressure ≈ 230× faster than a rhyolite one at the same pressure. Runny magma degasses and stays calm; sticky magma traps gas and pressurises. That is the real mechanism, in the right direction, with a big ratio.
  • Bubbles swell as they climb — pass. Conduit bubbles grow as they rise (radius capped at 11 px, growth faster at higher gas content) — decompression expansion, the right direction: pressure drops toward the vent, exsolved gas expands.
  • But bubbles are never born from pressure — bend. Bubble number is set by gas content alone (target ≈ 28 + 14 × gas wt%); nothing spawns more bubbles when chamber pressure falls. Real exsolution is pressure-driven — magma saturated at depth foams as it ascends — and the exhibit models the expansion half of that story, not the nucleation half. Its own Field Guide claim that basalt injection “triggers volatile exsolution” is, in code, a pressure-and-heat spike (+16 + 2.4 × gas MPa, −0.55 log viscosity at full pulse), not an exsolution event.
  • Plume buoyancy — direction right, targets generous. Ash puffs rise to a level of neutral buoyancy and spread into an umbrella cloud, capped at the 40 km ruler top. The targets assigned are 3.5 + 18.5 × power + 1.6 × gas + 0.06 × vent-width km: 36.6 km for a Vesuvius-power core puff and the 39.5 km ceiling for Yellowstone-power — the right neighbourhood for great Plinian columns (Pinatubo ≈ 40 km), and exactly the generosity that inflates the small presets in the table above.

What's true — the credit side

  • Every viscosity direction is correct, simultaneously. Silica up ⇒ viscosity up (≈ 7 orders across the slider); gas up ⇒ viscosity down; heat up ⇒ viscosity down. No drive the audit ran produced a wrong-signed response.
  • The magnitudes land in the real bands at both ends — tens of Pa·s for basalt, 10⁷–10⁸ Pa·s for dry rhyolite — and the preset ordering (Kīlauea 39 → Strombolian 320 → St. Helens 8 × 10³ → Vesuvius 7 × 10⁴ → Yellowstone 3 × 10⁶ Pa·s) is monotonic in silica exactly as the real volcanoes' magmas order.
  • Style follows viscosity + gas, not either alone — the Plinian gate needing both high silica and high gas, and the dry-rhyolite dome, are the two subtlest correct behaviours in the room.
  • Two preset VEIs are right: Vesuvius holds VEI 5 at a 27 km column (79 AD: VEI 5, ≈ 30 km+), and St. Helens reaches VEI 5 at 28 km (1980: VEI 5, column ≈ 24–31 km). Where the preset is a genuinely big explosive eruption, the plume table and the real scale agree.
  • The guide's framing physics is sound: SiO₄ polymerisation with silica, tropopause at ~12–14 km, umbrella clouds at the level of neutral buoyancy, PDCs from column collapse, ring-fault caldera collapse on chamber evacuation (the code really does gate collapse on chamber fill falling below 0.56 at silica ≥ 67%), and hydrostatic suppression of submarine explosivity.

Bends named honestly

VEI is a plume lookup, not the volume scale it is named after. The thresholds themselves are defensible as the scale's secondary, column-height criterion (VEI 5 above 25 km matches; VEI 4 above 15 km and VEI 3 above 6 km sit inside the published 10–25 km and 3–15 km bands). But no volume, duration or mass is ever computed, so the defining ×10-per-step volume ladder (VEI 4 = 0.1–1 km³, VEI 5 = 1–10 km³, VEI 8 > 1,000 km³) is neither reproduced nor contradicted — it is simply absent, behind a label that promises it.

The gas term is linear and small. Dissolved water's real effect on viscosity is strongly non-linear — the first wt% does most of the work. Here every wt% buys the same −0.22 log units, so wet rhyolite (÷38 across the full gas range) is plausible while the shape of the curve is not, and at the basalt end the 1.2 log floor silently eats the effect entirely above ≈ 3 wt% gas.

Temperature is silica in a costume. Because temperature is derived from silica, the model's “hotter ⇒ thinner” behaviour is really “less silicic ⇒ thinner,” plus the basalt-pulse term. A superheated rhyolite — the kind basalt injection actually makes — can only be faked through that one transient pulse.

Vesuvius's chemistry is borrowed. The preset gives Vesuvius 67.5% silica dacite; the real 79 AD magma was an alkaline phonolite at considerably lower silica, explosive because it was volatile-rich and evolved, not because it was dacitic. The VEI and plume come out right; the rock that supposedly made them is not Vesuvius's rock. (The reverse bend sits one preset up: St. Helens is button-labelled “Andesite” while the code's own classifier calls its 63% magma Dacite — and there the code is the accurate one; 1980's magma was dacite.)

St. Helens can detonate but cannot be named for it. The style classifier only awards “Plinian” at silica ≥ 65%, so the St. Helens preset — measured at VEI 5 with a 28 km column — keeps the label “Vulcanian Dome” throughout. Label and instruments disagree on the page at the same moment.

Caldera collapse is fast theatre. Yellowstone's chamber starts at 0.68 fill against a 0.56 collapse gate, so in the driven run collapse passed 0.35 within ≈ 4 simulated seconds and completed (1.00) inside the run — a process the exhibit needs for its finale, at a speed no timescale on the page justifies.
