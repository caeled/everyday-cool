Plate Tectonics, Convection & Subduction — the lesson

Tectonica is a cross-section sandbox with four regimes — Subduction, Mid-Ocean Ridge, Continental Collision and Hotspot — driven by three dials that carry real units: plate velocity in cm/yr, a geologic clock in Myr, and a slab-dip slider in degrees. That makes it auditable in a way most dioramas are not: its own functions can be driven headlessly and its numbers compared with the published Earth. The geometry mostly honours the dials — the slab really does steepen as you raise the angle, the magnetic stripes really are mirror-symmetric, and the Hawaiian chain really does age away from the plume at the rate its own drift implies. Verdict up top: the machinery is honest and the Pacific-side numbers are good — but the preset named for the Mid-Atlantic spreads two and a half times too fast, and the chain's oldest named island, Midway, is labelled less than half its real age.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE — the “Mid-Ocean Ridge” preset does not spread at a Mid-Atlantic rate. The preset is subtitled “Mid-Atlantic seafloor & stripes” and defaults to 6.2 cm/yr, split by its own on-screen tags into 3.1 cm/yr per flank. The real Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at a full rate of ≈ 2.5 cm/yr (≈ 1.25 cm/yr per flank) — the exhibit's default is ≈ 2.5× too fast. 6.2 cm/yr is a perfectly real spreading rate, but it belongs to a fast ridge such as parts of the East Pacific Rise (full rates ≈ 6–15 cm/yr), not to the Atlantic ridge the preset names. Run the preset's own arithmetic forward and the error compounds: at 6.2 cm/yr a 4,500 km Atlantic would open in ≈ 73 Myr; at the real ≈ 2.5 cm/yr the same width takes ≈ 180 Myr — which is the actual story, breakup beginning ≈ 175–200 Ma. The page makes no Atlantic-width or Pangaea claim of its own (the audit searched: none exists), so nothing else on the page contradicts this — the preset's number simply belongs to a different ocean.

FALSE — Midway Atoll is not 11.5 Myr old. The hotspot chain labels its islands with ages in Myr, and four of them check out (table below). Midway does not: the page seeds it at 11.5 Myr; the real Midway volcano is ≈ 28 Myr old, some 2,400 km from Hawaiʻi. The error is visible inside the page's own arithmetic, with no outside reference needed. The exhibit's drift rule, measured by driving it, ages an island at 0.0142 Myr per pixel travelled from the plume — and the first four islands sit almost exactly on that line (0.0136–0.0140 Myr/px). Midway sits at 0.0218 Myr/px and the “Emperor Guyot” at 0.0364 Myr/px: the chain's age = distance ÷ rate line, straight for four islands, bends sharply for the last two — and even the bent values undershoot reality (the Emperor chain is ≈ 47–81 Myr old; no single “Emperor Guyot” age exists, but nothing in that chain is as young as 24 Myr at Midway-like distances).

What this is

  • Four cross-sections, one engine. A subduction zone with trench, dipping slab, Wadati–Benioff hypocenters and an arc of named volcanoes; a mid-ocean ridge extruding mirror-image magnetic stripes; two continents colliding into a Himalayan belt with a crustal root; and a stationary plume stamping an age-progressive island chain. Mantle tracers ride a per-regime convection field underneath all four.
  • What it does not claim. There is no Pangaea timeline, no supercontinent cycle, no Atlantic-width figure and — importantly — no horizontal distance scale and no seafloor-age readout. Stripes carry polarity arrows but no ages, so the classic age = distance ÷ half-spreading-rate check cannot be performed by a reader from the screen; it can only be performed on the code, which is what this audit did (the drift-per-pixel measurements above are the substitute).
  • Every preset was tested — all four regimes were loaded through the page's own switchRegime() and run, not just read.

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out and a seeded random generator, at a 1,200 × 800 viewport. Its own animation frame was stepped at 16.7 ms, its own getSlabPoint() and yToDepthKm() were called directly for slab geometry, and its stripe, island and hypocenter arrays were read after real simulated time had passed. Nothing in the guest file was edited; the exhibit file is byte-for-byte the integrated copy (sha256 91451e9b…).

age = distance ÷ half-spreading rate The seafloor-age rule the exhibit teaches by picture but never prints: crust at a ridge is age zero, and each flank ages linearly as it rides away. The page's stripes obey the picture — polarity mirrors exactly — but with no kilometre scale and no age labels, the rule itself is untestable from the screen. On the hotspot chain, where ages are printed, the same rule is testable — and it is exactly there that Midway and the Emperor Guyot fall off the line.

What's true — the credit side

  • The stripe mirror is real, not painted. Both flanks are injected at the axis with the same current polarity and scrolled outward together; after simulated time the audit found 48 of 48 age-ranked pairs still matching. That is the actual Vine–Matthews mechanism — new crust records the field, then splits — implemented, not illustrated.
  • The slab bends the way slabs bend. At the default 38° the curve enters shallow (≈ 24°) and steepens with depth (≈ 50–63°), ending near the 660 km discontinuity — the measured behaviour of real Wadati–Benioff zones, whose hypocenters the page then scatters along that same curve out to ≈ 593 km.
  • The Pacific numbers are the right numbers. 8.6 cm/yr of plate drift over the plume, island ages for Maui, Oʻahu and Kauaʻi within a few tenths of a million years of the published values, and a dynamic aging rule (0.0142 Myr/px) that the young half of the chain actually sits on.
  • The Andes numbers are right too: 7.4 cm/yr convergence, a trench in the 8–9 km band, arc volcanoes growing toward Andean heights, and dehydration bubbles released from the slab in the depth window where the regime text says flux melting begins (80–150 km — the text and the bubble spawn window agree).
  • The deep-Earth furniture is standard: 100 / 410 / 660 / 2,900 km horizons, a ≈ 4,000 °C outer core label, and a mean heat-flow readout (88 mW/m²) that lands on Earth's measured ≈ 87 mW/m².

Bends named honestly

One preset, two margins. The subduction preset is subtitled “Andes / Cascadia” but carries one velocity, 7.4 cm/yr — right for the Nazca Plate under South America, roughly double the ≈ 3–4 cm/yr at which the Juan de Fuca Plate converges on Cascadia. A Cascadia visitor is watching Andean speed.

“Shortening” that is really convergence. The collision preset's 5.4 cm/yr is labelled Shortening Rate. India still converges on Eurasia at ≈ 4–5 cm/yr in total, but only ≈ 1.8–2 cm/yr of that is taken up as shortening across the Himalaya itself; the rest is distributed across Tibet and beyond. The number is defensible, the label is one step off.

The arc sits far from the trench. At the default dip, the page's own slab reaches the 80–150 km flux-melting window at ≈ 0.51 of the screen width, but its volcanoes stand at 0.60, 0.70 and 0.80 — above slab that is already ≈ 250 km deep and deeper. Real volcanic fronts sit ≈ 100–200 km from the trench, above ≈ 100 km of slab; the exhibit stretches that offset so the arc fits the scenery. The mechanism is right; the map is widened.

Steep sliders overshoot the mantle. At 65° the slab curve runs into the page's core–mantle-boundary clamp: its last segments fold to 145–180° in pixel space and its tip reports ≈ 2,566 km deep. Real steep slabs stall, tear or deflect near 660 km (a few are imaged deeper, none dive at 65° to the core). Between 18° and ≈ 50° the geometry is sound; treat the top of the slider as theatre.

Reversal chrons are stretched. Auto-reversals fire every 0.9–2.3 Myr of geologic time (measured mean ≈ 1.55 Myr). Earth's field has flipped far more restlessly — a mean chron of ≈ 0.2–0.3 Myr over the last 10 Myr. The stripes therefore record a calmer field than the real one; each coloured band represents more time than its real counterpart.

Stripe widths are scenery, not a timescale. Fresh stripes are injected at a fixed ≈ 22 px spacing and the pre-seeded belts are a uniform 28 px, whatever the polarity interval that produced them — so band width on screen does not encode chron duration the way real stripe widths (duration × spreading rate) do. Combined with the missing kilometre scale, the ridge teaches the pattern of seafloor spreading faithfully and its arithmetic not at all.

The divergent scroll runs hot against its own tags. The flank tags promise plateVel ÷ 2 per side, but the stripe belts are scrolled at 0.65 × the page's scroll rate rather than 0.50 × — in pixel terms the crust moves ≈ 30% faster than the labels beside it claim. With no distance scale this changes no printed number, but it is the same two-sets-of- books habit: label and motion computed in two places, never reconciled.

Subsidence is brisk. Off-plume islands sink at 1.35 height-units per Myr and bottom out at the guyot floor within the run — the direction (cooling lithosphere subsides, islands drown to guyots) is right and is even labelled as it happens; the pace is compressed like everything else on a 0.6 Myr/s clock.
