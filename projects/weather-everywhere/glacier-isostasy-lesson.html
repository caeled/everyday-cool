Glacial Isostasy & Rebound — the lesson

The exhibit is a 240 km cross-section — mountain crest to deep ocean, 200 grid cells — where ice grows and flows by Glen's creep law, carves its bed, bulldozes moraine, calves icebergs, moves global sea level, and bends the crust down into the mantle, which then flows back when the ice leaves. The isostasy at its heart is the part this audit drove hardest, and it is genuinely good: the Airy balance is exact to the last digit against its own densities, and the rebound is a clean exponential that returns to the original level without overshooting. Verdict up top: the buoyancy and the rebound clock are honest — but the “realistic forebulge” the code twice promises does not exist in the shipped mathematics. The flexure kernel is cut off before it can ever turn negative, so a pure ice load depresses the land beyond its edge and lifts nothing, anywhere.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE — there is no forebulge. The code comments say the flexure convolution “produces realistic forebulge!” (twice). A real elastic plate bends down under a load, flattens, and then rises into a low peripheral bulge beyond the load before settling — mathematically, the plate Green's function e−r(cos r + sin r) goes negative once r > 3π/4 ≈ 2.36, and that sign change is the bulge. The shipped kernel is truncated at ±46 cells with a flexural parameter of 24 cells, so r never exceeds 46/24 = 1.92 — the kernel never reaches its negative lobe. Measured directly from the shipped array: every one of its 93 weights is positive (minimum +0.00175, centre +0.0198, sum exactly 1.0000). A convolution of a downward load with an all-positive kernel can only push down. Driven with a pure 3,000 m ice block (cells 60–139, nothing else): equilibrium deflection at the block's centre −807 m, still −59 m a full 36 km beyond the ice edge, −1.4 m at 55 km beyond — and maximum deflection anywhere: 0.0 m. No uplift, at any distance, ever. What the page actually models is depression that leaks sideways, which is half of flexure — the spreading — without the other half, the bulge. (Small positive deflections do appear in some presets — up to +23 m in Post-Glacial GIA — but they come from erosion unloading and the domain edge, not from a bulge; see the bends.)

FALSE as seeded — “3 km ice load & 900 m crust sink.” That is the Ice Age Sheet preset's own subtitle. As the preset actually loads: maximum ice 1,150 m, local Airy target −320 m, flexural equilibrium −256 m, and the deflection actually applied on load only −115 m (the preset deliberately starts at 45% of equilibrium). The companion claim inside the Post-Glacial GIA preset — a comment promising it starts “fully depressed (~900 m!)” under 3,150 m of ice — measures −708 m at its deepest cell and −687 m under the ice centre: 21% short of 900. The shortfall is the model's own doing, and it is instructive: the preset pre-carves 280 m of erosion, and eroded rock is removed load, which buys back roughly 180 m of depression. Left running, the Ice Age Sheet does eventually blow past its subtitle — see the growth row in the table — so the claim fails in both directions: too big for the seed, too small for the run.

What this is

  • The load model is local Airy isostasy with the textbook densities, printed in its own legend: ice ρice = 917 kg/m³, crust 2,700, mantle ρm = 3,300. Each cell's target deflection is −(917·Hice + 2,700·(sediment − erosion) + water load) / 3,300.
  • The regional model convolves that Airy target with a 1-D elastic-plate kernel, e−r(cos r + sin r), flexural parameter ≈ 29 km — so the response is regional, not purely local: a narrow load is mostly supported by the plate's stiffness, a wide load is mostly not. That part is real, and measured below.
  • The clock is viscous relaxation: each step, w moves toward its equilibrium by a factor 1 − e−dt/τ, with τ set by the Mantle Viscosity slider (presets use 3.2–4.0 kyr; the slider runs 0.5–12 kyr). The formula chip on the page — τ·∂w/∂t = wflex(ρiH/ρm) − w — is exactly the equation the code integrates.
wAiry = − Hice · ρice / ρm = − Hice · 917 / 3300 = − 0.278 · Hice The independent check: a floating load displaces its own weight of mantle, so surface depression = ice thickness × ice density ÷ mantle density. (The ρm − ρice denominator you may have seen elsewhere gives the thickness of a crustal root below the original Moho — a different quantity.) The rule of thumb “depression ≈ ⅓ of ice thickness” assumes ρice/ρm ≈ 0.33; with the page's own densities the honest figure is 0.28, and that is what the page delivers.

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out, and its own functions — computeEquilibriumDeflection(), applyPreset() and stepPhysics() — were called directly: every preset loaded and read back, a pure uniform ice block driven on a clean bed, a narrow block driven beside it, and a full unload stepped forward 12 kyr with the ice held at zero so the relaxation curve could be read cell by cell. Nothing in the guest file was edited.

What's true — the credit side

  • The buoyancy is exact. Every preset's Airy centre ratio measured 0.2779 before flexure — the page's own densities, divided correctly, with no fudge factor hiding in the ice term. Where the ratio drops (0.218 in the GIA preset), the cause is visible in the same equation: sediment adds load, erosion removes it, both at crustal density 2,700.
  • Flexure is genuinely regional. The same 3,000 m of ice depresses the crust 807 m when it is 96 km wide and only 163 m when it is 12 km wide. That contrast — plate stiffness carrying narrow loads — is the single most important difference between Airy and flexural isostasy, and the exhibit has it, measurably.
  • The rebound clock is the right shape and the right size. A single exponential with τ = 3.2–4.0 kyr recovers 95% of the depression in ~10–12 kyr — the observed timescale over which Fennoscandia and Hudson Bay have risen since deglaciation (~10 kyr and still going). The preset note's “~4–6 kyr rebound like Fennoscandia & Hudson Bay” is a fair statement of the model's own behaviour at the top of its preset range.
  • No overshoot, no ringing. Unloaded, every cell approaches its target monotonically from below. A viscous mantle cannot bounce, and this one doesn't.
  • Erosion talks to isostasy. Carve 280 m of rock out of a valley and the model lightens that cell's load by 2,700 × 280 / 3,300 ≈ 229 m of equilibrium uplift before flexural smoothing. Glacial erosion really does drive rock uplift this way; most classroom demos leave the coupling out entirely.

Bends named honestly

The forebulge that appears isn't one. Positive deflections in the presets (up to +23 m in GIA, +7.5 m in Fjord and Milankovitch) sit at the far right edge of the domain or over the pre-carved trough. Their sources are erosion unloading and the convolution's edge handling (out-of-range cells clamp to the edge value, replicating it), not plate bending. A real forebulge also migrates — it collapses and moves inward as the ice retreats. Here the land beyond the ice simply un-depresses in place.

One time constant for a whole planet. Real glacial isostatic adjustment is a spectrum of relaxation times (a layered mantle answers at many τ at once), plus an instant elastic response the moment load changes. This model has the viscous exponential only: the instant the ice vanishes, uplift starts at full viscous speed with no elastic jump, and a single τ (slider 0.5–12 kyr) governs every wavelength at once.

Early rebound runs hot. Uplift rate is w/τ: from the GIA preset's −708 m at τ = 3.2 kyr that is 221 m/kyr ≈ 22 cm/yr at the start — plausible for the first centuries after a real unloading, but the same formula still gives ≈ 3 cm/yr when only 100 m of depression remains, about three times today's observed maxima (~1 cm/yr around Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia). Today's Earth is on the flat tail of a multi-τ decay; a single exponential can't be both as fast as this at the start and as slow as reality now.

The flexural reach is short and hard-cut. The flexural parameter is ≈ 29 km and the kernel stops dead at ±55 km (46 cells); continental lithosphere typically flexes over ~60–120 km. Beyond 55 km the model crust feels nothing of a load — no long tail — and at the domain edges the clamped convolution can manufacture small deflections of its own.

Sea level is eustatic-only, and its zero is per-preset. Sea level = −(ice volume − a baseline) × 0.35, clamped to [−135, +28] m. The baseline differs by preset (18–35 in the code's slice-volume units), so the same absolute ice sheet reads as different sea levels in different scenarios, and melting the GIA sheet out completely lands at +7 m rather than 0. What is missing by construction: the gravitational fingerprint — a melting ice sheet's mass pulls ocean water toward it, so when it melts, sea level falls near the former ice even as the global average rises. The page's sea surface is flat and uniform everywhere; the local relative sea level you see is only the bed moving under that flat surface, never the surface itself tilting.

The water load carries a quiet 0.55. In the load sum, the change in water depth is multiplied by 0.55 with no explanation on the page. Ice and rock enter at full weight; the ocean enters at fifty-five cents on the dollar. It mostly matters in the fjord and along the coast — exactly where water loading is a real effect worth doing at full strength.

After the ice, the land needn't come back to zero — correctly. In the audit's unload run the eroded trough's equilibrium target settled at up to +158 m above the pre-glacial datum (flexurally smoothed erosion unloading), while the deepest cell ended at −18 m. That is not an overshoot of the rebound; it is the isostatic price of the missing rock, and the exhibit's ghost line lets you see the bed finish above where it started over the trough. Worth knowing before you read “rebound” as “everything returns to normal.”
