Tsunami Shoaling — the lesson

A 100 km cross-section of ocean, 4,000 m deep at the left and rising to a coastal city at the right, driven by a real nonlinear shallow-water (Saint-Venant) solver — MUSCL reconstruction, an HLL Riemann flux, wet/dry shoreline handling, 380 cells. You can rupture a megathrust fault, drop a submarine landslide, splash an asteroid, sculpt the seafloor, and watch the wave slow down, steepen and grow as the water shallows. The audit drove the shipped solver headlessly and measured its fronts, amplitudes and wavelengths against shallow-water physics computed independently. Verdict up top: the wave-speed physics is genuinely right — measured front speeds track √(g·h) segment by segment, and Green's-law shoaling holds from the abyss down to about 400 m of water. Below that the wave stops obeying Green's law: its amplitude saturates near 5 m instead of climbing past 10 m, so the exhibit's own Green's-law envelope, drawn live on the canvas, visibly over-predicts the very wave underneath it in shallow water.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE in shallow water — the Green's Law envelope the exhibit draws. The dashed envelope (on by default) plots H = H0·(h0/h)1/4 all the way to the beach, capped only at 28 m. Anchored to the measured deep-water wave of the default megathrust (2.45 m at 4,000 m), Green's law predicts 7.7 m at 42 m depth and 10.1 m at 14 m depth. The solver's own wave, measured bin by bin as it crossed those depths, reached 5.14 m and 5.22 m — a growth of 2.1× where the envelope promises 4.1×. The envelope is honest physics in deep and mid-depth water (see the table below: at ~375 m the prediction and the measurement agree to within 1%), but as drawn it keeps climbing through exactly the zone where the real wave in this model saturates, from friction, partial reflection and its own breaking damping. A student comparing the dashed line to the water under it in the shallows is being shown a prediction the exhibit itself refutes.

FALSE — the “8–16+ m coastal shoaling surge” in the megathrust source note. The trigger code promises that its ~2.0 m deep-ocean wave becomes an 8–16+ m coastal surge. The deep half is right (measured 2.06 m at magnitude 1.0, 2.45 m at the preset's 1.15). The coastal half is not: measured coastal peak 4.78 m at magnitude 1.0 and 5.45 m at the preset, with maximum run-up elevation 2.41 m. Only at magnitude 1.9 — near the top of the slider — does the coastal peak reach 8.45 m, and 16 m was never produced at any setting tested.

FALSE — the asteroid's “dispersive ring.” The splashdown banner calls the outgoing wave a dispersive ring. This solver is the nonlinear shallow-water equations: its wave speed is c = √(g·h), with no dependence on wavenumber anywhere in the code, so by construction it cannot disperse — every Fourier component travels at the same speed. Real asteroid waves do disperse (they are short enough that deep-water dispersion matters), which is exactly why this model is the wrong tool for that one claim; inside the exhibit, the ring spreads because it is a collapsing cavity, not because of dispersion.

What this is

  • A real solver, not an animation. The page integrates the 1-D nonlinear shallow-water equations — ∂η/∂t + ∂(hu)/∂x = 0 and ∂u/∂t + u·∂u/∂x + g·∂η/∂x = −cf·u|u|/h — with a well-balanced second-order MUSCL/HLL finite-volume scheme, quadratic bottom friction, and wet/dry cells so the wave can actually run up the beach and flood the city plain. The equations printed in its Physics tab are the equations it solves.
  • Generation is seafloor displacement. A megathrust trigger raises (or, in drawdown polarity, differentially raises and drops) the seabed itself over a 12-second rupture, lifting the whole water column above it; a landslide shoves water with a moving sediment mass; an asteroid excavates a transient cavity. Those are the three real generation families, each implemented in its own way.
  • The reference physics used for comparison is textbook shallow-water theory, computed independently of the page: c = √(g·h), Green's law A ∝ h−1/4 (from conservation of energy flux E·cg with E ∝ A² and cg = √(g·h)), λ = c·T with T constant, and travel time as the integral ∫ dx / √(g·h(x)) over the page's own bathymetry.

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out and a seeded random generator. The page conveniently exposes its solver through a diagnostic hook (window.__TSUNAMI_SIM__), so the audit called its own stepShallowWater(), loadPreset() and trigger functions directly, stepped the simulation in 2-second increments for up to 2,600 simulated seconds per preset, and recorded the free surface η, the seabed, and the page's own telemetry from outside. Front speeds are arrival-time differences of the η > 1 m front between fixed gauges; amplitudes are the maximum η observed inside depth bins as the wave crossed them. Nothing in the guest file was edited.

Wave speed — claim vs measured

c = √(g·h)  →  198 m/s (713 km/h) at 4,000 m · 9.9 m/s (36 km/h) at 10 m The speed of a long wave depends only on depth. As the wave climbs the slope it must slow — and a wave that slows while its energy keeps arriving is a wave that grows. That is the whole exhibit in one line.

The page's headline speed is a formula it evaluates at the crest (√(g·hcrest) plus the local current). The audit instead timed the actual front between gauges on the standard megathrust run. Source-side gauges were excluded — the broad uplift dome contaminates them — and every clean segment lands within about 15% of √(g·h), most within 7%:

The wave really does travel at jet speed in the deep and at bicycle speed at the beach.

The energy-flux proxy tells the same story in one column: A²·√h, which Green's law holds constant, measured ≈ 379 for the propagated deep wave, 433 at ~1,000 m and 386 at ~375 m — conserved, as advertised — then 265 at ~130 m, 172 at ~42 m and 102 at ~14 m, only about a quarter of the deep value. The missing flux is not a bug: the slope partially reflects the wave, the quadratic bottom friction (cf = 0.0012, rising on reefs and land) eats it, and the model's own shallow-water breaking damping caps it. Green's law's assumptions — no friction, no reflection, amplitude small against depth — all fail together in the surf zone, in this model as in the real ocean. What is false is only the envelope line that keeps drawing the law where it no longer applies (see the false box).

The page's own “Shoaling Amp” HUD, which divides its coastal peak by its offshore peak, reads 2.22× at the end of the default run (2.45 m → 5.45 m) — an honest number, and the right headline: this wave roughly doubles, it does not quadruple.

Generation, steepening and breaking — what's true

  • Seafloor displacement → initial amplitude, measured. The megathrust lifts the seabed by 4.2 m × magnitude over 12 s. The deep wave that splits off shoreward measured 1.03 m at magnitude 0.5, 2.06 m at 1.0 and 3.90 m at 1.9 — almost exactly half the uplift, as linear theory expects when a broad uplift radiates both ways. Generation is linear in the slider; the coast is not (next point).
  • Steepening is real and measurable. The maximum surface slope |∂η/∂x| grew from 4.5×10−4 in deep water to 3.4×10−3 on the shelf (×7.6) and 6.8×10−3 in the shallows (×15). The front compresses as it slows — the mechanism that turns a barely-visible deep wave into a bore at the beach is in the solver, not just in the artwork.
  • Breaking is dissipation, honestly labelled as foam. There is no overturning in a shallow-water model — there can't be. What the page does is detect bore fronts (surface steepness, flow convergence, shallow depth) and render foam, plus apply a small turbulent damping where the wave-to-depth ratio exceeds ~0.65 in water shallower than 45 m. That damping is one of the reasons the amplitude saturates instead of following Green's law to the beach.
  • Drawdown is a source property, not a trick. Both polarities produce leading-depression N-wave behaviour somewhere: the drawdown preset leads with its trough at the shore, and even the crest-first megathrust drags a −1.46 m trailing trough behind it. The physics that empties harbours before some tsunamis is present in both directions.
  • Run-up is wet/dry physics, not a scripted flood line. Water first wetted dry land at 1,042 s on the default run and climbed to 2.41 m elevation, 2.37 km inland, then receded — the gauges show the shore oscillating (−1.46 m, later −0.7 m) long after the first surge, as a real tsunami's wave train does.

Bends named honestly

The magnitude slider is linear; earthquakes are not. The slider maps 0.5–1.9 to “Mw 8.7–9.5” and scales the uplift linearly with it (2.1 m → 8.0 m, a 3.8× range). Seismic moment grows as 101.5·Mw, so Mw 8.7 → 9.5 is a ~16× range in energy, not 3.8×. Worse, the coast saturates: coastal peak went 2.88 → 4.78 → 8.45 m and run-up only 1.09 → 2.14 → 2.93 m across that range. Treat the slider as “how big a wave,” not as a faithful moment-magnitude dial.

The displayed wavelength is a width, not a wavelength. There is no periodic wave train and no period T anywhere in the model — λ = c·T is printed in the equation box, but the HUD's λ is twice the full-width at half-maximum of a single long pulse. It behaves correctly (compressing with c, implied period steady within ~16%), but during the first minute it mostly measures the growing source dome — it ballooned to ~67 km before settling near 33 km.

The HUD speed is a formula at the crest, not a tracked front. It happens to agree with the audit's independently timed fronts within ~15%, but it is √(g·h) evaluated, not measured — and its “crest” is chosen by a scoring rule that favours shoreward cells, so late in a run it can sit on a secondary hump (it reported depths near 170 m while the main front was already at the beach).

“Coastal peak” counts the reef, in the defense preset. The telemetry's coastal peak is the maximum η over the shoreward third of the domain, which includes the offshore reef — hence 7.50 m in the defended run while the actual shore gauge saw 1.52 m. The defense's real effect (run-up 2.41 m → 0.29 m) is stronger than the headline numbers suggest; the headline numbers are just measuring the wrong water.

The fjord's “towering run-up” is a +7% effect. Its steep wall does deliver the wave faster and slightly higher (coastal 6.13 m vs 5.45 m), but measured run-up was 2.57 m against the standard margin's 2.41 m. Real fjord disasters (Lituya Bay, 524 m) are landslide-driven splash in confined water — a 2-D/3-D confinement effect a 1-D cross-section cannot represent. The banner promises the geography; the model delivers a ramp.

The last segment outruns √(g·h). Between 26 m and 7 m depth the front averaged 16.0 m/s against √(g·h) ≈ 11.4 m/s. That is bore celerity — a surge front propagates at roughly √(g·(h + η)) into still water — and it is also why the simple travel-time integral overestimates the wait. Named here because the equation box never mentions it.

Velocity is capped at 65 m/s and the asteroid is scaled by hand. The solver clamps water velocity for stability, and the asteroid's momentum injection carries an empirical ×7.5 factor. Neither changes any result in this lesson (measured surge speeds stayed under 5 m/s), but both are places where the model's hand rests on the scale.
