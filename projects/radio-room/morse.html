What you are looking at

Morse is a sender and trainer: type something, press Go, and hear it keyed in Morse code at real Morse timing. It is a port of Steven Powell's morse.py from his Bordeom workshop — the window, the controls and the timing are the Python program's, carried over line for line. Its header carries its dedication: For Dad (a ham since 1956).

The controls

Text — what to send. Characters with no Morse code (like #) are quietly skipped; everything else is sent as typed. Letter speed (5–40 WPM) is how fast the letters themselves are keyed. Overall speed is the Farnsworth control (below): leave "same as letter speed" ticked for ordinary Morse. Tone is the sidetone pitch, 300–1200 Hz. Repeats plays the message that many times with a pause of three word gaps between repeats — 0 means repeat until Stop. Export at least only affects Save WAV.

The timing — PARIS is the ruler

Morse speed is defined by one standard word: PARIS. A dit lasts 1200 / WPM milliseconds, a dah is 3 dits, the gap inside a letter is 1 dit, between letters 3, between words 7 — and on that timing PARIS plus its trailing word gap is exactly 50 dits, so sending PARIS at W words per minute takes exactly 60/W seconds. At 20 WPM a dit is exactly 60 ms and PARIS is 2.58 seconds of sound. Every number on this page comes from that one definition, exactly as in the original.

Farnsworth spacing — slow overall, fast letters

Learning to copy code letter-by-letter fails if the letters themselves slow down — you start counting dits instead of hearing each letter's shape. Farnsworth spacing keeps every letter at full letter speed and stretches only the gaps between letters and words until the overall speed is the slower one you asked for. One spacing unit is (60 / overall − 37.2 / letters) / 19 seconds: 3 units go between letters, 7 between words. (The 37.2 is PARIS's un-stretchable content — its tones and inside-letter gaps — counted in letter-speed seconds; the 19 is PARIS's stretchable gap units.) Set letter speed 20 and overall 10 and PARIS takes exactly 6.000 seconds — the proofs below hold the page to that.

Hide — listen first

Tick Hide dots and dashes and pressing Go plays the message without showing its code: you copy by ear, the way the skill is actually learned, then press Reveal to check yourself. It is the Python window's own learning aid, unchanged.

Save WAV

Save WAV renders the message to a downloadable morse.wav using the original's exact recipe: 44100 Hz, mono, 16-bit; every tone faded in and out over 5 ms with a raised cosine so there are no clicks; 0.2 s of silence at the head and 0.4 s at the tail; amplitude 0.6. Repeats follow the Repeats control — or, if Export at least is set, the message repeats in whole copies until the file is at least that long. An export is capped at 1800 seconds (half an hour — a WAV runs about 5 MB a minute), exactly as the original caps it.

The original

morse.py is a Python program: a small tkinter window, or the command line (python morse.py "CQ CQ" --wpm 15). On Windows its beeps come from winsound; here the same timeline is played by WebAudio, which is why this page works anywhere. The Stop button cuts in at once, like the original — its player wakes every 20 ms to check for a stop; this one silences the tone the moment you press it. The full write-up — the PARIS standard, the Farnsworth formula, the WAV recipe, and the proofs — is on the lesson page. The same code table and timing also power the sidetone in Skip, this room's skywave game.
