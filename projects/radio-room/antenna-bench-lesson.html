The Antenna Bench — the lesson

The bench cuts a half-wave dipole, draws where its signal goes, and shows what the feedline sees. Underneath it is the most-used antenna in radio and the three facts that govern it: a wire resonates when it is half a wavelength long — but a real wire is about 5% shorter than the free-space number; it radiates broadside and not at all off its ends; and its natural impedance is 73 Ω, not 50 Ω, so a "perfect" dipole on ordinary coax reads 1.46:1 and that is normal. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the physics, the math, the worked answers, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the bench.

The bench was built by Muse for Everyday Cool, at Steven Powell's direction. The dipole is the right first antenna for this room: his father spent five decades as a ham operator, and a dipole is the antenna a ham cuts first, argues about longest, and measures every other antenna against.

Why a half wave

Feed a wire in the middle with alternating current and the current has to go somewhere: it runs out toward the ends and back, sloshing like water in a bathtub. When the wire is exactly half a wavelength long, the slosh fits perfectly — the current is strongest at the centre, exactly where the feedline connects, and falls to zero at the two open ends, where there is nowhere left for it to go. That standing fit is resonance: at the resonant frequency the feedpoint sees a pure resistance, no reactance left over, and power flows out into space instead of rattling around in the matching. The geometry falls out of the fit: a half-wave dipole is two legs, and each leg is a quarter wavelength — the total length is λ/2, and each leg measures half of that total, λ/4.

So the whole cutting job is: pick a frequency, find λ/2, cut that much wire, split it in the middle. The only subtlety is which λ you use — and that is the end effect.

The end effect: why 468, not 492

In free space, a half wavelength at f MHz is a clean piece of arithmetic: light travels about 984 feet in a microsecond, so a full wavelength is 984/f feet and a half wavelength is 492/f feet. If dipoles were made of mathematics, you would cut to that. They are made of wire, and two things conspire to make a wire resonate shorter:

  • End capacitance. The open ends of the wire are not clean stops. The electric field fringes out past the physical end, so electrically the wire behaves as if it were slightly longer than the tape measure says.
  • Velocity factor. The wave travelling along a real wire in a real environment moves slightly slower than light in free space, so a given frequency's wavelength on the wire is slightly shorter.

Both effects pull the same way, and together they are worth about 5%. Generations of antenna builders folded them into one working constant:

L(ft) = 468 / f(MHz) The practical cutting formula for a wire half-wave dipole. Compare the free-space 492/f. The ratio is the end-effect factor K = 468/492 = 0.951220 — the wire resonates about 4.88% short. Cut to the free-space length instead and the antenna resonates low: it is already "too long" at the frequency you wanted.

Worked example — a 20 m-band dipole at 14.175 MHz

The bench's own core, run on this frequency:

  • Total resonant length: 468 / 14.175 = 33.015873 ft (≈ 33.0 ft, or 10.063 m).
  • Each leg: half the total = 16.507937 ft (≈ 16.5 ft, or 5.032 m) — a quarter wavelength on the wire.
  • The free-space half-wave would be 492 / 14.175 = 34.708995 ft. The end effect trims 1.693 ft off the total — about 0.85 ft per end. That is wire you would wrongly add if you cut by the physics-textbook number instead of the antenna-builder's number.

For scale across the presets on the bench: at 7.15 MHz (40 m) the same formula gives 65.454545 ft of wire — a serious span between two trees — and at 146 MHz (2 m) it gives 3.205479 ft, about 38.47 inches, an antenna you can hold in one hand. Same formula, same 5%, thirty-fold difference in lumber.

The pattern: a figure-eight that is really a doughnut

A dipole does not radiate equally in all directions. Off the ends of the wire it radiates nothing at all — think about the current fit: looking down the wire's own axis, the two legs' contributions cancel. Broadside — straight off the side of the wire — everything adds, and the radiation is strongest. In the plane that contains the wire, the normalised far-field pattern of a half-wave dipole is:

F(θ) = cos( (π/2)·cos θ ) / sin θ θ is measured from the wire's axis. F(90°) = 1 exactly (broadside). At θ → 0° and θ → 180° both numerator and denominator go to zero and the limit is F = 0: the end nulls are real zeros, not just small values — the bench's core takes that limit path explicitly rather than dividing by a near-zero sine. Sampled values from the shipped core: F(15°) = 0.206701, F(30°) = 0.417794, F(45°) = 0.627933, F(60°) = 0.816497, F(75°) = 0.950891.

Plot those numbers in the wire's plane and you get the classic figure-eight: two fat lobes broadside, pinched to nothing along the wire. But the wire's plane is only one cut. Swing that figure-eight around the wire's axis — the pattern is the same in every plane that contains the wire — and the two lobes sweep into a single ring: a doughnut, a torus, with the wire through its hole. A dipole hears and speaks all around its broadside ring and is deaf only in the two directions its own ends point. That is why the orientation of a dipole matters and its rotation around its own axis does not.

Γ, SWR, and why 1.46:1 is a good match

Whatever the antenna presents at the feedpoint, the feedline has its own characteristic impedance Z₀, and the meeting between them is scored by the same reflection coefficient as every other wave-on-a-line meeting:

Γ = (Z − Z₀) / (Z + Z₀)   ·   SWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|) Z is the feedpoint impedance — complex when the antenna is off resonance, and Γ is complex with it; SWR uses only its magnitude. |Γ|² is the fraction of your power that comes back down the line. An open or a short gives |Γ| = 1 and SWR → ∞: everything comes back. The bench returns that as Infinity, stated, not as a NaN or a quietly capped number. The returning wave itself is not an abstraction — it is the same wave you can fire and watch on the sibling bench, Transmission Line.

Now the part that surprises people the first time: a thin half-wave dipole in free space, at resonance, presents 73 Ω of pure resistance — not 50 Ω. That number is a consequence of the geometry, computed from the same current distribution that gives the pattern. Put that perfect, resonant, textbook dipole on ordinary 50 Ω coax and the arithmetic is unforgiving:

  • Γ = (73 − 50)/(73 + 50) = 23/123 = 0.186992.
  • SWR = 73/50 = 1.46 exactly — the bench's core returns 1.460000000000.
  • Power reflected: |Γ|² ≈ 3.5%. Power delivered: about 96.5%.

That 1.46:1 is not a defect to be fixed; it is the dipole's natural match to 50 Ω line, and generations of hams have run exactly it. (Feed the same dipole with 75 Ω line and the core gives SWR = 1.0274 — the dipole is nearly a perfect citizen there, which is one reason 75 Ω line keeps showing up in antenna work.) Chasing 1.00:1 with a tuner does not put more signal in the air in any proportion to the effort; it mostly makes the transmitter happy. Know the number, know why it is what it is, and 1.46 stops looking like a failure.

Detuning is a different story, and the bench makes you pay for it honestly. Move 5% off the frequency the wire was cut for and the model's reactance is no longer zero: at 0.95·fr, X = −74.9211 Ω (capacitive — below resonance the wire is electrically "too short," and a short wire looks like a capacitor); at 1.05·fr, X = +71.2619 Ω (inductive — too long, and a long wire looks like an inductor). On 50 Ω line the SWR climbs from 1.4600 at resonance to 3.3876 at −5% and 3.2263 at +5%. That asymmetry is real in the model — the (f/fr − fr/f) curve is steeper below resonance than above — and the sign flip of X across resonance is exactly what an antenna analyser shows you when you sweep a real dipole.

The model box — what the feedpoint numbers are, and are not

The model. Near resonance the bench models the dipole as a series RLC circuit: Z(f) = R + jX with R = 73 Ω and X(f) = Q·R·(f/fr − fr/f), Q = 10, and fr taken from the wire's length through the same 468/L relation used to cut it. At f = fr the bracket is exactly zero, so X = 0 and Z = 73 + 0j — the proof table holds it to exactly that.

Its band. This is a lumped near-resonance teaching model, trustworthy in roughly ±10% around resonance. Outside that band a real dipole's impedance wanders in ways a single series RLC cannot follow, and the bench's feed-frequency slider is deliberately fenced to ±20% with the model named on the panel — the far ends of that slider show the trend, not measurements. (Read: the trend is honest, the third decimal is not.)

What is missing — named. This is NOT an NEC simulation. A real dipole's impedance depends on its height above ground (ground reflections move the feedpoint resistance substantially — a low dipole can read well under 73 Ω), on wire diameter (thicker elements are broader-banded and resonate slightly shorter), and on its surroundings (nearby conductors, wet trees, the roof it hangs over). None of that physics is in this model. The 73 Ω figure is the free-space, thin-wire, textbook value — the right reference point, and not a promise about any particular backyard.

The pattern is the exact one. Unlike the impedance, the pattern formula F(θ) = cos((π/2)·cos θ)/sin θ is not a lumped approximation — it is the standard far-field result for a half-wave dipole with sinusoidal current, and the proof table checks it against independently computed values to ±1e-4. Its own bend: it assumes that sinusoidal current distribution and a wire in free space; ground will tilt and fill a real antenna's nulls.

The length constants are the standard ones. 468 and 492 are the antenna literature's working numbers, and the proofs hold the core to them exactly — what is proven is that the page computes the stated formulas without drift, not that 468 is a law of nature. For very thick or very thin elements the true constant moves a little; that is the same end-effect physics, one level down.
