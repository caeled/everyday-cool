What you are looking at

The half-wave dipole is the antenna almost every other antenna is measured against: two quarter-wave legs of wire, fed in the middle. This bench does the three jobs a dipole builder actually does — cut the wire to length, picture where the signal goes, and check what the feedline sees when the match is not perfect.

Panel A — Resonance & the wire

Set a frequency (slider, number box, or a ham-band preset). The bench computes the resonant length of a real wire dipole, L(ft) = 468 / f(MHz), and the free-space half-wave, 492 / f(MHz), and draws both to scale. The total length L is split into two legs; each leg is a quarter wavelength, so each leg measures L/2. The 468 number is smaller than 492 because of the end effect: the open ends of the wire add a little capacitance, and the wave travels slightly slower in a real wire than in free space (velocity factor), so a physical wire resonates about 5% short — the ratio 468/492 ≈ 0.9512 is the classic K ≈ 0.95 fudge factor every ham learns.

Panel B — Radiation pattern

The far-field pattern of a half-wave dipole, in the plane that contains the wire, is F(θ) = cos((π/2)·cos θ) / sin θ, with θ measured from the wire's axis. It is 1 broadside (θ = 90°, straight off the side of the wire) and exactly 0 off the ends (θ = 0° and 180°) — the classic figure-eight. Move the probe angle and read F and its level in dB (20·log₁₀ F). In three dimensions the pattern is not a figure-eight but a doughnut — a torus wrapped around the wire's axis; this plot is the cut through the plane of the wire.

Panel C — SWR & the feedline

Pick a feedline impedance Z₀ (50 or 75 Ω) and either feed the dipole — possibly at a frequency off the one the wire was cut for — or connect a plain resistive load. The bench computes the reflection coefficient Γ = (Z − Z₀)/(Z + Z₀) (complex when Z is complex) and the standing wave ratio SWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). An open or a short gives |Γ| = 1 and SWR → ∞: all the power comes back.

The feedpoint model, stated plainly

Near resonance the dipole is modelled as a series RLC circuit: Z(f) = R + jX with R = 73 Ω (a thin half-wave dipole in free space at resonance) and X(f) = Q·R·(f/fr − fr/f) with Q = 10, where fr is the wire's resonant frequency from its length via the same 468/L relation. Below resonance X is negative (capacitive); above, positive (inductive). This is a lumped near-resonance teaching model, valid in roughly ±10% around resonance. It is NOT an NEC simulation: a real dipole's impedance depends on its height above ground, the wire diameter, and its surroundings, none of which are in this model.

The sibling bench

The reflected wave Γ describes is not an abstraction — it is a wave that physically runs back down the line toward you. You can fire one and watch it come back on the Transmission Line bench in Everyday Electronics: the wave that comes back is the same wave you can fire there.

The Antenna Bench — cut it, aim it, match it

Half a wavelength of wire, fed in the middle, is the reference antenna of radio. Cut it a little short on purpose (the end effect demands it), know that it hears broadside and is deaf off its ends, and accept that on 50 Ω coax its honest match is 1.46:1 — not 1:1, and perfectly normal.
