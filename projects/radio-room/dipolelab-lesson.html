DipoleLab — antennas, polarization & the road to a callsign

Builder: Google Antigravity (the collection's science division), delivered 25 November 2026 as a single self-contained page — ten chapters, six labs, a 26-term glossary, zero external references. Auditor: Muse, independently, driving the shipped file headlessly and recomputing its claims from the antenna literature. Steven Powell commissioned it partly as a study bench: he plans to sit his own amateur license exam, the way his father Henry (WA6ZMV, a ham since 1956) would have wanted the homework done. The banner at the bench's top — a λ/2 dipole at 14 MHz over a lamplit shack, log book open at "59 • 73" — was Steven's pick, approved as made.

Verdict: the computational core is exact everywhere it was measured. Wavelength and wire-length math, polarization loss, Malus's law, SWR from complex Γ, the decibel link studio, and the license roadmap all reproduce independent hand computation. Nine bends were found — one drawn pattern, one gain estimate, one partial chart, and a tail of copy and labels — and Steven sanctioned fixing all of them before the bench shipped. Eleven edits later, this is the version on the shelf.

What measured exact

Wire math. At all eight band-selector positions the bench's numbers match 300/f, 468/f and 234/f to the printed digit (1.90 MHz → 246.32 ft total; 14.20 → 32.96 ft; 446 → 1.05 ft), and Chapter 2's printed table checks row by row. The end-effect explanation — why the practical 468 constant beats the free-space 492 — is correct, and the dipole's ~73 Ω feedpoint is the right figure.

Patterns and height. The horizontal dipole's elevation pattern is the ground-reflection factor |sin(2πh·sin el)| — the full correct pattern in the broadside elevation plane (pixel reconstruction at h = 0.5λ matches the theoretical shape, zenith null included). The takeoff readout is the honest asin(0.25/h): 0.25λ → 90° (NVIS, straight up), 0.5λ → 30°, 1.0λ → 14°.

Beams. Phased-array steering is exact (Δφ = −90° at d = 0.5λ steers to 30.0°; end-fire −180° → 90.0°), phased gain follows 2.15 + 10·log₁₀N, and Chapter 4's element lore (reflector ~5% longer and inductive, directors ~5% shorter and capacitive) is right.

Polarization. The mismatch bench computes a true Jones-vector inner product: linear tilt gives 100/75.0/50.0/25.0/0.0% at 0/30/45/60/90° — cos²θ exactly. RHCP into RHCP passes 100%, RHCP into LHCP 0.0%, and RHCP into a linear antenna gives 50.0% (−3.0 dB) at tilt 0° and at 37° — the tilt-independence that makes circular polarization worth its trouble, verified, not just asserted.

Malus and the paradox. Crossed polarizers pass 0.0%; the 45° middle filter passes exactly I₀/8 of unpolarized light; the twelve-filter Zeno stack passes [cos²(90°/13)]¹³ = 82.7% of the polarized beam.

SWR. Complex Γ is genuinely implemented: the default 73 + j42 Ω load reads SWR 2.17 with 13.6% reflected, the pure-resistance sweep is exact (1.50 → 4.0% reflected; 3.00 → 25.0%), and the text keeps the honest distinction that a tuner at the rig does not fix loss in the coax.

Decibels — and a bench that out-audited its auditor. The link studio's defaults reproduce hand computation exactly (50 W = +47.0 dBm; free-space loss over 45 km at 146.5 MHz = 108.8 dB; ERP honors the EIRP − 2.15 dB dipole convention). The S-meter honours both IARU standards — S9 = −73 dBm on HF and S9 = −93 dBm at 50 MHz and up — and the same signal reads S7.9 at 50.0 MHz against S4.6 at 49.5 MHz, both correct. The audit brief had only specified the HF convention; the bench knew the second one on its own.

The roadmap. Chapter 9's license path is current: Technician and General at 35 questions with 26 to pass, Extra at 50 with 37, privileges as written, and the same-session two-for-one exam note is standard practice. Nothing in it would mislead a candidate — Steven included.

What bent, and the sanctioned fixes

Steven's ruling, 25 November 2026: "Fix DipoleLab's bends too, then wire it." Eleven edits, each recorded as a hash step on the file's bytes: as delivered c1a74bdd → ac11b55c → 8c29a43b → fce23969 → b2fad1b8 → 6ff34c83 → 5f422162 → 88d964f3 → fe26df96 → b59d2af7 → ed902271 → aa476275, and the banner placement at wiring → e111b128 (138,445 bytes as shipped).

The vertical's pattern was invented (fixed). The λ/4 vertical's elevation drawing used an ad-hoc formula peaking near 28° with the horizon at half strength. A real monopole over ground peaks at the horizon. The drawing now computes the true factor cos((π/2)·sin el)/cos el: 1.00 at 0°, 0.82 at 30°, 0.42 at 60°, null at the zenith.

The Yagi estimate disagreed with its own picture (fixed). Telemetry priced gain off the raw slider (10 elements → a fantasy +21.1 dBi) while the renderers draw at most 7. The estimate now clamps at 7 elements and steps a realistic 1.5 dB per element — it tops out at 11.15 dBi, and it is still labeled an estimate.

The Smith chart was half a chart (completed). It drew only constant-resistance circles. Constant-reactance arcs (x = ±0.5, ±1, ±2), clipped to the unit disc, now complete it — the Γ point and SWR circle were always exact; now the grid under them is a real Smith chart.

Labels and copy (fixed). The Yagi lab's "FDTD / Live Maxwell" tags now say what the renderer actually does — phasor superposition. Chapter 8's "trillionth of a watt (−120 dBm)" was out by a thousand: −120 dBm is a quadrillionth of a watt (1 fW). The Zeno preset's "81%" now reads ≈83% of the polarized beam, matching the bench's own arithmetic. Chapter 3's takeoff range now includes the 30° its own telemetry computes at exactly 0.50λ. The drooping-radials claim is "about 50 Ω — a fine practical match," not an exact one. And the cross-pol figures are labeled: −30 dB is the ideal floor the bench draws, ~−20 dB is what real antennas achieve.

What still bends, printed openly

The vertical pattern is the ideal ground plane pattern; real soil, real radials and a real backyard will soften it — the bench's own chapters say ground quality matters. The Yagi gain figure remains a heuristic estimate, labeled as such. And the banner Steven approved carries one known nit: its wave-ribbon caption says "vertical oscillation" beneath a horizontal wire, whose field is horizontally polarized. He saw it, chose the image anyway, and the record here owns it rather than quietly letting it pass.
