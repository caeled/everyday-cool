# Collection origin

Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 17 November 2026 at Steven Powell's request. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that day he proposed "an online radio page for everyday cool? from servies like pandora and iheart to radio.com and brodcastify", decided it should be a NEW room in Everyday Cool, and named it himself: "The Radio Room". He approved the banner concept in his own words ("that sounds perfect") and added the build's key requirement: "If we can a retro interface for the radio" — the in-page player must look like an old radio. Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction ("Lets give it a try"); this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room follows the Games Room shape (a verified shelf of legitimate places, one thing that plays right on the page, a short workshop underneath) and the Find It pattern (Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the page and inside the desktop app, reciprocal links — here with Tonight's Sky and A Library, which links back). The updated Everyday Cool desktop app ships as app v1.0.11 through the app repo's existing build workflow.

The playable presets are SomaFM channels played from SomaFM's published public stream addresses (ice1.somafm.com), verified live on 17 November 2026. The big music services (Pandora, iHeart, Spotify, Audacy, etc.) are linked, never embedded — they require their own accounts and licences. Radio.com is noted on the page as Audacy's former name, since Steven listed it as radio.com. Scanner copy states plainly that feeds are public broadcasts and can be delayed. The WebSDR copy ties that listening porch to Tonight's Sky's radio astronomy.

The rules live in `radio.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Banner: `radio-room-banner.jpg`, AI-generated watercolor to Steven's approved concept — a robot in headphones, hand on the dial of a big old wooden radio, tuning between glowing station markers — generated 17 November 2026 as part of adding this room, in the style and dimensions (2736×912) of the collection's other banners.

The Antenna Bench, 23 November 2026: `antenna-bench.html` and `antenna-bench-lesson.html`, built by Muse at Steven Powell's direction to the Electronics Wave standard — one fenced pure core driving the bench and a 22-row `runProofs()` harness, all rows passing and independently re-run at integration. The bench cuts a half-wave dipole to a chosen frequency (resonance exact at 468/f feet — 33.015873 ft at 14.175 MHz — against free space's 492/f, ratio 0.951220, the end effect explained), plots the radiation pattern F(θ) = cos((π/2)·cos θ)/sin θ (broadside and both nulls exact inside 10⁻¹²; samples against independent references inside 10⁻⁴ — F(30°) = 0.417794, the audit correcting a slightly-off value in the commissioning brief), and computes Γ and SWR for dipole or resistive loads on 50 Ω or 75 Ω line (75 Ω on 50 Ω: Γ = 0.2, SWR = 1.500000000000; dipole at resonance on 50 Ω: SWR = 1.46 exactly; detuning ±5% costs SWR ≈ 3.39 / 3.23, reactance capacitive below resonance and inductive above). The feedpoint impedance is a stated series-RLC teaching model (R = 73 Ω, Q = 10, near-resonance band), fenced in the Help text, on the panel, and in the lesson as not an NEC simulation, with height, wire diameter, and surroundings named as the missing physics; the open-circuit SWR is printed as its 1 TΩ limit rather than a faked infinity. `index.html` features the bench in a new section directly under the hero (with a header nav link), ahead of the radio itself. `README.md` describes the bench. The bench cross-links Everyday Electronics' Transmission Line bench; its lesson is shelved at Nerd Heaven and at Java Young Robot Builders (back door 74), and Dad's Arcade carries its card (90 games, 90 lessons). Changed files: `antenna-bench.html`, `antenna-bench-lesson.html`, `index.html`, `README.md`, `COLLECTION.md`.

Skip, 23 November 2026: `skip.html` and `skip-lesson.html` — Steven Powell's own skywave game, written by him in Python (pygame) in his Bordeom workshop and ported to the web by Muse at his direction ("it needs to be added both to the arcade and our radio stuff as a webpage"). The port carries the Python original's physics and Morse cores over line for line into one fenced JS core: the ionosphere constants (E at 110 km, foE 3.5 day / 0.7 night; F2 at 230 km day / 280 night, foF2 9.0 / 4.2; D-layer absorption base 12 day / 2 night scaling as (4/f)² per hop), mirror-hop geometry (hop = 2h/tan(el)), the secant-law MUF (fo·√(1 + (d/2h)²)), the S-meter (S9 at 100 W, 6 dB per S-unit, −2.5 per extra hop, distance fade past 1500 km), the bracket (±(30 + 0.02·d) km), scoring (points = distance/√watts), the twelve Indiana-backyard stations, and the ITU Morse table at 20 WPM (dit exactly 60 ms). The page's `runProofs()` harness ports the Python original's own self-test (`skip.py --selftest`, run at port time: ALL PASSED, 33 checks) — 31 rows, all passing against the shipped core, independently re-run from the extracted core in Node at integration, matching the Python verdicts number for number (hop(30°, 250 km) = 866.03 km; MUF(fo = 9, el = 30°) = 18.000000000000 MHz; 20 m day F hop 986.5 km; 100 W single hop S8.67; PARIS 43 dits). pygame rendering became canvas (sky gradient, layer bands brightening with ionization, ghost of the last shot with landing flags, animated ray flight along build_path's curve, red night mode as a multiply pass); the winsound sidetone became WebAudio (700 Hz sine, the same schedule() timing); on-screen controls join the original keyboard map. Two port-level differences, both recorded in the lesson's model box: stations deal from the browser's random source rather than numpy's seeded generator (same cities, same ±5% jitter, no repeatable seed), and the Python's fixed-distance practice target is not carried over. For the collection's own records: the original's hidden tribute is carried over undocumented, exactly as shipped — the E key transmits "DAD" instead of the target's code and marks the first landing with a heart; it is for Steven's father Henry Powell (WA6ZMV, ham since 1956). Morse, 24 November 2026: `morse.html` and `morse-lesson.html` — Steven Powell's own Morse code sender, written by him in Python (`morse.py`) in his Bordeom workshop and ported to the web by Muse at his direction. The port carries the Python original's physics and timing over line for line into one fenced JS core: the ITU code table (all 52 characters, no two sharing a code), the schedule, the PARIS timing (a dit is 1200/WPM ms — exactly 60 ms at 20 WPM; PARIS plus its word gap is exactly 50 dits, the definition of WPM), the Farnsworth spacing formula (spacing unit = (60/overall − 37.2/letters)/19 seconds; PARIS at 20 WPM letters and 10 WPM overall takes exactly 6.000 s), the 21-unit loop pause, the repeat rule and the 1800 s export cap, and the WAV recipe (44100 Hz, mono, 16-bit; 5 ms raised-cosine fades, 0.2 s lead and 0.4 s tail silence, amplitude 0.6). The page's `runProofs()` harness ports the Python original's own self-test (`morse.py --selftest`, run at port time: all passed, 19 checks) — 19 rows, all passing against the shipped core, matching the Python verdicts number for number (PARIS at 20 WPM renders to 3.180 s holding exactly 14 tones; three Farnsworth loops 23.18 s and 42 tones; "at least 30 s" of SOS is 30.4 s in 11 whole repeats). The Python's `winsound` beeps became a WebAudio oscillator and its tkinter window became the page, both recorded in the lesson's model box; the Python source's dedication is carried in the page header — "For Dad (a ham since 1956)", for Henry Powell (WA6ZMV). The same Morse table and timing power the sidetone in Skip, as `skip.py`'s header states outright. `index.html` features Morse in a new section directly beside Skip, above the Antenna Bench (with a header nav link), and also gains a brief cross-note in the Antenna Bench section pointing to the Resonance Lab in Everyday Physics (`../everyday-physics/resonance-lab.html`) — resonance is how a radio tunes, and that lab's curve is the same curve as an electrical circuit. `README.md` describes the sender. `test.cjs` is unchanged: it asserts presets and Scanner Tuner counties, not exhibit or page counts, so no count was updated.
