# Collection origin

Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 17 November 2026 at Steven Powell's request. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that day he proposed "an online radio page for everyday cool? from servies like pandora and iheart to radio.com and brodcastify", decided it should be a NEW room in Everyday Cool, and named it himself: "The Radio Room". He approved the banner concept in his own words ("that sounds perfect") and added the build's key requirement: "If we can a retro interface for the radio" — the in-page player must look like an old radio. Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction ("Lets give it a try"); this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room follows the Games Room shape (a verified shelf of legitimate places, one thing that plays right on the page, a short workshop underneath) and the Find It pattern (Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the page and inside the desktop app, reciprocal links — here with Tonight's Sky and A Library, which links back). The updated Everyday Cool desktop app ships as app v1.0.11 through the app repo's existing build workflow.

The playable presets are SomaFM channels played from SomaFM's published public stream addresses (ice1.somafm.com), verified live on 17 November 2026. The big music services (Pandora, iHeart, Spotify, Audacy, etc.) are linked, never embedded — they require their own accounts and licences. Radio.com is noted on the page as Audacy's former name, since Steven listed it as radio.com. Scanner copy states plainly that feeds are public broadcasts and can be delayed. The WebSDR copy ties that listening porch to Tonight's Sky's radio astronomy.

The rules live in `radio.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Banner: `radio-room-banner.jpg`, AI-generated watercolor to Steven's approved concept — a robot in headphones, hand on the dial of a big old wooden radio, tuning between glowing station markers — generated 17 November 2026 as part of adding this room, in the style and dimensions (2736×912) of the collection's other banners.

Local change (18 November 2026, later, at Steven Powell's request — "a section on HAM radio [and] the citizen band", then "maybe even if we can find a stream to an international channel running", then "maybe a list of cool stations to try to listen"): a seventh shelf group, "Ham radio & CB — the two-way side", added between "Real radios you can tune" and "Old-time & archive". It carries a plain-language explainer (ham radio is licensed by exam with your own call sign and worldwide reach via repeaters and satellites; CB is unlicensed, 40 channels, a few miles, the truckers' band; both are two-way — you transmit as well as listen — unlike everything else in the room; Everyday Electronics' crystal radio is named as the two-way world's receive-only ancestor), six links verified live that day (ARRL's What is Ham Radio, QRZ.com, RepeaterBook, RadioReference, and the FCC's Amateur Radio Service and Citizens Band Radio Service pages — QRZ and the FCC pages bot-check bare scripts while serving browsers, verified by reading the pages themselves, the OpenMHz treatment), and a "Cool stations to try" list of eight entries with frequencies and how-to-hear notes: the University of Twente WebSDR as the named live international receiver (HTTP 200 that day; the list's live listen, absorbing Steven's stream request), UVB-76 "The Buzzer" (4625 kHz), WWV/WWVH (5/10/15 MHz), CHU Canada (3330/7850/14670 kHz), WBCQ "The Planet" (7.490 MHz, plus its own site's stream), the ISS amateur downlink (145.800 MHz, later linked to Satellite Watch for pass times, with the VHF-receiver and short-pass notes), the BBC World Service on shortwave (no frequency printed, deliberately — seasonal; read the Twente dial labels), and a far-flung KiwiSDR pick (Iceland or New Zealand) via the public map. No specific far-flung KiwiSDR endpoint is named: the public list is JavaScript-driven and no single Iceland/NZ receiver address could be stood behind that day, so the entry gives the map and the instruction instead — recorded here so the gap is a decision, not an oversight.
