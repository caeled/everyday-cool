Real radios you can tune

Not recordings and not stations — actual radios, connected to the internet, that strangers kindly share. Turning the dial here retunes a real receiver, right now, in someone else's town — the listening side of amateur radio, and the same radio sky that Tonight's Sky listens to with its radio telescope.

  • WebSDR

    A directory of shared shortwave receivers all over the world. Pick one, drag across the band, and listen to what's really in the air there.

  • KiwiSDR Public Radios

    Hundreds more shared receivers, with a map to choose by place — broadcasts, hams, and mysterious signals alike.

Ham radio & CB — the two-way side

Everything else in this room is listening. These are the radios where you talk back. Ham radio (amateur radio) is licensed: you pass an exam, earn your own call sign, and whole bands open up — talk across town through a hilltop repeater that relays your voice, across the state on a good antenna, or around the world when the sky cooperates, by voice, Morse code, or digital modes, even bouncing signals off amateur satellites. The shared receivers in the group above are the listening porch of that world, and this shelf is its transmitting side. CB radio is the unlicensed neighbour: no exam, no call sign, 40 channels around 27 MHz and a few miles of range on a good day — the truckers' band, with channel 19 for the highway and channel 9 kept for emergencies. Both are two-way: you transmit as well as receive, which makes them the step beyond everything else in this room — and the crystal set built in Everyday Electronics is their receive-only ancestor, a radio that hears this world and can never answer it.

  • ARRL — What is Ham Radio

    The national association's own front door: what ham radio is, everything you can do with it, and how the license exams work.

  • QRZ.com

    The call-sign directory of the ham world — look up who a call sign belongs to — with solar and band-condition data that tells you which frequencies are travelling far today.

  • RepeaterBook

    The repeater directory: find the hilltop repeaters near you — the relays that pick up a small radio's signal and carry it across a county or a state.

  • RadioReference

    The giant frequency database: who transmits on what, nearly everywhere — ham repeaters, public safety, aviation, and the frequencies behind the scanner group above.

  • FCC — Amateur Radio Service

    The official page: the rules, the license classes, and how examinations and grants actually work, from the people who issue the call signs.

  • FCC — Citizens Band Radio Service

    The FCC's own CB page: 40 channels between 26.965 and 27.405 MHz, no individual license required, and the rules exactly as written.

Cool stations to try — most of these need no radio of your own: a shared receiver (the Twente one first) tunes them all. Lower shortwave bands travel farthest after dark; that is propagation, not scheduling, and it is the honest rule behind several of these.

  • University of Twente WebSDR — start here

    A famous university receiver in the Netherlands that opens already listening, and the set of ears for most of this list. Checked live on 18 November 2026; shared receivers come and go, but this one has run for years.

  • UVB-76, "The Buzzer" — 4625 kHz

    A Russian military channel that has buzzed, day and night, since the 1970s; every so often the buzz stops and a voice reads out names and numbers, and nobody outside knows the whole story. On the Twente receiver, tune to 4625 kHz, USB mode. Best after dark.

  • WWV & WWVH — 5, 10, and 15 MHz

    America's clock, on the air: a tick every second and a voice announcing the exact time each minute — WWV from Colorado, WWVH from Hawaii. If you can hear WWV, that band is open at least that far; hams use it as a propagation weather report.

  • CHU Canada — 3330, 7850, and 14670 kHz

    Canada's time signal: ticks and announcements in English and French, from Ottawa. The 3330 kHz channel is an after-dark catch; 7850 reaches further in the daytime.

  • WBCQ, "The Planet" — 7.490 MHz

    A free-form American shortwave station broadcasting from Monticello, Maine — music, talk, and gloriously odd programming, strongest on 7.490 MHz in the evenings. It also streams from its own site, linked here.

  • The space station's downlink — 145.800 MHz

    The International Space Station carries an amateur voice repeater, and its downlink is 145.800 MHz FM — sometimes astronauts, often school contacts and other hams. It needs a receiver that covers VHF (most shared HF receivers, including the Twente one above, stop at 30 MHz; a scanner or a VHF-capable SDR works). A pass lasts about ten minutes, so start with Satellite Watch to see when the station next flies over you, then listen.

  • BBC World Service, on shortwave

    Still broadcasting on shortwave to parts of Africa and Asia. Its frequencies change with the season and the hour, so there is no single honest number to print: on the Twente receiver, the station labels along the dial show you where it is right now.

  • A receiver on the other side of the planet

    Open the KiwiSDR map and pick a receiver in Iceland or New Zealand, then tune the same bands you just tried from Twente. Same sky, different static: their local stations are your impossible catches, and yours are theirs.

Build one — the crystal radio bench

In Everyday Electronics, hands on

Everything on this page is a radio somebody else built. The crystal set is the one radio in either room you can build yourself — a coil, a diode, an earpiece, a wire for an antenna, and no battery at all, because the station pays for the power. Head to the crystal radio bench in Everyday Electronics and tune it: the schematic is drawn, the parts agree with the build plan, and the dial works — turn the variable capacitor, watch the stations rise out of the static, and try it again with the one-transistor amplifier switched in. Then come back here and hear the real sky it imitates.

03 / HOW RADIO WORKS HERE

Three kinds of listening.

A station stream

One broadcast, everyone hears the same moment

A real station — or an internet-only one like SomaFM — sends one stream, and every listener hears the same song at the same time, like radio always worked. The wooden set above is a station stream: press Lush anywhere on Earth and you hear what Lush is playing now. There is nothing to skip, because there is nothing stored — it's live.

Live means live for everyone

A personalized service

Pandora & friends

Pandora doesn't broadcast at all. It keeps a giant library of recordings and, the moment you ask, builds a private playlist shaped by what you told it you like. No two listeners hear the same "station," and nothing is live — which is exactly why it can't play inside this page: the library and the learning live behind its own account and licences.

A playlist pretending to be a station — cleverly

A scanner feed

Real radios, shared by volunteers

Police, fire, EMS, pilots, and train crews talk on radios that, in the United States, anyone may generally listen to when the traffic is unencrypted and not cellular — encrypted and cellular transmissions are off-limits, and some states restrict scanners in vehicles. A volunteer near you keeps a scanner (or a small software-defined radio) running and sends what it hears to a shelf like Broadcastify. You're hearing the real thing, second-hand, through a stranger's kindness.

Real broadcasts, relayed

Why scanner feeds lag

The delay is normal — sometimes on purpose

Expect a feed to run from half a minute to several minutes behind the real moment: the audio has to travel from the volunteer's radio to the service and back out to you, and some services add a deliberate delay before publishing public-safety audio. So a scanner is for understanding what's happening in your town — never treat it as an emergency service, and never act on it as if it were happening this second.

Interesting, not instant

What is WebSDR?

A real radio, shared

SDR means software-defined radio: a receiver whose dials and filters are software. A WebSDR or KiwiSDR is one of those receivers, in a real place with a real antenna, connected to the internet so visitors can tune it. When you drag the frequency, you are retuning that radio and hearing what its antenna hears — static, faraway stations, hams chatting, numbers stations being strange. Hams have shared their world this way for years; these sites open the door to everyone.

04 / HELP

Help — how The Radio Room works.

The retro player

The wooden set at the top

Press any cream station button. The needle slides to that station's spot on the dial, the dial window shows its name, and the stream starts. Stop halts it. The player is a normal web audio player wearing a wooden cabinet — everything it does, your browser's own controls could do; the cabinet just makes it feel like a radio.

Buttons play, Stop stops

Presets & the tuning knob

Six stations, one dial

The six presets are SomaFM channels, laid out left to right along a make-believe FM dial. The round Tuning knob steps to the next station (and wraps around at the end); the Volume slider is the set's volume knob, flattened so it's easy to grab. Your pick isn't saved anywhere — close the page and the set forgets, like a real radio with the power cut.

Nothing is remembered

The shelf & linking out

Why Pandora opens a new tab

The big services — Pandora, iHeart, Spotify, Audacy and the rest — only play inside their own sites and apps, under their own accounts and music licences. No page is allowed to embed them, so the shelf links out honestly instead of pretending. In the desktop app, those links open in your default browser, like every link that leaves the workshop.

Their licences, their living room

Scanners, briefly

Public, delayed, volunteer-run

Scanner feeds carry public broadcasts — dispatch and service radio. In the United States, listening to unencrypted public-safety radio is generally legal; cellular and encrypted traffic are off-limits, and some states restrict scanners in vehicles. Feeds can run minutes behind real time (see the workshop above), they depend on a volunteer keeping a radio on, and a silent feed usually just means a quiet hour in that town. Listen for understanding, not for emergencies.

Public broadcasts, kindly relayed

The scanner tuner

The small wooden box under the radio

The tuner finds your county and opens its page on Broadcastify, where the feeds play in Broadcastify's own player — scanner audio never plays on this page, and no Broadcastify API is used. Use my location asks your device where it is, then asks OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service to name the county; the ZIP or town box sends just what you typed to the same service. Neither answer is kept anywhere. The tuner's checked county links cover Ohio — all 88 counties, read from Broadcastify's own Ohio page on 17 November 2026. It uses those county links only when the lookup says the place is in Ohio; an Ohio place it does not have a county link for opens Broadcastify's Ohio page, and anywhere else opens Broadcastify's front door, rather than guessing a link.

A finder, not a player

Works offline?

Honest answer: no

Radio is the internet's live wire: every stream, station, and feed needs a connection, because the whole point is hearing what's happening now. The Help and workshop text are part of the page, so once it has loaded they stay readable if the connection drops — the music, sadly, stops with it.

About privacy, plainly: this page keeps nothing. Pressing a preset asks SomaFM's public stream server (somafm.com) for that channel's audio — it sees your internet address, the way any website does — and nothing else is sent anywhere by the player. There is no account, no sign-in, and no record of what you listened to; the volume and station you picked live only while the page is open. The scanner tuner under the radio is the one place this page sends anything else: Use my location sends your device's location, and the ZIP or town box sends what you typed, to OpenStreetMap's Nominatim service (nominatim.openstreetmap.org) so it can name your county — the answer is not kept, and the Broadcastify page it leads to is an ordinary link out, like the shelf's. Every shelf link opens its own service in a new tab; what happens there is that service under its own terms, and most of them do use accounts and recommendations of their own. Scanner feeds carry public broadcasts and can be delayed; they are not emergency services.
