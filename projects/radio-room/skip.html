What you are looking at

Skip is a skywave game: pick a frequency, a takeoff angle and a power, transmit, and watch your signal climb to the E or F2 layer of the ionosphere and come back down — hopefully on top of the target station. Land inside the green bracket with a readable signal (S3 or better) and the station replies 73 — ham for "best regards" — in Morse, with a sidetone you can hear. Low bands and high angles fall close; high bands and low angles go far; at night the high bands die and the low bands come alive.

The sky it plays under

The ionosphere is drawn as two mirror layers at their virtual heights: the E layer at 110 km and the F2 layer at 230 km by day, rising to 280 km at night. A ray reflects off a layer when its frequency is at or under that layer's maximum usable frequency for the hop, by the secant law: MUF = fo · √(1 + (d / 2h)²), which for a mirror hop is the same as fo / sin(elevation). The E layer gets first refusal; what punches through it meets F2; what punches through that is lost to space. Critical frequencies: E is 3.5 MHz by day and 0.7 at night (it nearly vanishes); F2 is 9.0 by day and 4.2 at night. A daytime D layer under both absorbs signal — worst at low frequencies, scaling as (4/f)² — which is why 80 m by day is rough and by night is a highway.

The dials

Frequency 3–30 MHz (arrow keys step 0.1, Shift steps 1.0; the band button jumps to the next ham band: 80, 40, 30, 20, 17, 15, 12, 10 m). Angle 3–80° — high angles fall close (steep enough and you are working NVIS, nearly straight up and back down); low angles reach far but need the frequency to hold up. Power 5 / 25 / 100 / 400 W: power buys S-meter, never distance — but the score pays distance / √watts, so a 5 W contact is worth over four times a 400 W one at the same range, and the logbook tracks your best miles-per-watt. Every hop past the first costs ground-reflection loss, and the signal can land up to four hops out — watch the landing flags.

The keys

↑/↓ angle · ←/→ frequency · B band · +/− power · T transmit · N new target · D day / night / auto · M sound · R red night mode · Space pause · Backspace reset score · F fullscreen · S save a picture · H help lines. Every dial also has an on-screen control, so the game plays by mouse or touch alone. In auto the sun follows your own clock, the way the original does: day from 07:00 to 19:00 local.

The model, stated plainly

Simplified but honest: a flat Earth, mirror hops off fixed virtual heights (so every hop of a multi-hop path is the same length), the secant-law MUF, a per-hop absorption tax, and an S-meter that is a game scale, not a calibrated receiver. There is no sporadic E, no greyline, no solar weather, and the stations sit at their great-circle distances from a central-Indiana backyard, jittered ±5% each time one is dealt. A real ray trace bends gradually through a layered sky over a round Earth; this sky is the teaching version of that sky. The Morse, though, is the real thing: the standard ITU table, timed at 20 WPM with a dit of exactly 60 ms. The full write-up — formulas, worked numbers, and the proofs — is on the lesson page.

The original

Skip was written by Steven Powell in Python (with pygame) in his Bordeom workshop. This page is a port: the physics and Morse cores were carried over line for line, and the proof table below runs the original's own self-test checks against the shipped code. The Python original's two practice options are here as well — a fixed-distance target and a seeded, repeatable station order, both set in the rig panel.

On a touch screen: the rig is buttons and sliders — dealing, calling and logging all work by tap. Only the fine tuning uses the arrow keys.
