Morse — the lesson

Type something, press Go, and hear it in Morse code. Morse is a sender and a trainer in one: it keys your text at the standard timing, at the letter speed you choose, with Farnsworth spacing when you want the overall pace slower than the letters, a Hide mode for learning by ear, and a Save WAV button that renders the audio to a file. This page is the other half of the exhibit — where "words per minute" actually comes from, why the gaps stretch the way they do, what the WAV writer is doing, and the proofs — readable while you listen, from the 📖 Lesson button on the sender.

What it does

The sender takes any text — characters with no Morse code are quietly skipped, as the original skips them — and plays it as a sidetone. You set the letter speed (5–40 WPM), the overall speed if you want Farnsworth spacing, the tone (300–1200 Hz), and the repeats: the message again and again with a pause of three word gaps (21 spacing units) between passes, or 0 for "until Stop". Save WAV renders the same sound to a downloadable file instead of playing it. The dots and dashes are shown as it plays — unless you ask it not to (below).

PARIS: the ruler of Morse speed

"Words per minute" sounds like it should depend on the words. It doesn't — it is defined by one standard word, chosen because its letter mix is typical: PARIS (.--. .- .-. .. ...). The timing rules are fixed:

dit = 1200 / WPM ms  ·  dah = 3 dits  ·  gap in a letter = 1  ·  between letters = 3  ·  between words = 7 Count PARIS on those rules: its tones take 22 units, the gaps inside its letters 9, the gaps between its letters 12 — 43 units of word. Add the 7-unit word gap that follows it and the word plus its breathing room is exactly 50 units. So at W words per minute, 50 dits take 60/W seconds, one dit takes 60/(50W) seconds — 1200/WPM milliseconds. At 20 WPM a dit is exactly 60.000000 ms and PARIS is 43 × 0.06 = 2.580 s of sound. Every speed on the sender is that one definition, unwound.

That is why the proofs can be so blunt: the standard is arithmetic, not opinion, and the original's self-test checks the arithmetic outright — PARIS plus its gap is 50, or the program is wrong.

Farnsworth: slow overall, fast letters

The beginner's trap is learning at slow letter speed: at 5 WPM the dits and dahs arrive so far apart that you learn to count them, and counting stops working the moment the code speeds up. The Farnsworth method refuses to slow the letters down. Each letter is keyed at full letter speed, so you learn its shape — its rhythm as a single sound — and only the spaces between letters and words are stretched, until the overall rate is the gentle one.

spacing unit = (60 / overall − 37.2 / letters) / 19  seconds 3 units go between letters, 7 between words. The constants are PARIS again: of its 50 units, 31 can never stretch — the tones and the gaps inside letters — and at letter speed they are worth 31 × 1.2/letters = 37.2/letters seconds. The other 19 units (four 3-unit letter gaps plus one 7-unit word gap) are the stretchable part, and they must fill whatever is left of the word's 60/overall seconds. Worked at letters 20, overall 10: the unit is (6 − 1.86)/19 = 0.2179 s, and PARIS with its word gap takes exactly 6.000 s — 60/10, the definition, recovered. The proofs hold the page to that to a nanosecond.

Notice what the formula protects: a spacing unit can grow without limit as the overall speed falls, but a tone never changes length. The letter .-. sounds identical at overall 18 WPM and overall 5 — only the silence around it breathes.

Hide: listen first

The sender's one deliberate piece of pedagogy is the Hide checkbox, carried from the Python window unchanged. Tick it and Go plays the message without showing its dots and dashes: the readout says only "(hidden: listen first, then press Reveal)". You copy by ear — write down what you heard — and then press Reveal to check yourself against the true code. Reading code off a screen teaches reading; copying code off the air teaches Morse. The hide is the whole lesson plan, in one checkbox.

The WAV recipe

Save WAV builds the audio sample by sample with the Python render_wav recipe, unchanged:

  • Format: 44100 Hz, mono, 16-bit — CD sample rate, one channel, the container the Python wave module writes.
  • No clicks: every tone fades in and out over 5 ms on a raised cosine, env = 0.5 − 0.5·cos(πi / ramp) — a tone that starts at full amplitude in one sample is a click, and a file full of clicks is unlistenable.
  • Silence at the ends: 0.2 s before the first tone, 0.4 s after the last, so players and editors don't clip the edges.
  • Level: the sine is packed at 0.6 of full scale — loud, with headroom.
  • Repeats: the file repeats the message the number of times Repeats says — or, with Export at least set, in whole copies until it is at least that long (never a message cut in half). Either way an export is capped at 1800 s: a WAV runs about 5 MB a minute, and the original caps it at half an hour.

Measured against the shipped render code: PARIS at 20 WPM renders to exactly 3.180 s — 43 dits of sound plus the 0.6 s of end silence — holding exactly 14 tones, with a silent lead-in and a peak of 0.600. Three Farnsworth loops of it: 23.18 s, 42 tones. "At least 30 s" of SOS: 30.4 s in 11 whole repeats, and one fewer repeat would be short — the same numbers the Python self-test prints against the original.

The port, stated plainly

What carried over exactly. The ITU code table (all 52 characters, no two sharing a code), the schedule, the PARIS timing, the Farnsworth formula, the 21-unit loop pause, the repeat rule and the 1800 s cap, and the WAV recipe — fades, lead and tail, level, format — are the Python's, function by function. The proofs below are the original's self-test checks, and their measured values match the Python run number for number (3.180 s, 14 tones, 23.18 s, 42 tones, 30.4 s in 11 repeats, a 1798 s worst case under the cap).

The sound source changed. On Windows the Python beeps with winsound — there is no winsound in a browser, so the same timeline is played by a WebAudio oscillator instead. The timing it is fed is the original's; only the beeper differs. One honest consequence: winsound.Beep plays each element out, so the Python's Stop is heard at the next element boundary (its player checks the stop flag between elements, waking every 20 ms); the web Stop turns the gain down at once, mid-element. The web version stops faster than the original — a difference, recorded rather than hidden.

The window became a page. The tkinter window's controls are all here with their defaults (letter speed 12, tone 600 Hz, repeats 1) and its Hide checkbox; the command-line form (python morse.py "CQ CQ" --wpm 15) has no web counterpart, because a web page has no command line. The Python renders its WAV with numpy into a file; the port builds the same samples in memory and hands them over as a download — the bytes are the recipe's, the filing cabinet differs.

Its sibling in this room

The same table and timing power the sidetone in Skip, this room's skywave game — skip.py's header says so outright: "The Morse table and timing are the same as morse.py." In Skip the code arrives the way it does on the air — a station code keyed live, a contact answered with 73 — which makes the game, accidentally or not, this page's listening exam: train here until the shapes are familiar, then go and copy a call sign off the sky.
