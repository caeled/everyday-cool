The Radio Room · The back door to Skip

Skip — the lesson

Skip is a game about the oldest long-distance trick in radio: aim a shortwave signal at the sky at the right angle, on the right frequency, and the ionosphere throws it back to Earth hundreds or thousands of kilometres away — where, if you judged the skip distance right, a station is waiting. Pick the frequency, the takeoff angle and the power; transmit; land inside the target's green bracket with an S3-or-better signal, and it answers "73" in Morse. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the sky it models, the math under the dials, what the model leaves out, and the proofs — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

Skip was written by Steven Powell in Python (with pygame), in his Bordeom workshop — a ham's game, built by a ham's son: his father Henry spent five decades as an amateur operator (WA6ZMV, licensed 1956), working exactly this sky. The web port is by Muse: the physics and Morse cores were carried over from the Python line for line, and the proof table below is the Python original's own self-test, run against the shipped page code.

How a contact works

The game deals you a target station — a real US city at its great-circle distance from a central-Indiana backyard, jittered ±5% — and draws a green bracket on the ground around it, ±(30 + 0.02 × distance) km wide: ±50 km around a 1000 km target. Your ray climbs, mirrors off a layer, and comes down at a distance set by your angle and whichever layer caught it. It may land up to four hops out, each hop the same length, each landing marked with a flag. The landing nearest the target is judged:

  • Contact — inside the bracket at S3 or better. The station replies 73, and you score distance / √power points (so QRP pays: 1000 km at 100 W is 100 points; at 5 W it is 447).
  • Faint — inside the bracket at S1–S3: heard, not readable. More power, or fewer hops.
  • Weak — inside the bracket under S1: below the noise.
  • Miss — the hops fell elsewhere; the game reports long or short and how far, which is the game's real teaching loop: adjust angle or band and shoot again.
  • Escape — the frequency punched through both layers. Gone to space.

The sky: two mirrors and a tax collector

The model's ionosphere has three actors. The E layer, at a virtual height of 110 km, critical frequency 3.5 MHz by day and 0.7 MHz at night — after sunset it nearly vanishes. The F2 layer, 230 km by day and rising to 280 km at night, critical frequency 9.0 MHz by day and 4.2 at night. And underneath both, the D layer, which reflects nothing and absorbs: a per-hop tax with a daytime base of 12 (2 at night) that grows as (4/f)² — quadrupling every time you halve the frequency. That one scaling is most of why 80 m is a struggle at noon and a superhighway at midnight.

A ray's hop is pure mirror geometry:

hop = 2h / tan(elevation) h is the layer's virtual height. Check it against the game: at 30° off a 250 km mirror, hop = 866.03 km. At the F2 day height of 230 km, the game's own core gives 5° → 5,258 km, 15° → 1,717 km, 25° → 986 km, 45° → 460 km, 75° → 123 km — and at 80°, 81 km: nearly straight up and back down, the NVIS shot hams use to cover their own region.

Whether a layer catches the ray at all is decided by the secant law. A layer reflects a vertically-fired signal up to its critical frequency fo; tilt the ray and it can hold proportionally more:

MUF = fo · √(1 + (d / 2h)²) = fo / sin(elevation)  for a mirror hop The highest frequency that still reflects at that geometry. The two forms are the same statement — the proof table holds the core to it: with fo = 9 MHz at 30°, the computed MUF is 18.000000000000 MHz. Flatter rays tolerate more: at fo = 9, MUF is 51.83 MHz at 10° elevation and only 11.75 MHz at 50°. The E layer gets first refusal of every ray; what punches through it meets F2; what punches through that is lost.

Day and night: the bands change shifts

Everything the game teaches about propagation falls out of those constants, and it is the same behaviour the real bands show:

  • 20 m by day (14.2 MHz at 25°): reflects off F2, one hop of 986.5 km, and at 100 W it lands at S8.67 — the daytime DX band, working as advertised.
  • 20 m at night: F2's critical frequency has fallen to 4.2 MHz, the MUF at 25° is under the band, and the same shot escapes. The band is closed — not weaker, closed.
  • 40 m at night (7.2 MHz at 25°): reflects off the risen F2 (280 km), one hop of 1,200.9 km. The night band owns the night.
  • 80 m by day (3.0 MHz at 45°): caught by the E layer for a short 220.0 km hop — and anything lower or longer pays the D-layer tax at (4/3)² and worse. Four hops of it at 5 W reads S−19.28 in the core: not faint, gone.
  • 10 m nearly straight up (28.5 MHz at 60°) punches through everything, day or night — some shots are just donations to the cosmos.

In auto the game's sun follows your own clock — day from 07:00 to 19:00 local — so the sky over the game is, roughly, the sky over you. The D key overrides it, for practising the other shift.

The S-meter

S = 9 + (10 · log₁₀(P / 100)) / 6 − 2.5 · (hops − 1) − 0.35 · hops · absorb − max(0, d − 1500) / 1000 absorb = day/night base × (4/f)². Read it term by term: 100 W is the S9 reference and power counts 6 dB per S-unit, so 400 W buys exactly +1 S and 5 W costs about −2.2 S; every hop past the first costs 2.5 S of ground-reflection loss; the D-layer tax is charged per hop; and paths past 1500 km fade a little with distance. Worked by the core: 100 W on 20 m, one hop, 986 km by day — absorb = 12 × (4/14.2)² = 0.95, so S = 9 − 0.35 × 0.95 = S8.67. Three hops of the same path: S1.54 — the far landing is a whisper of the near one.

Power, notice, never moves a landing — it only decides whether a landing counts. Distance is bought with angle and band choice alone. That is the game's whole strategy in one sentence, and it is true of the real sky too.

The Morse

The sidetone is the standard ITU table, timed the standard way: a dit is 1200/WPM milliseconds — exactly 60.000000000 ms at the game's 20 WPM — a dah is 3 dits, letters are spaced 3, words 7. The word PARIS is the metrologist's ruler of Morse speed (50 dits with its trailing word gap; 43 without it), and the core's on-air time for PARIS at 20 WPM is 2.58 s, exactly 43 × 0.06. On this page the tone is a 700 Hz WebAudio sine; in the Python original it was winsound, on Windows only. Your call is sent as CQ plus the station's code; a contact is answered with the code and 73, keyed live.

The model box — what the sky is, and is not

Flat Earth, mirror hops. The game mirrors rays off fixed virtual heights over a flat Earth, so every hop of a multi-hop path is exactly the same length (the proofs hold it to ratios of exactly 1, 2, 3). A real ray bends gradually through a refracting layer over a curved Earth; hop lengths vary, and there is a maximum one-hop range the flat model never meets. The mirror picture is the standard first teaching model of skywave — the geometry a ham sketches on paper — not a ray trace.

Virtual heights. The ray is drawn kissing the layer's virtual height (the two-hop path peaks at 229.4 of 230 km — the drawing's sine curve, sampled). A real signal slows and turns below the true reflection height; the virtual height is the mirror that would have to exist to produce the same path, which is the honest fiction the whole model runs on.

Two sun states, fixed constants. Day and night are single snapshots — foF2 of 9.0 and 4.2 MHz — with the change thrown like a switch. Real critical frequencies slide with the hour, the season, and the 11-year solar cycle; there is no greyline (the dawn/dusk enhancement real operators chase), no sporadic E, no solar flares or geomagnetic storms in this sky.

The S-meter is a game scale. Its shape is honest — power in 6 dB S-units, per-hop losses, absorption growing as (4/f)², distance fade — but no receiver was calibrated to produce it, and it is uncapped in both directions (a hopeless path can read S−19). Treat the number as the game's consistent physics, not as a prediction about a real rig.

The stations are real distances, dealt approximately. The twelve cities sit at their approximate great-circle distances from central Indiana and are jittered ±5% each deal. The stations deal from a seeded generator, as the Python's do: the seed sits in the rig — set it, and the station order repeats from the top — and ?seed= in the address does the same. The proof table runs the original's own station checks (same seed, same order; another seed differs; every dealt station within 5% of its city). One residue, named: the generator is the web's mulberry32, not numpy's PCG, so a seed number repeats an order within each program but does not deal the Python's order. The Python's fixed-distance practice target is here too — set a distance in the rig (or ?dist=) and every target is PRC at that distance until you clear it.

What is exact. The Morse table and timing, the hop geometry, the secant law, the bracket, and the scoring are not approximations of the original — they are the original, carried over function by function, and the proof table below is the original's self-test checking them.
