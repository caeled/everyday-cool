Decisions Everywhere

Most people are taught how to read, write, and calculate. Very few people are taught how to decide. This workshop makes decision-making visible.

Not politics. Not ideology. Not persuasion. Eight rooms of observation, reasoning, evidence, probability, troubleshooting, and consequences — a detective notebook for everyday choices. The goal is never to tell you what to think. It is to practice how to think.

Activities work offline. This page sends nothing, stores nothing, and needs no account. Every example is fictional.

The cycle, and what it keeps teaching: good decisions come from good information · good information comes from careful observation · confidence is not the same as certainty · every decision has tradeoffs · mistakes are information.

ROOM 1

Guessing and Knowing

See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

How do we know what we know?

Sort each statement into its true category: an Observation (sensed right now), a Fact (anyone can check it), a Prediction (about the future), an Opinion (a judgment or feeling), or a Guess (a maybe, offered without evidence).

Discovery. Many arguments happen because people confuse observations, conclusions, and predictions with one another. Sorting them is the first decision skill: know what kind of claim you are holding before you decide what to do with it.

ROOM 2

The Broken Flashlight

See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

How do we find the real cause of a problem?

A flashlight will not light. Five things could be wrong. First, take a guess — then run the tests and watch the clues remove possibilities, one by one.

Before any tests: what is your first guess?

The suspects

      Discovery. Troubleshooting is decision-making. Good troubleshooters do not guess randomly — they eliminate possibilities. Each clue that removes a suspect is worth more than a lucky first guess.

      ROOM 3

      The Tradeoff Store

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      Can we always get everything we want?

      Welcome to the Tradeoff Store, where every product offers three wishes and grants exactly two. Pick a pair for each product and meet what you actually bought — and what you gave up to get it.

      Discovery. Most decisions involve tradeoffs. Choosing one advantage often means giving up another — and naming the thing you gave up, out loud, is what turns a wish into a decision.

      ROOM 4

      The Coin That Lies

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      Are humans naturally good at probability?

      Predict first. Then run the experiment and compare what you expected with what actually happened. These benches use your browser’s random number generator — a simulation, honest about being one.

      The coin bench

      In the next 10 flips, what do you predict?

      The dice bench

      The card bench

      You will draw 10 cards from a shuffled deck. What do you predict?

      Discovery. Human intuition often misjudges probability. Random events cluster, streak, and clump — random often looks non-random. The cure is not a better gut feeling; it is more flips.

      ROOM 5

      The Missing Puzzle Piece

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      What information is missing?

      Exhibit A: “Sales soar at the little shop!”

      A shop shares this chart. Look at those bars climb! Before you decide anything, notice what the chart does not tell you.

      Three pieces of information are missing from this chart. Reveal them one at a time.

      Timescale: those four points are four days, not four years. A rise across four days is weather, not climate.

      Sample: the count comes from one small shop. One shop can have one busy stretch for a dozen local reasons — a road closure ending, a school trip passing by.

      Starting value: the axis starts at 985, not at zero. The “soaring” rise is 991 → 999 — under one percent — stretched tall by the missing floor.

      Same numbers. Same shop. The drama was never in the sales — it was in the missing floor.

      Discovery. The most important information is sometimes absent. A careful thinker learns to ask: What am I not being shown?

      ROOM 6

      The Time Machine

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      What happens later?

      Every decision is really two decisions: the one you feel tomorrow, and the one you live with next year. Choose a path in each story and ride the Time Machine to see both.

      Discovery. Many consequences are delayed. Immediate results and long-term results are often different — and neither column is automatically “the smart one.” The skill is seeing both before you choose.

      ROOM 7

      The Confidence Meter

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      How certain should we be?

      For each claim, slide the meter to your honest confidence: Very uncertain (0–24) · Some evidence (25–49) · Strong evidence (50–74) · Extremely likely (75–100). Then check the evidence and see whether your meter was calibrated — set where the evidence actually points.

      Discovery. Certainty, confidence, and evidence are three different things. Confidence is a feeling; evidence is a pile of checkable facts. The meter trains the feeling to follow the pile.

      ROOM 8

      The Big Decision Lab

      See→Question→Test→Decide→Review

      One budget. Three wishes. Your call.

      A school has money for exactly one improvement: faster computers, better internet, or more playground equipment. There is no answer key — real decisions rarely have one. Make the decision, write your reasoning, and then run it through the full cycle. Nothing you type leaves this page.

      The budget allows exactly one. Which do you choose?

      Discovery. Good decisions require information, priorities, reasoning, and review. Two people can choose differently and both decide well — the quality lives in the process, not only in the outcome.

      FIELD MISSIONS

      Take the notebook outside

      The workshop ends; the deciding does not. Four missions, no screen required — bring back one observation from each.

      Mission 1 · The advertisements

      Observe three advertisements. For each one, ask Room 5’s question: what information is missing? Prices without sizes, claims without timescales, “everyone is buying” without a count.

      Mission 2 · The forecast

      Watch or read a weather forecast. Sort its statements the Room 1 way: which parts are observations, and which parts are predictions? Notice how the forecast itself labels its confidence.

      Mission 3 · The broken object

      Find a broken object at home. Before touching a single tool, write down three possible causes — then test the way Room 2 taught you: cheapest, safest test first, one elimination at a time.

      Mission 4 · The interview

      Ask a trusted adult: “What was a decision that looked smart at first but turned out differently later?” Listen for the Time Machine: what did tomorrow look like, and what did next year look like?

      EDUCATOR GUIDE

      Running Decisions Everywhere

      Visitors should finish with these skills: observation · verification · troubleshooting · risk assessment · tradeoff analysis · probability awareness · reflection. No prior technical knowledge is required. The workshop suits upper elementary, middle school, and high school learners, and adult learners.

      How to run it

      Each room takes roughly ten to twenty minutes and stands alone; Rooms 1 and 2 make a gentle first session, and Room 8 works as a capstone discussion. Everything runs from this folder, offline, with no accounts and nothing collected — learners can share one machine and argue productively. The full design brief is preserved in DESIGN.md in this folder.

      Discussion prompts

      After Room 4: “When did the result surprise you — and was the surprise in the coins, or in your expectation?” After Room 5: “Where have you seen a chart like Exhibit A in the wild?” After Room 8: “Whose voices are missing from this decision, and what would they ask to SEE?” Keep the emphasis on exploration and practical reasoning; there are no lectures to give, only experiments to notice.
