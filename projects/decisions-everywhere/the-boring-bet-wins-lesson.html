The Boring Bet Wins — the lesson

The game puts three doors in front of you and prints the price on each one. This page is the working behind the prices: what expected value is, how to compute it by hand for all three doors, why a single round tells you almost nothing, and why the long run — and only the long run — is where the price collects.

▶ Play The Boring Bet Wins

THE ONE NUMBER ON EACH DOOR

Expected value, by hand

Expected value (EV) is the average profit or loss per play if you played the same bet over and over, forever. The recipe is always the same: multiply each outcome by its probability, then add. (Expected value, on Wikipedia ↗)

All three doors in the game stake 10 coins. Here is every door, worked in full:

The Boring Bet — stake 10

Nine times in ten it pays +4; one time in ten you lose the 10.

EV = (9/10)(+4) + (1/10)(−10) = 3.6 − 1.0 = +2.6 coins per playAs an exact fraction: 36/10 − 10/10 = 26/10 = 13/5. Positive — the door pays you, a little, on average.

The Fair Coin — stake 10

Heads +10, tails −10, a half each way.

EV = (1/2)(+10) + (1/2)(−10) = 5 − 5 = 0 coins per playPerfectly fair, perfectly still. A thousand plays expect to gain exactly nothing.

The Flashy Game — stake 10

One chance in twenty pays +140; the other nineteen chances lose the 10.

EV = (1/20)(+140) + (19/20)(−10) = 7 − 9.5 = −2.5 coins per playAs an exact fraction: 140/20 − 190/20 = −50/20 = −5/2. The headline prize is real — and it is already counted, in full, in that 7. The price is the other 9.5.

Notice what the flashy arithmetic is telling you: the jackpot is not hidden from the calculation, and the calculation is not close. A bet can offer a life-changing prize and a negative price at the same time, because the prize is rare and the stake is collected every single round.

That last column is the whole lesson in one glance. Nothing about the doors changes between round 1 and round 1,000 — only the count does, and the count is where a per-play price turns into a fortune or a hole.

WHY ONE ROUND PROVES NOTHING

EV is not variance

Expected value is an average, and averages hide the weather. The spread of outcomes around the average is called variance, and the three doors have wildly different weather:

  • The Boring Bet barely swings: +4 or −10, never more. Its result after 30 rounds is already fairly predictable — the challenge in the game is built on that.
  • The Fair Coin swings ±10 a round with no drift. After 30 rounds it can easily be 40 coins up or down — pure weather, no climate.
  • The Flashy Game is almost all weather: a long flat slide of −10s, interrupted — maybe — by a +140. In 20 rounds the chance of hitting the jackpot at least once is about 64% — yet only about 26% of those runs finish ahead, because one jackpot does not repay twenty rounds of slide; it takes a second one. All on a door priced to take 50 coins from you in expectation over those same rounds.

So: a good bet can lose, and a bad bet can win — once. Judging a bet by one outcome is like judging a climate by one afternoon. The game's Free Rounds ledger shows both bankrolls — yours, and the one the prices predicted — precisely so you can watch the weather wobble around the climate, round by round.

WHERE THE PRICE COLLECTS

The law of large numbers

The law of large numbers ↗ says that as you repeat a bet, your average result per play closes in on its expected value. Not because the doors remember, and not because a loss makes a win "due" — but because the lucky and unlucky streaks, which dominate a short run, become a smaller and smaller fraction of a long one.

That is what the game's Long Run shows: 100 runs of 1,000 rounds each, dealt by machine. The boring line's finishes gather in a hill around 500 + 1,000 × 2.6 = 3,100. The fair line's finishes scatter around its start of 500. The flashy line's finishes gather at the bottom — nearly every run ruined. Same doors as round 1. The only new ingredient is repetition.

There is a family resemblance here to the rest of this collection, and it is worth naming: the boring bet gets there by halves — small corrections, compounded — the same way the Map Room's navigator corrects a course a few degrees at a time instead of betting everything on one perfect bearing. A small edge, applied relentlessly, beats a brilliant guess, applied once.

THE BARRIER AT ZERO

Gambler's ruin: the schedule, not the luck

Every game above has a rule that matters more than any door: if your bankroll drops below the 10-coin stake, you stop. Zero is an absorbing barrier — every path that reaches it stays there. There is no borrowing, no next round, no recovery.

Against a negative-EV door, that barrier turns a bad price into a certainty given enough rounds. Your bankroll drifts downward on average; the barrier waits at the bottom; a walk that drifts down and cannot pass zero ends at zero — not might, ends, with probability approaching one as the rounds accumulate. That is the gambler's ruin ↗, and the Long Run's flashy histogram is its portrait: the ruin is not a run of bad luck. It is the schedule the price was always keeping.

Even the fair door is not safe from the barrier: with no drift at all, a bankroll wanders, and a wandering walk eventually visits low ground — in the game's Long Run, a noticeable share of fair runs die by drift alone. A fair game gives you no reason to win and every opportunity to wander into the wall. Only the positive edge walks, on average, away from the barrier.

A POINTER, NOT A LESSON

Sizing the bet: the Kelly criterion

This game fixes every stake at 10 coins so the prices stay comparable. Real bettors with a real edge face a second question the game deliberately does not ask: how much of the bankroll to stake each time. The famous answer is the Kelly criterion ↗ — a formula for the stake fraction that grows a bankroll fastest in the long run without courting ruin. It is named here as a pointer only: if the fixed-stake simplification itches, that is the door to open next.

HOW IT'S BUILT

The machinery

  • Exact fractions, not floats. Each door is a table of outcomes with probabilities stored as numerator/denominator pairs. The core adds them in rational arithmetic and reduces by greatest common divisor, so the boring bet's EV is the fraction 13/5 and the flashy game's is −5/2 — the same numbers this page computed by hand, with no rounding anywhere.
  • A seeded generator. Random draws come from a mulberry32 generator with a fixed seed per game, so any run — including every run in the Long Run — replays exactly, draw for draw. That is what lets a proof suite (and a sceptical reader) check the game's claims instead of trusting them.
  • The Long Run is just the core, repeated. One function plays a whole run of one door against one seed, stopping at ruin; the Long Run calls it 100 times with 100 seeds and reports the distribution — median, mean, best, worst, and the ruin count.
  • The proof. Before this game shipped, a headless test played it by the thousand: the boring line met the challenge in about 93 of 100 seeded attempts; the flashy line failed it in about 87 of 100; and in the Long Run the flashy line ruined in essentially every run (99–100 of 100, batch to batch) while the fair line's median finish sat exactly on its start. The doors behave the way their cards say they do.

Simplifications, named.

Fixed stakes. Every door stakes 10 coins every round. Real edges are usually bet in proportion to a bankroll (see the Kelly pointer above), which changes both the growth and the ruin arithmetic.

Independent rounds. Each draw here is fresh; no door gets hotter, colder, or "due." Real systems sometimes carry memory — form, fatigue, markets moving — and this game models none of it.

No skill element. There is nothing to get better at except choosing the door. Real "boring" edges — index investing is the standard example — carry their own genuine risks: markets fall, sometimes for years, and a positive long-run average is not a promise about any particular decade. The game's shopkeeper never has a bad year; the real world's do.

Coins, not rent money. The bankrolls here are tokens. The arithmetic of negative EV is identical with real money, which is the point of practising it with tokens.
