Time.

You cannot see time, touch it, or keep it in a jar — so humans measure it by watching things that move evenly: the sun across the sky, a pendulum on its swing, the Earth around its star. This room takes the agreements apart. Why a sundial and a clock disagree, and by how much. Why February sometimes grows a day. Why it is already tomorrow in Tokyo. And how many heartbeats fit in a life, counted honestly.

01 / THE SUNDIAL BENCH

The oldest clock is a shadow.

A horizontal sundial is a flat plate, a triangular blade called a gnomon, and a fan of hour lines. The lines are not evenly spaced — they are squeezed by latitude, through one formula: tan(angle) = sin(latitude) × tan(hour angle). Pick a place, slide the hour, and watch the shadow fall exactly along its line. That shadow landing true, at every hour, is the whole invention.

Honest limit: a sundial reads local solar time — the sun's own schedule where you stand — not clock time. Two separate effects pull them apart, and the bench above adds both up for your place and date: the equation of time (Earth's elliptical orbit and tilted axis make the sun run up to about 16 minutes fast or slow across the year), and longitude (solar time is local, so every degree you sit from your time zone's standard meridian shifts the sun 4 minutes against the clock). In Defiance the longitude shift alone is worth 37 minutes.

The shadow itself gets a bench of its own next door: Everyday Light's shadow bench walks a stick's shadow around a compass through the day and across the seasons — this dial's geometry, read as light rather than as time.

02 / THE LEAP-YEAR MACHINE

A correction scheme, not a count.

The year is not a whole number of days — it is about 365.2422 of them — so every calendar is a patch over that awkward fraction. The Gregorian patch is three gates: divisible by 4, leap; except divisible by 100, not leap; except divisible by 400, leap after all. Feed the machine a year and watch the gates rule on it.

Why three gates?

THE CALENDAR, PATCHED TWICE

Julius Caesar's calendar used gate 1 alone — leap every four years, an average year of 365.25 days. That overshoots by about 11 minutes a year, which sounds like nothing until it has 1,500 years to accumulate: by 1582 the calendar had drifted ten days against the seasons. Pope Gregory's reform deleted the ten days and added gates 2 and 3, landing on 365.2425 — within about a day per 3,200 years of the truth. The gates are not pedantry; each one is a scar from a real drift.

365 + ¼ − 1⁄100 + 1⁄400 = 365.2425 days. The sky says 365.2422.

03 / TIME ZONES

One moment, seven wall clocks.

Time zones are the sundial's local truth, standardised: the Earth turns 15° an hour, so the world is sliced into bands roughly an hour apart, each keeping the solar time of one chosen meridian. This bench starts from a single moment in UTC — it never peeks at your clock — and reads that moment on seven walls around the planet.

    04 / THE SCALE LADDER

    From a heartbeat to the age of the Earth.

    Everyday Measure climbs sizes; this ladder climbs durations. Each rung is a real length of time with something human standing on it. The bottom rungs you can feel in your own chest; the top one holds every heartbeat that has ever happened.

    Heartbeats in a lifetime

    The week is ours

    Every other rung on the ladder is written in the sky: the day is one turn of the Earth, the year one trip around the Sun, the month roughly one Moon. The week is the exception — no sky motion takes seven days. It is a pure human agreement, thousands of years old, that outlasted every empire that used it. Calendars are astronomy; the week is culture — and it is the rung nobody can imagine living without.

    A pregnancy is about 40 of them; a school year about 40 more. The week is the ladder's human-sized handle.

    05 / THE PENDULUM BENCH

    The swing that only cares about length.

    Galileo's great discovery, and the engine of three centuries of clocks: a pendulum's period depends on its length and nothing else — not the weight of the bob, not (for small swings) how wide it swings. T = 2π√(L/g). Slide the length and the pendulum below swings at its true speed.

    Honest limit: the formula is the small-swing approximation — swing wide and the true period creeps a little longer (about 1% at a 23° swing). And real clock pendulums fight a subtler enemy: metal rods grow in summer heat, lengthening the period and losing time, which is why fine clocks used gridiron and mercury compensation, and later near-zero-expansion invar. The formula is exact about the physics it includes; it just doesn't include everything.

    Honest limit: the arithmetic is proleptic Gregorian — the modern rules run backwards as if they had always held. Before October 1582 the dates on the wall were Julian, and in Britain and its American colonies they stayed Julian until September 1752, when Wednesday the 2nd was followed by Thursday the 14th. Ask this bench about a date before your country's switch and it answers in the calendar we use now, not the one on the wall then.

    09 / HELP

    Help — how Everyday Time works.

    The manual for this workshop — what each part does, the simplifications it makes, and where its information comes from. This page and the full README work offline; the GitHub links need the internet.

    The sundial bench

    Draws a horizontal sundial for any latitude from the equator to 65°N: hour lines by tan(angle) = sin(latitude) × tan(hour angle), the gnomon, and the shadow for the chosen solar hour, which falls along its hour line by construction. Six preset places set the latitude slider and supply the longitude for the second half of the bench — the sundial-vs-clock comparison, which adds the equation of time (the standard approximation, good to well under a minute) to the longitude shift of 4 minutes per degree from the zone's standard meridian. Daylight saving is named as a further full-hour gap, not folded in. At latitude 0 the bench shows the honest failure: the hour lines collapse and a horizontal dial cannot be built.

    The leap-year machine

    Runs any year through the Gregorian gates in order — ÷4, ÷100, ÷400 — showing each gate's arithmetic and verdict, then the summary: the rule averages 365.2425 days against the tropical year's ≈365.2422, drifting about a day per 3,200 years. The companion card tells the two patches' history: Caesar's single gate drifting ten days by 1582, Gregory's reform deleting them and adding the century gates.

    The time zones bench

    Takes one UTC moment the visitor enters — a date and an hour — and reads it in seven cities at their standard offsets, with the weekday and the day shift (same day, a day ahead, a day behind) computed by integer day-counting. It deliberately does not use the visitor's clock, and it does not hide daylight saving: the confession list under the table names each city's daylight offset and its rough season, including Sydney's southern-hemisphere reversal.

    The scale ladder & heartbeats

    Eight durations from a second to the age of the Earth, each with its length in seconds and a human anchor; selecting a rung shows its multiples of the rungs below. Years on the ladder are mean Gregorian years (365.2425 days). The heartbeat bench multiplies a resting rate by the minutes in that many mean years — an order-of-magnitude portrait of a life (about three billion beats, and about three billion seconds, at ordinary rates), and it says plainly that no real heart holds one rate for a lifetime.

    The pendulum bench

    Period from length by T = 2π√(L/g) at g = 9.81 m/s², ticks per hour at one tick per half-period, and an SVG pendulum animated at the computed period — a one-metre swing really takes its two seconds on screen. The seconds pendulum (0.994 m) is called out when the slider reaches it. The small-swing approximation and the temperature problem (rods grow; fine clocks compensated) are stated on the bench, not buried.

    Days between dates

    Counts days between two dates with integer calendar arithmetic (a days-from-civil algorithm), reports the span in days and in weeks-plus-days, and names each date's weekday from the same count. The calendar is proleptic Gregorian: the Julian reality before 1582 — and before September 1752 in Britain and America — is stated on the bench as its limit.

    Honest limits

    Every bench carries its limits on its face: the sundial reads solar time, with the equation of time and the longitude shift itemised rather than averaged away; the leap rule's 3,200-year drift is printed beside its verdicts; the zones are standard time, with daylight saving confessed city by city; the heartbeat count is the size of a number, not a measurement of a person; the pendulum formula is the small-swing approximation, with heat named as the clockmaker's real enemy; and the date bench's calendar is proleptic, with the Julian centuries flagged. Where this room simplifies, it says what it simplified.

    Works offline?

    Yes. The benches and this Help run entirely from the files in this folder, including inside the desktop app — the zones bench takes its moment from your input, never from a network clock. Only the Links Out shelf needs the internet, because those pages live on other people's sites.
