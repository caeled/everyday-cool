How to Make a Workshop

Every workshop in this collection was made the same way — and the collection itself is the worked example. A question somebody actually had. A small bench that lets them ask, try, notice, and understand. An audit that doesn't take the builder's word for anything, with the receipts printed where anyone can read them. Honest wiring. And at the end, a human who decides. This page teaches that way of making — and leaves the door open at the end, because the next good question probably isn't ours.

Help ▾ — about this workshop

What this is. A method page, not a software tool: the five moves below are the actual pipeline every room in this collection went through, and the checklist is the real wiring checklist used to add them. The examples named are real and linked.

Honest limits. This is one collection's practice, described from inside it — it is not the only way to make a teaching page, and it is not a promise that following it makes a good one. Move 5 is the reason: a checklist can carry a workshop to the door, but a person still decides whether it teaches. The plumbing descriptions are summaries; each tool's own page is the authority on it.

Offline & privacy. This page is static and works offline. It stores nothing and sends nothing. The contribution links at the bottom go to GitHub, which needs internet and has its own terms.

The five moves

MOVE 01

Start with a question

Not a topic — a question, in the words of the person who has it. "Why does the horizon dip?" became Is the Earth Flat? "Can you steer a ship home on gun recoil alone?" became the Recoil Experiment inside the X-Ray Time Machine. If the question isn't real, the bench won't be either.

MOVE 02

Build the smallest bench

One page, one idea, controls a visitor can drive in the first minute. The rhythm is ask, try, notice, understand — in that order. Anything that doesn't serve the question is decoration, and decoration is where errors hide. Guest builders are welcome at this move: much of this collection was built by other hands — Grok, ChatGPT, Antigravity, Meta AI — and integrated exactly as delivered wherever possible.

MOVE 03

Audit it like you don't trust it

Because you shouldn't — not even your own build. The audit drives the shipped page's real controls, recomputes its numbers independently, and prints the results in a lesson beside the work: what holds, what bends, what failed. When an audit falsified a law printed on one of our own games, the fix ran the same night and the lesson tells the story. Receipts are the difference between "trust me" and "check me."

MOVE 04

Wire it honestly

A workshop isn't done when it works; it's done when it's findable and checkable: a catalog entry, counts generated from that one source, a provenance record for every file, a sitemap line, a changelog line, a documentation row. The checklist below is the whole move, verbatim.

MOVE 05

A human decides

Contributions are proposals. The curator reads, the audit runs, and a person says yes or no — and the no is a real answer, not a failure state. This is the move that keeps a collection a collection instead of a pile.

The plumbing

Three unglamorous tools are what turn a shelf of pages into a collection that can grow without falling apart. Each has its own home; these are the summaries.

AI-Delivery — files move

Builders don't share one desk, so files travel in labelled boxes — one inbox per builder — with a human as the router who copies, never moves. Nothing arrives silently and nothing is overwritten without a person carrying it. It's slow on purpose: the routing is the review.

Visit AI-Delivery on GitHub →

The Baton Box — state moves

A small relay server that knows no games and just carries the baton: one player sets up a position, the next inherits it. It's how pass-and-play games travel between people who are never online at the same time — and the model for how a workshop's state should behave: opaque, handed over whole, never silently rewritten.

Open the Baton Box →

Provenance & the gate — claims move

Every file in the collection carries a recorded hash, every room keeps a COLLECTION.md saying where it came from and what changed, and a checker — the gate — refuses the collection if a file changed without its note saying so. A claim about the collection can be re-verified by anyone, from the files alone.

The wiring checklist

This is the actual list a new workshop goes through here. Steal it whole — it is the workshop, in one box.

  1. The page builds and its lesson page states what it claims, what was measured, and what bends.
  2. The audit drove the shipped controls — not a copy, not a screenshot of one.
  3. A catalog entry exists, and every count on the homepage is generated from the catalog, never typed by hand.
  4. The hub card, the room's shelf, and the Full Index all point at it.
  5. A provenance record covers every file; the room's COLLECTION.md names what changed, in the open.
  6. The sitemap gained its lines; the changelog gained one dated line.
  7. The documentation directory lists it.
  8. The collection gate passes — and the live page is byte-compared against the audited file before anyone says "it's live."

Bring one

The contribution guide used to be the abstract version of this page. Here is the concrete one: an idea is enough. You don't have to build a workshop to start one — a good question, a clearer explanation, or a "someone should make the thing that teaches X" is a complete contribution, and it will be read by a person. If you do build, build in your own fork and propose it; the five moves above are the whole standard, and Move 3 means your work will be checked the same way ours is — which is a compliment, not a hurdle.

The standing rules are few: free forever, no ads or subscriptions, sources someone can follow, accessibility treated as a requirement rather than a finish. Everything else is negotiable in the open.
