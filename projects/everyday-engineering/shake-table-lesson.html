The Physics of Shake Table

The back door to the game: what the shaking is really doing, the maths underneath it, the game's own levels taken apart with their real numbers, and how the simulation is built.

The game in one paragraph

In Shake Table you stack a tower to a target height, spend a budget on bracing, base isolators, or a tuned mass damper — and then the ground starts to shake at one steady rhythm. Your tower survives if no storey ever slides sideways past its limit. The trap the game is built around is that the quake's strength is not the main danger: a weaker quake whose rhythm matches your tower's own natural wobble will tear it down, while a stronger quake at the wrong rhythm barely moves it. Everything below is that one idea, unfolded.

The physics

Every building has a natural wobble

Shove a building and let go, and it doesn't just stop — it swings back and forth at one preferred rate, its resonant frequency (also called its natural frequency), the way a guitar string prefers one note. Tall, flexible buildings wobble slowly — a skyscraper might take several seconds per swing. Short, stiff buildings wobble fast. The wobble is not a flaw; it's what mass on anything springy does. The only choices are what the rate is, and how quickly the swinging dies away.

An earthquake is a rhythm, not just a strength

We talk about quakes as if they were a single shove with a size. Under a building, what arrives is a back-and-forth motion with its own rhythm, lasting seconds. So the real question is never just "how hard does the ground push?" — it is "how hard, and at what rhythm, and for how long?" That is why the game shows you two numbers side by side before you shake: your tower's wobble and the quake's frequency. The comparison between them is the whole forecast.

Resonance: pushes that arrive on time

Push a child on a swing at random moments and little happens. Push at exactly the swing's own rhythm — each push arriving just as the swing starts moving your way — and the arcs grow and grow, from pushes no stronger than before. Resonance is that timing effect in a building. When the quake's rhythm matches the tower's wobble, every push from the ground lands while the tower is already swinging that way, so each cycle adds its energy to the last one's. The sway grows cycle over cycle until something stops it: the motion damping out, the quake ending — or a storey bending past what it can survive. Nothing about the individual pushes is extraordinary. The timing is the destructive part. (The classic write-up is at Wikipedia's Resonance article.)

Storey drift is what actually breaks buildings

A tower rarely fails because the whole thing moved — it fails because one floor slid sideways relative to the floor beneath it, further than its columns and walls can bend. That relative slide is called storey drift, and it is the number the game watches: each storey has a drift limit, the floors glow hotter as they approach it, and the storey that crosses it is the storey that gives way. This is also why the top of a resonating tower moves so much more than the bottom: the drifts of every storey stack up, floor over floor.

The three escapes

Real earthquake engineering has three families of answers, and the game gives you one tool from each:

  • Detune it. Change the tower's wobble so it no longer matches the quake. In the game that's bracing: cross-braces make a storey stiffer, a stiffer storey wobbles faster, and the rhythm match is broken. (Adding mass would slow the wobble instead — the same trick from the other side.)
  • Isolate it. Let the ground move without the building having to follow. A base isolator is a soft, flexible layer at the foundation — real ones are stacks of rubber and steel, or sliding bearings — that lets the ground thrash sideways while the building above sways gently at the isolator's own slow rhythm. The isolator itself travels a long way; that travel is the price, and it's paid where nothing breaks. See Wikipedia on base isolation.
  • Absorb it. Fight sway with sway. A tuned mass damper is a heavy weight mounted near the top, on springs tuned to the building's own wobble. When the tower starts swinging one way, the weight swings the other way a half-beat later, and its pushes cancel the tower's. Taipei 101's giant golden pendulum ball is the famous one. See tuned mass damper.

The math

One mass on one spring

Start with the simplest wobbling thing there is: a single mass hanging on a spring. Its resonant frequency is

f = (1 / 2π) · √(k / m)

Taken apart, symbol by symbol:

  • f — the frequency of the wobble, in hertz: complete back-and-forth swings per second.
  • k — the spring's stiffness: how much force it takes to stretch it a given amount. Bigger k, stiffer spring, and the fraction grows — a stiffer spring wobbles faster.
  • m — the mass. Bigger m, heavier load, and the fraction shrinks — a heavier mass wobbles slower. It is the tug-of-war between these two, stiffness pulling the rate up and mass dragging it down, that sets every wobble in this lesson.
  • √ — the square root is why the effects are gentler than you'd guess: doubling the stiffness does not double the frequency, it multiplies it by √2 ≈ 1.41. To double a wobble you must quadruple the stiffness (or quarter the mass).
  • 1 / 2π — bookkeeping between two ways of counting swings. The spring's own arithmetic naturally produces a rate in radians per second (ω = √(k/m)); one full swing is 2π radians, so dividing by 2π converts to swings per second.

Stacking storeys lowers the frequency

A tower is masses stacked on springs in a chain — each floor a mass, each storey the spring under it. Add storeys and two things happen: more total mass, and a longer, more flexible chain for the motion to travel through. Both push the wobble down, so taller towers wobble slower. The game's towers are uniform (every floor the same mass, every storey the same stiffness), and for that case the chain's first wobble has an exact closed-form answer. Here is what the game's own frequency calculator produces — these are the numbers the game itself displays:

Tower (plain, unbraced)Natural frequencyOne full wobble takes
3 floors1.4166 Hz0.71 s
5 floors0.9060 Hz1.10 s
8 floors0.5874 Hz1.70 s

Read the table the way the game wants you to: the 8-storey tower sways at barely two-fifths the rate of the 3-storey. A slow quake that a short building would shrug off can be an exact match for a tall one — which is precisely Level 3's trap.

Damping: the wobble's leak

Nothing wobbles forever. Friction, joints flexing, air, the materials themselves — all of it bleeds a little energy out of every swing. Engineers package that leak as the damping ratio ζ (zeta): the fraction of "critical" damping, the amount that would stop the wobble dead without a single overshoot. ζ = 1 is critical; real buildings live far below it, around ζ = 0.01–0.05 — they are lightly damped, which is exactly why resonance has time to build in them. The game uses ζ = 0.03, in the honest middle of the real range. Damping is also what the tuned mass damper really sells: not strength, but a much bigger leak, installed exactly at the wobble that matters.

Worked examples — the game's own numbers

Every number below comes from the game's proof runs: before the game shipped, a test drove its actual physics code and recorded what happened to each design. These are not illustrations of what might happen — they are what the shipped simulation did.

Level 2, "The Resonance Trap": a weaker quake wins

Level 1's quake shoves at amplitude 30. Level 2's quake is weaker — amplitude 12, less than half — but it shakes at 0.906 Hz, and a plain 5-storey tower wobbles at 0.9060 Hz. An exact match. Here is the plain tower's fate, three ways:

Same plain 5-storey towerPeak storey driftResult (limit 0.55)
Level 2 quake, 0.906 Hz, amplitude 122.002Fails — 3.6× over the limit
Same quake strength, moved off-frequency to 0.5 Hz0.289Survives comfortably

That pair of numbers is the resonance signature: change nothing but the rhythm — same tower, same push strength — and the worst drift changes by a factor of 6.92×. Strength didn't do that. Timing did.

And here are the level's two intended saves, as the proof runs recorded them:

SavePeak driftWhat happened
Brace all five storeys ($50 tower + $40 bracing = $90, the whole budget)0.207 Survives — bracing stiffened the tower, its wobble quickened off the quake's rhythm, detuned.

Level 2, walked through: how to think about it

  1. Read the two frequencies first. Before touching anything, compare the numbers on the stat line: tower wobble ≈ 0.906 Hz, quake 0.906 Hz. They match. That match — not the quake's size — is the problem to solve.
  2. Name what must change. You cannot change the quake. You cannot change the target height (5 floors) or the drift limit. The one thing that is yours is the tower's wobble: move it, decouple from it, or absorb it.
  3. Price the detune. Bracing a storey doubles its stiffness. Bracing all five lifts the tower's wobble clear of 0.906 Hz — but at $8 a storey it costs $40 on top of the $50 tower, exactly the $90 budget. It works (peak drift 0.207), with nothing left over.
  4. Price the isolation. The isolator costs $20 — half the bracing bill — and doesn't move the match so much as refuse it: the ground floor rides on bearings, the ground's rhythm spends itself in the isolator's travel, and the tower above sways at its own slow pace. Peak drift above the isolator: 0.065, the safest number on the level.
  5. Check the trap before you shake. The plain tower, built free and shaken as it stands, is the level's lesson: 2.002 against a 0.55 limit. If your design's wobble still reads ≈0.906 Hz with no isolator, you are about to watch that number happen.
  6. Shake, and read the debrief like an engineer. The result line reports the worst storey's drift against its limit and both frequencies. Win or lose, those three numbers tell you why — and which of the three escapes to buy next attempt.

Level 3, "The Damper": when the first two escapes are gone

Eight storeys wobble at 0.5874 Hz; the quake sits at 0.587 Hz with amplitude 15. The isolator is off the table (soft clay soil, says the briefing). Detuning by bracing fails twice over, and the proof run shows both failures:

DesignPeak driftResult (limit 0.85)
Plain 8-storey tower (the trap)3.575Fails — over 4× the limit
Two storeys braced1.404Still fails — and $80 + $16 = $96 is over the $94 budget anyway
Tuned mass damper ($80 + $14 = $94, exactly the budget)—Survives — the only design that does

Why bracing can't get there: two braces only nudge an 8-storey chain's wobble — the tower is still near the quake's rhythm, and at 18 seconds the resonance has plenty of cycles to build in. The damper attacks a different term entirely. It doesn't move the wobble; it drains it, swinging against each sway so the pushes stop accumulating. When detuning is priced out and isolation is banned, absorption is the door left open.

How it's built

The coding choices, for builders — because the model here is a lesson in itself: the simplest simulation that still tells the truth.

The lumped-mass shear model

The tower is simulated as a chain: each floor is a single point mass (1 unit), each storey a spring between the floor below and the floor above — engineers call this a shear building model, and the floors move only sideways. No bending curves, no individual beams. The reason for choosing the crudest model that could possibly work: resonance is not a detail that a fancy model adds — it falls out of masses and springs by themselves. If the simple model reproduces the real phenomenon exactly, the simple model is the honest one, and everything it leaves out can be named (see the simplifications below) instead of hidden.

The frequency is computed, not faked

The game could have hard-coded each tower's wobble from a lookup table. Instead it computes the frequency from the design you actually built — braces, isolator and all — by inverse iteration: start with a guessed sway shape, solve the stiffness system against it, take the resulting shape as the new guess, and repeat until the shape stops changing (it converges to the tower's first wobble shape); then a Rayleigh quotient over that shape gives the frequency. The receipt that this machinery is right: for plain towers it reproduces the closed-form answers exactly — 1.4166, 0.9060, and 0.5874 Hz for 3, 5, and 8 floors, matching the formula for a uniform chain to every digit the game prints. When you brace a floor mid-game and the wobble number moves, that's the solver re-running on your design, not a script.

Damping, honestly small

Damping is applied as Rayleigh damping — the damping matrix built as a small multiple of the stiffness matrix (C = βK), with β chosen so the first wobble mode gets ζ = 3%. It's a standard engineering device, chosen because it damps the big slow sway (the dangerous one) at a stated, checkable rate instead of smearing an arbitrary "friction" over everything. The isolator gets its own, much stronger dashpot — that's what real bearing layers do — and the damper's spring and dashpot are tuned by the classic Den Hartog formulas to the tower's measured first wobble.

Why 480 steps per second

The simulation integrates the motion in steps of 1/480 s. That sounds excessive until you look at the stiffest parts: a braced storey is a stiff little spring, and stiff springs oscillate fast and punish big steps — too-large steps don't just lose accuracy in a spring simulation, they gain phantom energy and explode. 480 Hz keeps even the braced tower's arithmetic stable and honest, and the system is tiny (at most nine masses), so the cost is nothing. The game then plays the recorded quake back at 1.5× speed — labelled as such on the game page, because an 18-second quake at real speed is a long sit, and a sped-up playback you announce is an honest shortcut while a silent one wouldn't be.

Simplifications, named

Named plainly: everything the game leaves out, in one place.
  • Floors are lumped masses. Each floor is one point mass and each storey one spring; a real building bends in a smooth curve, with every beam and joint flexing. The chain captures drift and resonance; it does not capture local bending.
  • The quake is a single steady note. The game's ground motion is one pure frequency, swelling and fading smoothly. Real earthquakes are a jumble of frequencies arriving together, changing as different waves reach the site. A single note is what makes the resonance lesson clean enough to see — and real design codes exist precisely because the jumble means some part of a real quake can sit near a building's wobble.
  • One drift number per storey. A storey lives or dies by a single sideways measurement against a single limit. Real frames tear progressively — connections crack, redistribute load, and sometimes hold in ways no single number predicts.
  • The damper is oversized. The game's tuned mass damper is a quarter of the tower's mass. Real ones are a percent or two of the building's mass, working alongside the structure's own damping. Ours is huge so that its counter-swing is visible and its effect decisive in an 18-second quake; the tuning maths is the real Den Hartog maths either way.
  • Playback at 1.5×. The simulation runs true; only the replay is hurried, and the game page says so.
