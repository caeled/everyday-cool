Engineering.

Every tool in the shed is one of six simple machines wearing a disguise. A bottle opener is a lever. A doorknob is a wheel and axle. A flag pole hides a pulley, a hill path is a ramp, an axe is a wedge, and a wood screw is a ramp wrapped around a pole. This room takes the disguises off, puts four of the machines on benches you can play with, and states the one rule none of them can break: they multiply your force, never your energy — you always pay in distance.

01 / THE SIX SIMPLE MACHINES

Six ideas. Everything else is packaging.

Engineers have sorted every mechanical advantage in the world into six families for over two thousand years. Each one trades the same bargain: spread your effort over more distance, and the force you need shrinks to match. Learn to spot the six and you can look at any tool — a car jack, a pair of scissors, a crane — and say which machine is doing the work inside it.

The lever

A BAR AND A PIVOT

A stiff bar resting on a pivot — the fulcrum. Push down far from the pivot and the short end rises with multiplied force. Bottle openers, crowbars, wheelbarrows, and seesaws are levers; so is your forearm, with your elbow as the fulcrum.

Move the fulcrum toward the load and the lift gets easier. Bench below.

The wheel & axle

A LEVER THAT GOES AROUND

A wheel fixed to a rod: turn the big wheel and the rod twists with multiplied force — or spin the rod and the wheel covers ground. Doorknobs, jar lids, screwdrivers, steering wheels, and every bicycle wheel are this machine.

The bigger the wheel next to its axle, the stronger the twist.

The pulley

A WHEEL THAT TRADES IN ROPE

A wheel with a groove for a rope. One pulley changes a pull's direction — that's all a flag pole does, and it's plenty. Add pulleys so several rope segments hold the load, and each segment carries only its share. Cranes and sailboat rigging stack them by the dozen.

Count the segments holding the load: that's your advantage. Bench below.

The inclined plane

A HILL, PUT TO WORK

A slope. Rolling a load up a gentle hill takes far less force than lifting it straight — paid for with a longer walk. Loading ramps, switchback trails, wheelchair ramps, and the thread of every screw are inclined planes.

Gentler slope, less push, longer path. Bench below.

The wedge

TWO RAMPS, BACK TO BACK

A wedge is a ramp that moves: drive it forward and it pushes things apart sideways with multiplied force. Axes, chisels, doorstops, ploughs, and your own front teeth are wedges. Splitting firewood is this machine at work — the Survival Guide's fire starts here.

Thin wedge, mighty split — and a longer push to drive it.

The screw

A RAMP WRAPPED AROUND A POLE

Take a ramp and wrap it around a pole and you have a screw: turning it walks the load up the slope a tiny step per turn, with enormous force. Wood screws, jar lids, car jacks, and the Archimedes screw that lifts water are all this one idea, coiled.

A ramp wrapped around a pole — that is the whole secret.

02 / THE LEVER BENCH

Slide the fulcrum. Lift the crate.

A crate, a beam, and a stone to pivot on. Physics' seesaw balances people; this bench does work: the crate has to go up. Slide the fulcrum along the beam and watch the push you need change. The nearer the stone sits to the crate, the longer your end of the beam — and the lighter the crate feels.

The lever bench

The bench's beam is 3 metres long. The slider's left end parks the fulcrum near your hands; the right end parks it near the crate. Real levers bend and fulcrums slip — the numbers here are the ideal bar's, and the shape of the trade is what carries over.

03 / THE PULLEY BENCH

Less force. More rope.

Rig the hoist with one, two, three, or four rope segments holding the moving pulley. Every segment carries an equal share of the load, so the pull in your hands is the load divided by the count. The price is printed on the same line: each metre of lift costs you that many metres of rope through your hands.

The pulley bench

Rope segments holding the load

Safety: Never stand under a hoisted load, and never trust a rope, hook, or branch you haven't checked. A machine that multiplies force multiplies the damage when something lets go.

04 / THE RAMP BENCH

The gentler the slope, the longer the walk.

Push the load up the slope instead of lifting it dead. The bench reads everything out for a 1-metre climb: the push along the ramp, the ramp's length, and its rise-to-run ratio — the way ramps are actually specified on building plans.

The ramp bench

The accessibility anchor: a wheelchair ramp is built to at most 1:12 — one unit of rise for every twelve of run, about 4.8°. That is not bureaucracy; it is this bench's arithmetic, written into law so a person pushing their own chair can make the climb. The bench's push numbers leave out rolling friction, which a real ramp charges on top.

05 / THE FRAMES BENCH

Why triangles hold.

Push a square frame sideways and it folds into a parallelogram — builders call it racking. Its corners are hinges and nothing in its shape resists. A triangle cannot rack: its angles are fixed by the lengths of its sides. That is the whole reason bridges, cranes, roof trusses, and farm gates are full of triangles — and why a wobbly gate gets one diagonal board screwed across it. Push each frame and watch its top corner.

The frames bench

The frame

The model is honest geometry, not a simulation of wood: a square's lean equals the push because its joints swing freely, and a triangle's lean is zero because its sides fix its angles. Real frames also depend on their joints — a triangle with rotten corners is a story, not a structure.

06 / THE TWO THAT MOVE

The wedge splits. The screw climbs.

The wedge at work

SPLITTING WOOD, ONE BLOW AT A TIME

An axe head is a wedge: the hammer's downward blow becomes sideways force that pries the log's fibres apart. The thinner the wedge, the greater the split per blow — and the further it must travel to open the crack. Same bargain as every machine on this page.

Force sideways, paid for in travel downward.

The screw, unwrapped

A RAMP WRAPPED AROUND A POLE

Unwrap one turn of a screw's thread and lay it flat: it is a ramp — a long, gentle one. That is why a screw bites so hard. One full turn of the handle walks the load up that whole ramp's length while lifting it only one thread's height.

A ramp wrapped around a pole. Turning is climbing, a little per turn.

Safety: Wedges and splitting tools are struck tools — eye protection, a clear swing, and nobody in the line of the split. A wedge that mushrooms at the head gets ground or retired, not hit harder.

07 / THE RULES NONE OF THEM BREAK

Honest machines.

Force is multiplied. Energy is not.

THE ONE RULE

Every bench on this page says it in its own numbers: halve the force and you double the distance; quarter it and you quadruple the rope. The work that comes out equals the work that goes in — minus friction's cut. Any machine, product, or person claiming more is selling something.

Work in = work out − friction. There are no exceptions on record.

Friction always takes a cut

THE HONEST DISCOUNT

The benches compute ideal machines. Real rope stretches and squeaks over real wheels, real ramps drag, real fulcrums sink into real ground. Expect a real rig to need somewhat more effort than the bench says — and a jammed, rusty one to need far more.

The bench shows the shape of the trade; friction sets the exchange rate.

Multiplied force, multiplied danger

THE SAFETY LINE

A lever that can lift a car can drop one. Rigging rules exist because the machines work: never stand under a hoisted load, never put a body part where a slipping load will land, chock and block anything you lift, and let a doubtful rope retire.

Respect the machine precisely because it works.

11 / HELP

Help — how Everyday Engineering works.

The lever bench

Set the crate's weight, then slide the fulcrum along the 3-metre beam. The bench divides the beam at the fulcrum into the load arm (fulcrum to crate) and the effort arm (fulcrum to your hands); mechanical advantage is effort arm ÷ load arm, and the push you need is the load ÷ that advantage. Slide the fulcrum toward the crate and the push shrinks — while the distance your end travels grows by the same factor, which the readout states.

The pulley bench

Set the load's weight and choose how many rope segments hold the moving pulley — one to four. Each segment carries an equal share, so the pull is the load ÷ the segments, and the rope you pull is the segments × the height lifted. The drawing redraws the rig for the count you pick. A single segment is the flag-pole case: it changes the pull's direction and multiplies nothing.

The ramp bench

Set the load and the slope. The push along the ramp is the load × sin(slope) — the share of its weight that pulls it back downhill — read out for a 1-metre climb with the ramp's length and rise-to-run ratio. The wheelchair standard, 1:12 (about 4.8°), is printed under the bench for comparison: slide down toward it and watch the ramp stretch.

The frames bench

Pick a frame — square, triangle, or square with a diagonal brace — and push. The square's top leans by exactly the push, because its joints swing; the triangle and the braced square lean zero, because a diagonal turns two corners of the square into two triangles, and a triangle's angles are fixed by its side lengths. The bench models the geometry, not the strength of any real wood or joints.

Honest limits — ideal machines, real friction

Every number on this page is an ideal machine's: bars that don't bend, ropes that don't stretch, wheels that spin free. Real machines lose some advantage to friction, flex, and slip, so treat the readouts as the best case and the shape of the trade — which is exact — as the lesson. The tests in this folder re-check the arithmetic on every run.

Works offline?

Yes. The six machines, the four benches, and this Help all run from the files in this folder — including inside the desktop app. Only the Read deeper shelf needs the internet, because those pages live on other people's sites.
