# Collection origin

Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 19 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that evening he named the gap himself — "I keep having a nagging feeling that there is a STEM subject we are missing" — and from the four candidates laid out (Math, Engineering, Earth, Computing, a list he ordered saved so all four will be built) he picked this one first: "lets do Engieering. That links to/from survival too." Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction as the twenty-sixth catalog entry and the seventh workshop inside The STEM Room; this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room follows the Science block's shape (benches that work right on the page and show their working, a verified shelf of legitimate places, Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the live page). It enters the catalog under "Science" with `hubParent: stem-room`, immediately after Everyday Measure, so the STEM Room's bench now reads Physics, Chemistry, Electronics, Measure, Engineering, Body, Radio. The Survival Guide cross-link runs both ways, as Steven specified: this room's "Out in the field" section links to the Guide, and the Guide's Links Out section gained a card naming this room the machine shop behind its field skills (recorded in the Guide's own COLLECTION.md). The room ships web-first: Steven's plan, set the same evening, is that the four queue rooms go live on the web one at a time and reach the desktop app together in a single later release — so no app build carries this room yet, by design.

Everything interactive is computed in the visitor's browser from rules baked into `engineering.js` — the page sends nothing anywhere. The arithmetic is ideal-machine physics, stated as such at every bench: lever MA = effort arm ÷ load arm; pulley effort = load ÷ supporting segments with rope pulled = segments × lift; ramp effort = load × sin(slope), with the wheelchair-ramp standard 1:12 (≈ 4.8°, ADA §405.2) computed from the same rules; the frames bench is pure geometry (a square racks by the push, a triangle and a braced square lean zero). `test.cjs` re-checks all of it on every run. Facts, sources, and the deliberate scope lines (ideal machines, one-to-four segments, geometry-only frames) are recorded in THIRD-PARTY.md.

The rules live in `engineering.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Add, the same evening, at Steven's word while the build was in flight ("Logarithms were the engineer's pocket calculator for three centuries — a compact log bench belongs in this room"): **the pocket log table** — ninety four-decimal mantissas for 1.0–9.9, the characteristic-plus-mantissa rule that stretches the small table to any number, a bench that looks numbers up by the table's own method (linear interpolation — proportional parts — with its one-unit fourth-decimal wobble shown, not hidden), a worked interpolation example (log 2.53), and the slide rule named in one line as the same table turned into lengths on a stick. It was added to the room the same evening, before the room's web release settled, at his instruction.

Second add, the same evening, also at Steven's word: the **Links Out shelf** grew from four links to twelve in three groups, on the Survival Guide's shelf pattern — the six machines one by one (Wikipedia), the arithmetic behind the benches (Mechanical advantage, Logarithm, Slide rule), and one plain-English explainer (Explain That Stuff). Every destination was verified live (HTTP 200) before shipping; Britannica's simple-machine article was considered and left off because it answers automated checks with a 403 and so could not be verified the collection's way (recorded in THIRD-PARTY.md).

When the Math room was built later that evening, it linked here from its log section, and this room's log section gained the return link in the same pass — a short paragraph under the log cards pointing to Everyday Math's full table, its proportional parts, and its printable sheet. The loop now runs both ways, the way the Survival Guide loop closed in this room's own build.

Game add, 20 November 2026: `shake-table.html`, `index.html`, `README.md`, `THIRD-PARTY.md`, `COLLECTION.md`, and `test.cjs`. At Steven Powell's direction, the room gained its game — Shake Table, an original Everyday Cool game built by Muse, in which the player designs a tower (stacking floors, bracing storeys, a base isolator, a tuned mass damper, all against a budget) and then rides out an earthquake simulated as lumped masses on springs. Its lesson is resonance: the killer is the tower's own wobble matching the quake's rhythm, and the fix is changing the rhythm rather than adding strength. It shipped only after its proof run passed: natural frequencies reproducing the closed-form values, the level-2 resonance trap failing at 3.6× the drift limit while braced and isolated solutions survive, the same tower peaking at 2.002 drift on its natural frequency against 0.289 off it, and the damper standing as level 3's only surviving design. The game is linked from the frames bench — this room's structures material — as a "Play it:" paragraph; its card on the Physics Game Table is added with the table's own pass. `test.cjs` asserts the page exists, loads nothing external, and is linked from the room. Facts, simplifications (lumped masses, a single-frequency quake, one drift number per storey, an oversized damper), and the proof numbers are recorded in `THIRD-PARTY.md`.

Lesson added, 20 November 2026: `shake-table-lesson.html`, `shake-table.html`, and `COLLECTION.md`. The game became a frontdoor/backdoor lesson pair: a lesson page carrying resonance in plain language, the mass-spring math and the game's computed natural frequencies, worked examples from its proof runs (the 6.92× on/off-frequency signature, Level 2's two saves, Level 3's damper-only answer), and the coding choices — plus a 📖 Lesson pop-up inside the game that opens the lesson while playing, pausing the quake. The physics core is byte-identical after the pop-up edit and the proof suite re-passes.
