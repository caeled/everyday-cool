Find the Fault — the lesson

Find the Fault is the Tech Bench turned into a game: a broken machine, a story told by the person it happened to, six suspects, and a bench of tests that each cost points. Nothing in it is reflexes. The whole game is one skill — the skill this room exists to teach — and this page is the other half of it: the method, the small amount of math underneath it, one case worked in full from the game’s own proof run, and how the game is built. Readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The subject: diagnosis is a pattern, not a path

Hypothesis before hands

Every case in the game opens with a list of suspects, and that order matters: the list comes before the first test. A test run without a hypothesis is just an event — it happens, it costs, and it teaches nothing, because you never said what you expected it to separate. The bench habit is the other way round. Hold the suspects in mind, ask “which test splits these into the most even, most informative piles?”, run that one, and let the result move the suspects. The game’s suspects panel is that habit made visible: it dims each suspect at the exact moment the pattern rules it out.

The cheapest discriminating test

Tests are not equal, and neither are their prices. A result only earns its points if it discriminates — if different suspects would have answered it differently. The wall test in the first case is the honest dud: the lamp glows for every suspect on the list, so against this case it buys exactly zero information (in a real house it earns its keep — the wall is sometimes guilty; in the game the fault is in the machine by construction). The craft is picking the test whose possible answers divide the remaining suspects best, for the fewest points — and re-asking that question after every result, because the best next test changes as the pattern grows.

Keep a ledger — trust the ledger, not your memory of it

The game writes every result into a lab notebook and never lets you edit it. That is not bookkeeping fussiness; it is the method’s spine. Human memory quietly revises results toward the theory it currently likes. The ledger doesn’t. When you commit to a fault your own notebook has already ruled out, the game tells you which result contradicts you — and in a real shop, that contradiction is the moment a good tech stops, re-reads the page, and finds the case still open. Diagnosis runs on receipts: what the test actually said, written down when it said it.

Elimination is the engine; confirmation is the trap

Most results in the game don’t point at the fault. They remove suspects — and that is how the real work gets done. The beginner’s instinct is to hunt for the result that confirms a favourite theory; the bench instinct is to hunt for the result a favourite theory couldn’t survive. A suspect that survives every attempt to kill it earns its place. A suspect you merely failed to test against is just a hope with good PR. Notice, when you play, how often the winning feeling is not “that result proves it” but “there’s nobody else left standing.” That feeling is the method working.

The heart of it: pattern recognition across the gates

The panel over this room’s bench — the one with the little diagram of logic gates — says it in the curator’s own words:

“The problem … is that most people troubleshoot or navigate a problem by ‘rules’: gates, like it is some kind of map. A useful one to be sure, but really just a map.”

“The real trick to troubleshooting is not logic gates but pattern recognizers that see similarities across gates that may disagree or eliminate a branch. The inference being that something here might apply there is the real vudu.”

The game is built to make you feel both halves of that. The gates half is real: each test is a gate, each result rules branches in or out, and the map works. But in three of the four cases the matrices are designed so that no single gate can name the fault — every result, read alone, is shared by at least two suspects. The only way through is the pattern across the gates. And the sharpest part of the pattern is the resemblance: every case hides an odd result, a reading that belongs — word for word — to a different branch. A dark standby light that means “no power” in one case and “dead board” in another. A BIOS clear that “cures” a dying memory stick by retraining it gently. The debrief names the odd result in every case you solve, because noticing resemblances without surrendering to them is the whole trick. The panel again, on navigation: “It is a ‘go thataway’ — and you get there.” Diagnosis is the same. You rarely march down the one true path; you keep the pattern in view, correct as results arrive, and you get there.

The math the game runs

There is one idea underneath the scoring, and it is small enough to hold in one hand: uncertainty, measured in bits. If n suspects are equally likely, your uncertainty is log2(n) bits — the number of yes/no questions a perfect interrogator would need, on average, to pin the fault.

uncertainty = log2(suspects still standing) Six suspects: log2(6) ≈ 2.58 bits. Two suspects: exactly 1 bit. One suspect: 0 bits — the case is closed.
information gain = uncertainty before − expected uncertainty after A test splits the standing suspects into piles, one pile per possible result. “Expected” means: average the uncertainty of the piles, weighted by how likely each pile is. A test that splits 6 suspects into 3 and 3 gains exactly 1.00 bit whichever way it lands. A test that splits them 6 and 0 — the lamp glowing for everyone — gains 0 bits, and the script never runs it.

Par is built from that one idea. The game contains a scripted diagnostician — a greedy solver — which at every step runs the unused test with the maximum information gain over the suspects still standing (ties broken by price, then by menu order), reads the result, and repeats until one suspect remains. Par for a fault is how many tests the script needs against that fault; par for a case is its worst case across all six faults. Par is not a claim about genius. It is a receipt about the matrix: this is what disciplined elimination costs here, so a player can be measured against something fairer than vibes.

Worked example — Case 1, worked by the script

Before any human played Find the Fault, the script did. Its physics is a matrix, so the test that ships with the collection lifts the game’s core out of the shipped file and plays every case against every fault. Here is its actual line through Stone Dead on a Monday, when the hidden fault is a memory stick — receipts, not illustrations.

StepTest (cost)ResultSuspects leftGain

1Press and watch (2)“Fans spin and it sounds alive — but it never reaches a desktop.”3 — memory, graphics card, BIOS settings1.00 bit

Three tests, eight points, case closed. Read the line the way the method reads it. The first test is the great divider: one press splits the case into the stone-dead branch (power supply, button, motherboard) and the alive-but-blind branch, a full bit for two points. The beeps then do quieter work — the BIOS-settings suspect halts with a screen message, not a beep pattern, so it falls — and notice what the beeps do not do: they don’t separate memory from the graphics card, because this board beeps for both. The monitor swap closes it: a blind-booting machine would have lit the known-good screen, and this one didn’t.

The odd result in this case

Now the trap the script avoided. Suppose, like many good techs, you’d reached early for New battery, safe defaults — the BIOS clear. For this fault it reports: “It boots clean to the desktop.” Those are the exact words it reports for scrambled BIOS settings — the other suspect in its pile. The tempting verdict is “settings, cured, close the ticket.” The truth is subtler: safe defaults retrain memory gently, and a marginal stick can pass at gentle timings. The machine would have failed again at full speed, on a warmer day, in front of the customer. The result was real. The resemblance was real. The inference — something here might apply there — is where the case is won or lost, and it is why the game’s debrief always names the odd result out loud.

And the shotgun, for contrast

Run all ten tests on this case’s menu and you also arrive at the memory stick — in ten tests and 36 points, having paid for the wall, the meter, the bench supply, and six other results the pattern never needed. Same answer, four times the price, and nothing learned about why. The grade line in the debrief (“Solved — the shotgun method”) is the game’s way of saying the answer is the least interesting part of a diagnosis.

How it’s built — the coding choices

Find the Fault is one HTML file with no libraries. The game was built by Muse for Everyday Cool; it is curated by Steven Powell, whose bench panel supplied its method. Three choices do most of the work.

The matrix is the machine

Each case is a table: for every test and every fault, the exact words that test reports when that fault is the true one. Everything else — the notebook, the suspects panel, the scoring, the solver — is arithmetic over that table. “Which suspects are still standing?” is one filter: keep the faults whose column matches every result in the ledger. There is no hidden state and no dice. If a result is in your notebook, the suspects panel is a pure consequence of it, which is what makes the game’s central promise — trust the ledger — literally true of its own code.

The core is a pure-function block, so a script can check the game against itself

The data and the reasoning live in one sealed block of code, fenced between two marker comments (CORE markers), knowing nothing about the page. The proof that ships with the collection lifts that exact block out of the shipped file and asserts: for every case and every fault, the greedy solver identifies the correct fault; every cell of every matrix is filled; par never exceeds the menu; and in Cases 1, 2 and 4, every single result is shared by at least two suspects — the no-single-test property the lesson above describes. (Case 3, the printer, keeps three honest exceptions — a clean print over Wi-Fi really can only be the cable — because a good model doesn’t sand off the parts of reality that don’t fit its favourite rule.) The worked example on this page is the proof’s own trace, not a story written afterwards.

The solver is greedy, and that is deliberate

The scripted diagnostician always takes the locally best test. Greedy is not optimal — a planner that looked two tests ahead could occasionally beat it — and the game says so by letting a thoughtful player finish under par. That gap is a feature: par is a strong, honest baseline, not a ceiling dressed as one. The solver also breaks its ties by price, because on a real bench the cheaper of two equally-informative tests is the right one, and somebody has to pay for the expensive ones.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: each case has exactly one fault. Real machines are under no such obligation — the dead power supply and the marginal memory stick arrive together, in the same week, blaming each other. The suspect list is closed: real benches meet faults nobody listed, and half of diagnosis is noticing the list itself is wrong. Results here are tidy, repeatable, and identical every time; real tests are noisy and intermittent, the fault hides when the meter comes out, and the same test lies politely twice before telling the truth. Points are not money or time — a bench-supply swap costs an hour and a stripped screw, not 6 points, and some real tests (the ones that can kill the patient) are priced in risk, which this game doesn’t model at all. The customer’s story is fixed at the top of the case; real customers remember three new facts the moment you name a suspect. And the matrices are teaching models: real beep codes, rail tolerances, and emulation modes are specific to the exact board, supply, and printer on the bench, and the manual outranks this page every time.
