Bug Hunt — the lesson

Steven's what-if, asked on 25 November 2026, an hour after Shardstorm shipped: Duck Hunt, except the ducks are software bugs — "Bug hunt with weird names like lookerror or something." Then the refinements arrived in the same conversation: the shots should ricochet, with an X-Ray to watch the vectors; the dog who presents your misses "could be a clippy"; and Clippy "could even make an occasional appearance as a bug… boss level as it were." Muse built it the same day. The design rule is the joke and the lesson at once: every bug behaves like the error it is named after. This lesson is what the audit measured, bug by bug, on the shipped file.

The one defect — the builder's, caught before shipping

The audit passed thirteen areas outright and the fourteenth with one defect, and the defect was in the builder's own X-Ray, not in a guest's code. The game page promises that after a shot, its travelled path "stays on screen briefly" so you can compare the finished line with the prediction. It didn't: the path was drawn only while the shot lived, and the shot's final point — the floor point, or the fourth wall — was recorded in the same step that discarded the shot, so the completed path was never visible whole. Fixed before shipping: the completed path now lingers, fading, for 1.4 s. The spot re-audit measured it on the corrected file — after a steep shot died, the trail held 5 points including the terminal point (597.95, 644.08), on the floor where the shot ended, with 1.35 s of life remaining at capture and 3,343 gold-ish pixels of trail and aim path on the canvas; 1.7 s later it had decayed to nothing, as coded. The promise on the page is now true of the page.

What's real

  • The behaviours are mechanisms, not costumes: LOOKERROR's freeze is a distance check against your actual cursor; HEISENBUG's teleport is computed from the shot's predicted path at the moment you fire; OFFBYONE's miss is geometry — its hitbox genuinely sits 1.6 radii beside its body, and the X-Ray draws both.
  • The X-Ray's prediction and the shot's flight are the same function — one tracer computes both — which is why the audit's path-truth numbers are overshoot-sized rather than argument-sized.
  • Clippy's boss form is a real borrow, not a reskin: the same behaviour code runs, with only the body, the name tag and the flat 750 value his own. The X-Ray reads his borrowed behaviour off the same state a player's shot would meet.
  • Every score in the roster was measured paid (or, for the FEATURE, measured charged) on the shipped file — nothing in the table above was read off the source.

What bends

  • LOOKERROR's freeze is a hard edge at 115 px — real look errors are sneakier about the distance at which they hide.
  • HEISENBUG teleports to a uniform-random point in the upper field; a real heisenbug relocates somewhere much more annoying and much less random.
  • The boss's 750 is flat whatever behaviour he borrowed — a MEMORYLEAK-running Clippy swells like a leak but pays like a boss.
  • Recorded path vertices sit up to one substep (≤7 px) past the wall before the clamp — the prediction is the clean line; the flight overshoots it by that much at each bounce, measurably and harmlessly.
  • There is no sound. Clippy's lines are text. He would want it that way.

Verdict

Ship it — one defect found, fixed, and re-measured first. The taxonomy is mechanically honest: the bugs do what their names say, the X-Ray shows the truth about them, and Clippy ships what you miss. Play Bug Hunt.

The flight recorder — the Time Machine, fitted

Bug Hunt took the third fitting of ChatGPT's X-Ray Time Machine, after the Time Machine exhibit itself, Shardstorm, and Shadowline — and it needed three conversions the others didn't. Its bugs wobbled on the wall clock (performance.now()), so the step now reads a tick clock and the wobble is a function of the tick count. Its controller is the mouse: the cursor is a recorded input (a LOOKERROR can see it coming — freezing under your gaze is its whole behaviour, so the gaze had to be in the recording), and a click is an input that lands on a tick boundary, at most a sixtieth of a second later than your finger said it. And every random draw in the game — round compositions, spawn sides and speeds, Clippy's mimic and his lines, the HEISENBUG's teleport, the crash sparks — became one seeded stream carried in the snapshot. Fourteen Math.random() calls in all.

The audit, run against the shipped file on 25 November 2026: an adaptive hunt — the driver aimed at live bug positions with a lead, the way a player does — recorded 1,021 states across two rounds: two BOHRBUGs fixed, a FEATURE shot at −150, a round cleared. Verify replayed it 1,021 of 1,021 states identical. The same recorded inputs on a fresh load reproduced the identical opening state, an identical state at tick 300, and an identical state at the recording's end. An unchanged-replay branch matched at all 819 of its states; a straight-shot branch — the debugger loses its ricochet, shots die at the first wall — diverged in bug population exactly where the original's bank shots had done their work. Zero exceptions.

What bends: the fixed tick replaces the variable step, as in the other fittings; clicks land on tick boundaries rather than mid-frame; the recorder holds sixty seconds (3,600 ticks), after which the hunt continues unrecorded and the panel says so; and the drawing clock still runs on wall time — wing-flutter and Clippy's bob are presentation only and touch no state, so they were deliberately left unseeded, the one clock in the game the recorder doesn't own.
