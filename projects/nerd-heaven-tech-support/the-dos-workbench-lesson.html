The DOS Workbench — the lesson

The DOS Console in this room began as an exhibit — a machine behind glass, with doors. A morning of hard use (26 November 2026) turned it into an instrument: it learned to load a whole archive lazily, to warn its visitors in its builder's own words, and to hand files back out through a rescue door. Steven's naming of what it had become is this page's reason to exist: "it honestly becomes really DOS Workbench under Nerd Heaven… a real honest to god OS you can play with." A workbench is not an exhibit. It is a machine that is already on.

The difference between an exhibit and a bench

The Console page explains itself as it goes — doors in a row, each with its story — because an exhibit's job is to be understood. The Workbench assumes you came to work. It boots DOS by itself when the page opens, onto DOSBox's built-in drive Z:, and waits at a live prompt; the same four doors sit below as benches (mount a drive, one game, the public shelf, rescue files), and mounting anything stops the empty machine and restarts the workbench onto yours. Nothing about the iron changed — same DOSBox core, same js-dos player, same vendored rack, same doors' machinery, all carried over from The DOS Console and audited there. What changed is the posture: the OS first, the explanation in the lesson where it belongs.

What the audit drove

The shipped page, driven untouched: with no input at all it booted to DOSBox's welcome and a live Z:\> prompt — its own autoexec visibly ran (SET BLASTER=A220 I7 D1 H5 T6) — and the status line settled on the workbench's own words. Feeding it the 42 MB test drive through the folder door then restarted the machine onto the drive: the skeleton status landed, the lazy panel armed, the restarted machine's filesystem tree contained the test drive's files, and the rescue door stood enabled against the new machine. Zero page exceptions across the whole drive. The mount, lazy, warning and rescue machinery is the Console's, with its own receipts in the Console's lesson; the two behaviours new here — booting unassisted, and restarting onto a mounted drive — are the ones measured above.

The honest bends

Z: is not your drive. The prompt the page opens on is DOSBox's built-in drive — the machine's own tools, not a C:. Your software arrives when you mount it, and the workbench restarts to do so.

A restart is a restart. Mounting a drive pulls the plug on the empty session: anything unsaved in it goes, exactly as it did on the hardware. The benches stay live after a mount, so drives can be swapped the same way — stop, restart, prompt.

The rest is inherited. The size wall, the streaming door, the lazy drive's empty-folder honesty and the rescue door's readable-copy boundary are all stated in the Console's lesson and apply here word for word; this page does not re-litigate them.

The pack template — build a C:, teach from it

Pack 001 proved the format; the template makes it a trade. A pack author — Steven's workflow, in his words — should only have to build the C: and the machinery around it should already exist. So the room ships a template pack (packs/pack-template.zip, under 2 KB): a skeleton drive whose README.TXT carries the scenario in bracketed slots, and whose TUTOR.BAT is Steven's in-machine lesson idea made real — a small batch-file tutor that lives on the drive, walks the student stage by stage, and checks the work (an IF EXIST proof file) before advancing, or repeats the stage with a hint. Its sample course teaches the two oldest moves — make a file out of nothing with COPY CON, then give it a home — and an author replaces the stage text, the proofs and the hints with their own course. The audit drove the shipped template on this page: the title gate, stage one's text, the failed check wearing its hint, and — with the proof file present — the advance to stage two. One machine truth the template's authoring notes state plainly: a check sees what DOS sees, and DOS sees work done inside the machine at once. Beside the drive sit AUTHORING.txt (the seven steps, including this repo's zip-ignore trap) and Build-C.ps1 — Steven's "C" Builder: a plain PowerShell script that picks a template, stages its drive, adds your files and folders, and exports the pack zip with the drive at its root, ready to mount or expand anywhere. The script is written for Windows PowerShell and is unrun as yet — its first run is its author's, the way this room proves things.

These packs are shareware, in the old sense: if one teaches you something and you want to keep them coming, the tip jar is ko-fi.com/caeled. The same note rides inside every pack as REGISTER.TXT; it is not mentioned anywhere else.
