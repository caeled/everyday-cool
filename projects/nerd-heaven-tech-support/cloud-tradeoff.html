📚 The Lesson — Five Pillars, Plainly

When engineers design a system "in the cloud," they aren't buying magic. They're renting computers in someone else's building, and deciding how many copies, how many locks, and how much speed the job is worth. The Well-Architected frameworks (Amazon and Microsoft both publish one, free) judge every design against five pillars:

🛡️ Resilient
Things will break — a server, a building, a whole region. A resilient design keeps working anyway, because copies of everything important live in more than one place. Reliability people count this in "nines": 99.9% uptime still means about 43 minutes down a month. Each extra nine costs real money.
🚀 Performant
Fast enough for the people using it, even at the busiest moment. Bigger machines, copies close to the user, and room to grow automatically all help — and all cost.
🔒 Secure
Encryption, strong sign-ins, constant updates, and somebody (or something) watching for trouble. Security is a dial, not a switch — and the right setting depends on what you're protecting. A blog and a hospital do not get the same answer.
💰 Cost-Optimized
Not "cheapest" — no waste. Paying for a fortress to host a hobby blog is just as much a design failure as hosting hospital records on one bargain server. The right cost is the one that matches the job.
🧭 Operationally Excellent
The quiet pillar: backups that are actually tested, alarms that somebody reads, changes made carefully, and practice runs for bad days. It's what turns the other four pillars from a drawing into a system that behaves at 2 a.m.

The takeaway: you cannot max everything. Every slider you push up pushes the cost up too, so an architect's real job is choosing — on purpose, with the numbers in front of you — which pillars matter most for this system. Try it above: set up the hospital, then set up the blog, and look at how different the right answer is.
