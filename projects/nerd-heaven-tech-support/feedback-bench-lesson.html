The Feedback Bench — the lesson

The exhibit flies a point-mass rocket that wants to hover at a target altitude, steered by a PID controller running twenty times a second. Underneath it is the single most useful idea in all of engineering: do not try to get it right in one move. Sense the error, apply a fraction of the correction, sense again. That loop — measured, corrected, measured again — holds aeroplanes level, keeps your house at temperature, and is the whole content of this bench. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the three terms, the gravity tax, the sampled world's cliff, the worked proofs, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button on the bench.

The bench was built by Muse for Everyday Cool's Nerd Heaven, at Steven Powell's direction. The philosophy on it is his, stated plainly below in his terms: this bench is corrected by halves as an instrument.

What this is

Open-loop control is a guess: compute the thrust that should hold the rocket at 20 m, fire the motor, and hope the world agrees. It never quite does — the mass is off, the wind is up, the motor is tired. Feedback control refuses to guess. It measures where the rocket actually is, compares it with where it should be, and lets the error — the gap — drive the correction. The controller does not need to know why the rocket is low. Low is enough.

The plant on the bench is stated exactly, and it is small:

ÿ = u − g − (c/m)·ẏ The rocket is a point mass, m = 1, so thrust u is an acceleration. Gravity g = 9.81 (in the bench's arbitrary "metres"), drag coefficient c = 0.2 — light drag, there to keep the physics honest, not to fly the rocket for you. The controller commands u, clamped to 0…40: a motor cannot pull down, and it cannot push infinitely hard. Hover needs u = g = 9.81, so there is headroom — but not infinite headroom, and the clamp matters (see the bends).

The controller is a real discrete PID, one update per step at a fixed dt = 0.05 s — a 20 Hz loop, the way real flight controllers actually run — integrated with semi-implicit Euler (velocity first, then position from the new velocity):

e = r − y  ·  I += e·dt  ·  u = Kp·e + Ki·I − Kd·ẏ Three terms, three jobs. P reacts to the error now. I reacts to the error accumulated. D — on this bench it acts on measurement, −Kd·ẏ — reacts to how fast the error is closing. The rest of this page is those three sentences, taken slowly.

P — and the gravity tax

Proportional control is the obvious idea: the further below target, the harder you push. u = Kp·e. It works — the rocket rises, settles, hovers. But watch where it hovers: below the line, permanently, by an amount no patience will shrink. This is not a tuning failure. It is arithmetic. At a steady hover the acceleration is zero and the velocity is zero, so the plant equation collapses to u = g: the motor must produce exactly gravity's worth of thrust, forever. But a P controller only produces thrust out of error. The only way it can hold u = g is to hold the error that generates it:

ess = g / Kp The gravity tax. With Kp = 4: ess = 9.81 / 4 = 2.4525 m — the rocket hovers at 17.55 m under a 20 m target, and the 2.45 m gap is not sloppiness, it is the payment mechanism: that standing error is what buys the standing thrust. Raise Kp and the tax shrinks (Kp = 9 → 1.09 m) but never vanishes — and raising Kp has its own price, charged at the cliff described below. Any constant load on any P-only loop — gravity, a spring, a steady wind — is paid for the same way, in standing error. Proof 1 below measures it.

I — what the integral remembers

The integral term is memory. Every step, it adds the current error to a running total and lets the total command thrust: Ki·∫e dt. Sit 2 m low for a while and the total grows and grows, pushing thrust up past what the present error alone would ask — and it keeps the extra thrust after the error reaches zero, because the total is still there. That is exactly the property P lacks: the integral can hold thrust with no error at all. It remembers the debt and pays the gravity tax out of savings instead of out of a standing shortfall. Add Ki on the bench and the red "will settle here" line walks up to the target and the gap dies — proof 3 measures the corpse: a mean steady-state error of 1.78×10⁻¹⁴ m, which is zero wearing a lab coat.

Memory has a failure mode, and the bench states its defence. If the thrust saturates — motor pinned at 40 while the rocket is still far below target — a naive integral keeps accumulating error it cannot act on, and when the rocket finally arrives it overshoots wildly while the bloated integral unwinds. That is integral windup. This bench uses conditional integration: on any step where the thrust is saturated and the error would drive it deeper into the same saturation, the integral is simply not allowed to grow. The memory only records debts it is actually paying.

D — what the derivative anticipates

The derivative term is a brake with foresight. On this bench it acts on measurement: the thrust includes −Kd·ẏ, proportional to the rocket's own climb speed. Rising fast toward the target? The term subtracts thrust before the target arrives, in proportion to how hard the arrival is going to be to stop. (Acting on measurement rather than on the error signal also means a setpoint jump does not kick it — the setpoint is not in the term — and it uses the plant's measured velocity directly instead of differencing a noisy error.)

With Ki = 0 the closed loop is a textbook second-order system, and the derivative's effect is a number, not a metaphor:

s² + (Kd + c)·s + Kp  →  ωn = √Kp,   ζ = (Kd + c) / (2√Kp) Note the c: the plant's own drag is damping too, so the damping ratio ζ counts total damping, controller plus plant. With Kp = 4 and Kd = 1.0: ωn = 2, ζ = 1.2/4 = 0.30. A step response at ζ = 0.30 overshoots by exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)) = 37.23%. With Kd = 0 the only damping is drag, ζ = 0.05, and the same formula predicts 85.45% — a rocket that sails past its floor and comes back, twice. Proof 2 checks the 37.23% against the simulation; proof 5 checks the calming directly.

How the overshoot proof is framed — honestly

Gravity complicates the naive version of this measurement: step the setpoint and the response is a step plus a constant load. The bench's framing separates them. Gravity shifts the equilibrium — the rocket settles at r − g/Kp, not at r — but it does not touch the dynamics about that equilibrium: subtract the equilibrium and the motion is the clean second-order system above. So the proof settles the rocket at the equilibrium for setpoint 10 (measured: 7.5475 m), steps the setpoint to 14 (new equilibrium: 11.5475 m), and reckons the overshoot as the excursion past the new equilibrium as a percentage of the equilibrium's own 4 m move. No gravity feedforward is smuggled in anywhere — the controller never sees one, in the proofs or on the bench. The load is handled by measuring around it, and the lesson says so because that distinction is the kind of thing that quietly invalidates prettier proofs.

The cliff — instability belongs to the sampled loop

Now crank Kp on the bench, with some Ki in, and keep cranking. The corrections get stronger, the ringing gets harder, and eventually the loop shakes itself apart — thrust banging between the clamps, the rocket chattering and never settling. It is tempting to file that under "too much gain is bad" and move on. The truth is more specific and more useful: an ideal continuous PD loop on this plant does not go unstable with Kp at all. The continuous closed loop is s² + (Kd + c)s + Kp, whose roots sit in the left half-plane for every positive Kp — push as hard as you like, it only gets stiffer and faster, never unstable. The instability on this bench is real, but it is the sampled loop's: the controller measures, computes, and holds its thrust for a full dt = 0.05 s before it may correct again, and at high gain the correction it is still applying belongs to a world one step out of date. Past a boundary gain, each stale correction overshoots further than the last and the error alternates and grows, step to step, at the loop's own Nyquist edge. This is not a defect of the bench. It is the bench's main lesson: every real digital controller — every flight controller, every thermostat with a microchip, every motor drive — lives in the sampled world, and the sampled world has a cliff that the continuous mathematics does not warn you about.

Proof 4 measures the cliff two independent ways and makes them agree. From the simulation: a bounded test — start exactly at the hover equilibrium, step the setpoint by a whisper (+0.002 m), run 150 s, and call the loop unstable if the rocket is still moving fast in the last 20 s (max |v| > 0.05 m/s); scan Kp upward in steps of 25 and take the first gain where the test fires and keeps firing at the next two steps — the persistence rule exists because we found, and report, a lone flicker at Kp = 1475 where a saturation limit cycle briefly sustains motion just below the true boundary and then dies at 1500. From the mathematics: build the 3×3 state matrix of the discretised closed loop (state [y, v, I], step order exactly as the core performs it — integral first, then velocity, then position), take its characteristic polynomial, find its poles numerically, and bisect Kp for the crossing max|pole| = 1. The two methods meet at 1.74% — the simulation's first persistently unstable gain, 1525, against the poles' 1551.97. Both numbers belong to the discretised system at dt = 0.05 s; halve the step and the cliff moves out, which is also true of real controllers and is left for the player to reflect on. (The classical way to find such a boundary by experiment — raise the gain until the loop oscillates steadily, then back off by rule — is the Ziegler–Nichols method, in the references; this bench measures the boundary instead of tuning by it.)

Corrected by halves

Steven's philosophy, stated whole elsewhere in this collection, is: don't try to get it all done at once — head the general direction of the problem, see what looks right and what doesn't, correct, and get there by halves instead of full moves. A PID loop is that philosophy with a sample rate. It never computes the one perfect thrust. It senses the error, applies a fraction of the correction the error calls for, senses again, and lets the repetition do what no single perfect move could — including absorbing a world (gravity, drag, gusts) that it never modelled. The gains are just how big a fraction, how long a memory, and how much anticipation you mix in. This bench is that idea as an instrument, and it has siblings: the Map Room's Go Thataway — navigation by heading and correction rather than by perfect route — and Nerd Heaven's own Corrected by Halves, the same loop drawn as a way of working. Sense, correct by halves, sense again. Twenty times a second, or once a decision — the loop does not care about the clock. It cares that you keep closing it.

Bends named honestly

Point mass. The rocket has no attitude, no tilt, no gimbal, no thrust lag, no flex. It is one number for altitude and one for velocity. Everything the bench claims is about the feedback loop, and the loop's behaviour — the tax, the memory, the brake, the cliff — survives into real systems; the rocket's simplicity is the price of seeing the loop bare.

No sensor noise, no sensor delay. The controller sees the true altitude and the true velocity, instantly, every step. Real sensors are noisy and late, and noise is exactly what makes derivative action dangerous in the field (this bench's derivative-on-measurement with a directly measured velocity is the well-behaved arrangement; a real build would filter). What the bench does include is the one delay no digital controller escapes: the sample step itself, thrust held constant for dt = 0.05 s — and that alone owns the stability boundary in proof 4.

Thrust clamp, stated. u is clamped to 0…40 every step. Hover needs 9.81, so ordinary play rarely touches the clamps — but the instability test's chatter and any violent transient live against them, and the clamp is part of why a saturated loop can sustain a limit cycle (the Kp = 1475 flicker in proof 4's scan is one, caught and named rather than averaged away).

Anti-windup choice, stated. Conditional integration: the integral does not grow on a step where thrust is saturated and the error points deeper into that saturation. Other schemes exist (clamping the integral, back-calculation); this one is simple, stated in the core, and sufficient for every proof here.

The discretisation owns the boundary. Proof 4's cliff at Kp ≈ 1552 is a property of this loop at dt = 0.05 s, computed from the discretised state matrix and confirmed in the discretised simulation. The continuous plant with this controller structure has no such boundary in Kp. Anyone quoting the number without the dt is quoting half a fact.

The ground is the room, not the physics. The core has no ground. The bench page clamps the displayed rocket at altitude 0 (downward velocity zeroed) so a crashed rocket rests on the pad instead of falling through the floor of the world. No proof goes near it — every proof hovers at 20 m or steps between equilibria near 10 m.

Drag is linear and light. c = 0.2, force proportional to velocity. Real drag is quadratic and this rocket is imaginary; the drag's job here is to be the plant's own small damping so that proof 2's ζ honestly counts it — and it does: ζ = (Kd + c)/(2√Kp), stated wherever the number is used.
