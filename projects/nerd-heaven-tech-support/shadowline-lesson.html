Shadowline — the lesson

Steven's third what-if of 25 November 2026, out of the Vectrex conversation: Zaxxon "had elements of flight in it… you had to THINK in 3d." The isometric screen never shows your height; it gives you a shadow and an altimeter and makes your brain rebuild the third dimension. Muse built the tribute the same day — an isometric fortress run over an honest 3D world, with Steven's standing option built in from birth: press T and the altimeter goes away, and you fly by shadow alone. This lesson is what the audit measured, instrument by instrument, on the shipped file. It passed all nine areas with no defects — the first of the day's three originals to come through clean on the first pass.

What's real

  • The 3D is the physics, not the picture: collisions are decided in world units (lateral, altitude, forward) against boxes with stated heights, and the isometric projection is a pure function used only to draw. That's why the boundaries land on the exact tenth of a unit the page claims.
  • The shadow is not decoration. It is the ship's own silhouette projected onto the ground plane by the same function that draws everything else, so its separation cannot drift from the truth — the audit checked the function, then checked the pixels, and both agree.
  • The altimeter's gap highlight and the profile strip are drawn from the same obstacle state the collisions use; they cannot show a gap the wall doesn't have.
  • The generator's fairness is a proved bound, not a hope: gap centres move at most 16 units between walls, walls are never closer than ~130 units of travel, and the ship climbs at 27 units a second — the audit's hardest measured demand was 3.6.

What bends

  • The ground shears so that ahead is up-and-to-the-right — a drawing choice in the 1982 style; lateral movement on screen is therefore diagonal, and takes a minute to feel natural.
  • Ground targets count as 5 units tall for collision, whatever their drawn size; fly into one below that and you clip it.
  • While a ship is lost, the world holds still for the 1.2 s before the next one launches — the corridor does not scroll under a crash.
  • A fresh ship launches with 70 fuel, however the last one died.
  • There is no sound.

Verdict

Ship it — clean. The screen still won't show your height, but now the receipts show the instruments never lie about it: the shadow is the altitude, the altimeter reads true, and the walls are always fair. Play Shadowline — then press T, and fly it the 1982 way.

The flight recorder — the Time Machine, fitted

Late the same night, Shadowline took the second fitting of ChatGPT's X-Ray Time Machine — the flight recorder first proven on the Time Machine exhibit and fitted to Shardstorm earlier that evening. The operation is the same one, and Shadowline needed it in the same places: the variable frame step became a fixed 1/60-second tick, and every one of the game's fourteen Math.random() calls — the course generator's gap centres, spawn distances, obstacle placements, even the crash sparks — became a seeded stream whose state rides inside every snapshot. That second change is the one that matters most here: Shadowline's course was always generated ahead of the ship, so seeding the generator makes a course a repeatable thing. The same sector, flown twice, is now a controlled experiment instead of a memory.

The audit, run against the shipped file on 25 November 2026: a scripted 900-input flight recorded 1,119 states — it threaded a wall, crashed three times, and ended the run — and the recorder's Verify replayed it 1,119 of 1,119 states identical. The same 900 inputs on a fresh load produced the identical opening state and an identical state at tick 300. An unchanged-replay branch from tick 150 matched the original at all 969 of its states. And the branch this game was built to argue with — the thirsty ship, fuel burning twice as fast from the fork — separated from the original by exactly the arithmetic: sixty ticks after the fork its gauge read 2.200 less (2.2 a second × 1 second), with altitude and lateral position still identical, because fuel doesn't steer the ship until it runs out. Zero exceptions anywhere.

What bends, stated on the page as in the others: the fixed tick replaces the variable step, so a slow machine drops drawing frames instead of slowing the flight; the recorder holds sixty seconds (3,600 ticks), after which the flight continues unrecorded and the panel says so; and the prediction branch judges altitude two seconds out — a drum refuel in those two seconds is the recording's business, not the branch's.
