Shardstorm — the lesson

Steven's what-if, asked on 25 November 2026 minutes after playing Mine Storm on the Vectrex Console: what if the rocks in Asteroids weren't blobs but honest polygons — triangles, hexagons, other polygons — in a mixed field, and they broke up the way geometry says? Muse built it the same day: your bullet's path through a rock is the chord, and the rock splits along that line into the two real polygons on either side. This lesson is what the audit measured — including the part where the audit caught the builder's own headline claim being wrong, and the page had to be corrected before it shipped.

The law that failed, and the one that holds

The first version of the game page printed this law: cut an n-sided polygon and the pieces' sides add to n + 2. It was the builder's claim, made in the chat where the game was proposed, and it is the rule for a cut drawn between two existing corners — a diagonal. A bullet doesn't draw diagonals. Its line crosses two edge interiors and creates two new corners, which both pieces own. The first audit's 600 cuts falsified the printed law completely: 572 generic cuts, and not one satisfied n + 2 — every one summed to n + 4. The game's own counter said so in live play: one cut through a hexagon, 6 sides became 10.

The page was corrected and the splitter fixed (it had also been double-counting a corner that lies exactly on the cut). The re-audit then decomposed all 597 two-piece cuts, and the true law is prettier than the false one — it counts how the line meets the corners:

How the cut crossesCuts measuredPieces' sides sum to
Two edge interiors (the ordinary bullet)572n + 4
Exactly one existing corner9n + 3
Two existing corners — a true diagonal16n + 2

No exceptions in any class. Steven's standing rule for this whole collection is that breakage is the only honest audit; this exhibit is that rule catching its own builder, in print, before a single player saw the wrong law. The page now states the measured law, and its counter agrees with it.

What's real

  • The split is genuine polygon clipping (Sutherland–Hodgman, once per half-plane) against the bullet's actual line of travel — the pieces are computed from the parent's vertices, never canned shapes.
  • The mixed field is real: each wave spawns polygons of 3–10 sides with irregular radii, some stretched, so the field is never one shape repeated.
  • Every number on the game page — scores, the floor, the turn rate, the respawn — was measured on the shipped file, not read off the source.

What bends

  • Rocks pass through each other; only the ship and the bullets interact with them.
  • The cut uses the bullet's whole line at the moment of contact, so a graze near a corner can still split the rock along a line the visible bullet only touched at one point — the chord is the path, not the segment.
  • Ship collision is a distance check against the polygon's edges, not pixel-perfect hull contact.
  • There is no sound.

The X-Ray layer — added the same day, at Steven's word

Steven played the shipped game for a few minutes and asked for the Emulator X-Ray treatment on it too. F2 (or the X-RAY button under the field) now draws every live bullet's full chord across the field, labels each rock with its side count and area — gold when within twice the vaporize floor — shows the drift arrows and the ship's collision circle, and captions every cut at the cut site with its own arithmetic. The X-Ray audit (19/19 checks) read two captions verbatim off the shipped page: "6 → 5 + 5 · THE ORDINARY CUT · n + 4" with "area 9,353 → 4,677 + 4,677 · conserved", and, for a shot fired exactly along a square's diagonal, "4 → 3 + 3 · TRUE DIAGONAL · n + 2". Captions arrive within ~53 ms of the cut and fade at their coded 2.8 s (measured gone at 2,865 ms); the render census found the dashed chord at 590 lit pixels with the layer on and 0 with it off; the base game underneath is unchanged — same +10 cut, same counters, zero errors. One cosmetic note, recorded rather than smoothed over: a caption rounds each piece's area independently, so the hexagon's halves read 4,677 + 4,677 against a parent's rounded 9,353 — the underlying areas conserve exactly, as the split-law audits established; the rounding is in the caption, not the cut.

Bumps — the station-keeping is gone

Steven's second play-test note, same afternoon: a rock that bumps you should shift your motion — the ship seemed to have "a magical station keeping ability." He was right; contact had been binary, death or nothing. Now a gentle contact trades momentum along the line from the rock's centre to the ship, with the rock's mass set by its area and a restitution of 0.55; only a closing above 160 px/s destroys the ship. The bump audit (27/27) drove the contacts as single synchronous steps so the numbers are exact: a hexagon of mass 3.74 closing at 40 px/s left the ship drifting at 48.92 px/s — the predicted impulse to the digit — while the rock slowed from 40 to 26.92, momentum along the normal conserved at 149.65 before and after. A light triangle (mass 0.52) took the bump the other way: the ship gained 21.26 px/s and the rock nearly stopped and reversed, the velocity changes splitting in the exact inverse-mass ratio of 1.92. A 300 px/s closing cost exactly one life and respawned the ship at the centre. One contact, one BUMP caption, clean separation — the pair's distance grew from 59.70 to 76.61 px over the following second, no machine-gunning. The game is the realer for it, which was the point of the note.

Rock on rock — the sandbox note

Steven's fourth note came from the sandbox that evening: rocks should not pass through each other — they should bounce. Now they do. Every pair of rocks is tested each step: each rock collides as a circle of its mean vertex radius around its centroid, with the same mass model (area / 2500, floored at 0.35) and the same restitution (0.55) as a ship bump; an impulse trades momentum along the line of centres, and a positional push-apart, weighted by inverse mass, keeps pairs from resting overlapped. Spin is not exchanged — the model's named simplification, printed in the game's Help beside the rule.

The bounce audit drove the shipped page through its test hooks. Two equal hexagons (area 4,157 each) closing head-on at 60 px/s separated at exactly 33.0 px/s — 0.55 × the closing speed, the restitution to the digit — and their closest sampled approach was 80 px, exactly the sum of their radii: they touched and parted, never interpenetrating in any sample. An unequal pair (areas 8,148 vs 2,037) traded by the book: the big rock slowed 25 → 14.2 px/s while the small one was knocked away −10 → +33.4 px/s, both figures matching the impulse formula to the hook's rounding, and total momentum held (73.3 → 73.5 in the hook's integer areas). Under the X-Ray, a bounce is captioned BOUNCE with the closing speed it traded.

Recoil — Newton's third law, kept slight

Steven's third note, minutes later: shouldn't shooting push the ship backwards, slightly? It should, and now it does — 5 px/s per shot, opposite the muzzle. The recoil audit (12/12) measured a single shot from rest at exactly Δv = (0, +5.000) px/s, the kick reversing to (0, −5.000) when the ship turned 180°, and a one-second burst of six shots summing to 26.536 px/s — the predicted damped sum to the third decimal, bounded, no runaway. Three of this game's physics rules were now written by its first player's hands on the controls, which is how the collection's games are supposed to grow.

Chords off — visualize the line yourself

Steven's fourth note, the same afternoon: the X-Ray's full-screen chord tracer should be optional — "not always off, just an option — force you to visualize that line." It is now. The T key (or the CHORDS button beside X-RAY) hides only the dashed chord lines; the rock labels, drift arrows, the ship's circle and the cut captions all stay, and the bullet still cuts along its whole line of travel — a line you now hold in your head from the moment you fire. The spot audit on the shipped file: a horizontal bullet's row carried 414 lit pixels of dashed chord with the lines on and 6 with them off; a rock's gold side-count label still drew with the chords hidden (67 px at its centre); a real T keypress toggled the state; and a shot fired blind still split a hexagon for +10 into two pieces. The option changes what you see, never what the game does — which is exactly what an option is.

The flight recorder — the Time Machine, fitted

Steven's question, the night the X-Ray Time Machine shipped: can we do it with shards? The recorder ChatGPT designed was adopted as a core, and Shardstorm was rebuilt around it. The step is now a fixed 1/60-second tick; every random draw in the game — wave spawns, rock shapes, split pushes and spins, even the sparks — comes from a seeded generator whose state rides inside the recording; and your controls are logged tick by tick. The panel under the field rewinds the run, branches it (fly it differently, replay it unchanged, predict, or flip one rule — the recoil — and replay your exact controls against the original flying beside you as a dashed gold ghost), and verifies it: re-simulate the whole flight from tick zero and compare every state.

The retrofit audit drove the shipped page headlessly, through its own recorder:

  • A scripted flight of 607 ticks under continuous fire verified 607/607 — every state reproduced from the recorded inputs alone.
  • An aim-driven flight of 2,972 ticks with 54 cuts verified 2,972/2,972.
  • Two fresh page loads, same seed and same 300 inputs: byte-identical first 301 states, the wave-one field included.
  • An unchanged-replay branch of the long flight matched the original at all 2,974 states.
  • The recoil branch — Steven's experiment, on the game that asked it: identical to the fork, and the first divergence landed exactly one tick after the first post-fork shot, the branch ship's velocity differing by exactly 5.000 px/s with its position still identical — the kick itself, isolated. The branch then verified 440/440 on its own.
  • Clean shots in the recorded stream still obey the old physics to the digit: post-shot velocity equals the damped velocity minus 5 px/s down the muzzle line, error 0. The cut captions kept their law — n + 4, n + 3, the true diagonal's n + 2 — throughout.
  • Zero JavaScript exceptions across every run.

What bends, printed plainly. The old loop ran on a variable step capped at 1/30 s, so on a slow machine the game itself slowed down; the fixed tick keeps real time at any refresh rate and drops a drawing frame instead. The recorder holds sixty seconds — past that, the flight continues unrecorded and the panel says so. And the audit's scripted fields never emptied, so a mid-run wave spawn rests on the seeded stream's construction and the identical wave-one fields across loads rather than on a replayed wave transition; the first player to clear a field on the recorder will exercise it live.

The designer, playing the fitted build (25 November 2026, verbatim — this is the receipt the recorder was built for):

“Shardstorm gets more fun the longer you leave it running and playing. You start using your own motion in both drive and rotation and even brief thrust bursts to Tai Chi your way into the right firing position. Thrust vector becomes a tool instead of a control is a wierd way to descibe it.”

“…even the recoils from a bounce. It really is facinating how much TaiChi demonstrates all of this from redirection using thrust as an aim even in the reflection and shadow bench. Skip. Its all digitial taichi.”

That is the momentum model — recoil per shot, the ship-bump trade, rocks bouncing off rocks — discovered by hand: thrust stops being how you move and becomes how you aim. The same redirection runs through Bug Hunt's banked shots, Shadowline's drift, and Skip's long way around. Digital Tai Chi is the designer's own name for the family.

Two laws of contact — Flow and 3 Lives (26 November 2026)

Steven's ask: the game as it stands plays as a no-death game, and that should stay the default — but a 3-life version, switched on deliberately, would let a player test progress against themselves, and foster a bit of the friendly competition Asteroids itself ran on. Lives lost by a full-on collision, whole — not by a damage meter.

The audit first read the code before touching it, and the code held a surprise: the lives system was already there — three ships, an extra at 10,000, a game-over state — standing behind the bump law. Only a closing faster than 160 px/s could reach it, so played the Tai Chi way, yielding and redirecting, the game behaves exactly as Steven described it: no death. What shipped is therefore a second law beside the first, not a repair. Flow is the default and is untouched. 3 Lives — the MODE button or the M key, between runs only — makes any full contact cost a ship: no closing-speed test, no partial damage, a life lost whole. The mode keeps your best score on this machine and shows it on the title and game-over screens: a run measured against your own last one, and a number a friend has to beat. The flight recorder logs which law a run was flown under, and restoring a recording reinstates its law, button label and all — a replay is judged by its own rules.

Measured, driving the shipped page: a motionless rock laid over the ship in Flow — a bump, lives stay 3. A rock driven in at 800 px/s in Flow — a ship lost, 3 to 2: the old law intact. The same motionless rock in 3 Lives — a ship lost at effectively zero closing speed. Three soft contacts drained the mode to game over, and the best score the run had earned (45, from one vaporized triangle) was the score found stored on the machine afterward, to the digit. Replay verification on runs flown with recorded inputs only passed exactly in both laws — 241 checks in 3 Lives, 150 in Flow. (The collision tests placed rocks through the game's test hooks to make the geometry exact; hooks are not inputs, so no recording made that way is asked to verify.) Zero page errors across all three drives.

Verdict

Ship it — corrected, and now two ways to fly it. The game does exactly what its page now says, and the page says only what was measured. Play Shardstorm.
