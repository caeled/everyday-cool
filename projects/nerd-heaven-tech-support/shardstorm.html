Shardstorm — the chord is the cut

Steven's what-if, asked the morning he played Mine Storm on the Vectrex Console: what if the rocks in Asteroids weren't blobs, but honest polygons — triangles, hexagons, heptagons — and they broke the way geometry says they should? Here they do. Your bullet's path through a rock is a chord, and the rock falls apart along exactly that line, into the two real polygons on either side of it. You don't just break the rock. You choose the cut.

The law of the field: cut an n-sided polygon with one straight line and the two pieces' side counts add to n + 4 — the cut creates two new corners, and each piece owns both of them. Thread the cut exactly through two existing corners — a true diagonal — and it's n + 2 instead. Either way the counter keeps the honest total: watch it climb as you cut. Pieces too small to cut again (under the area floor) vaporize when hit, and pay by their sides. Clear the field and the next wave brings more polygons. Press F2 (or the X-RAY button) and the field turns transparent: every bullet's full chord is drawn before it lands, every rock wears its side count and area, and each cut is captioned with its own arithmetic as it happens. And if the drawn chord does the visualizing for you, press T (or the CHORDS button) to hide just the full-screen chord lines — the labels, arrows and captions stay, the cut still happens along the same line, and the line is one you have to picture yourself.
Help ▾ — how Shardstorm works, in full

The game. Pilot the ship, shoot the polygons, don't touch them. The field wraps at the edges: fly off one side and you come back on the other, and so do the rocks. Bullets don't wrap.

The cut. When a bullet reaches a polygon, the game takes the bullet's line — the straight path it was travelling — and splits the polygon along that whole line, the way a chord crosses a circle. Both pieces are real polygons, computed from the parent's own vertices, and both keep drifting with a push apart along the cut. Where you hit matters less than the angle you hit at: the same rock cut two ways gives two different fields.

Scoring. A cut that produces two pieces: 10 points. A piece that vaporizes — because it was already under the area floor, or because the cut left it under the floor — pays 15 points per side it had. An extra ship every 10,000 points.

Recoil. Firing kicks the ship backwards — 5 px/s per shot, straight down the line opposite the muzzle. Slight per shot, as it should be; hold the trigger through a long burst and it sums to a drift you can feel, and thrust is how you pay it back. Steven's note again: the push had to be there.

Bumps. Touching a rock is not automatically death.

A gentle contact trades momentum along the line from the rock's centre to the ship: the rock's mass is its area, so a big decagon shoves you hard and barely notices, while a small triangle gets knocked away — and you are set drifting, to correct with thrust. Only a hard closing — relative speed toward the rock above 160 px/s — destroys the ship. After a bump you're untouchable for three-quarters of a second so one contact can't machine-gun you. Steven's play-test verdict stands as the design note: before this, the ship had a magical station-keeping ability. It doesn't anymore.

Two laws of contact (26 November 2026). The bump law above is Flow, the default, unchanged. Steven asked for the other classic: a 3 Lives mode, switched with the MODE button or the M key between runs, where any full contact with a rock costs a ship — no closing-speed test, no damage meter, a life lost whole, the way Asteroids dealt it. Three ships, an extra at 10,000 as always, and the mode keeps your best score on this machine, shown on the title and game-over screens — a run you can measure yourself against, and a number a friend has to beat. The flight recorder logs which law a run was flown under, so a replayed run is judged by its own rules.

Rock on rock. Rocks are bodies too — they no longer drift through each other. When two rocks meet they trade momentum along the line between their centres, with the same mass rule (area) and the same spring (restitution 0.55) as a ship bump, and they are pushed apart so they cannot rest overlapped. Each rock collides as a circle of its mean radius, and spin is not exchanged — those are the model's named simplifications. Under the X-Ray a bounce is captioned BOUNCE with the closing speed it traded. Steven's sandbox note, again the design: rocks should bounce off each other. Now they do.

The floor. A piece whose area is under 800 square pixels can't be cut — a hit vaporizes it outright. Slivers a cut leaves under the floor vaporize on the spot and pay out the same way.

The X-Ray layer (F2). The same treatment the Emulator X-Ray gives its little games, turned on this one: dashed lines extend every live bullet's path across the whole field — the chord it will cut with; arrows show every rock's drift and the ship's velocity; each rock is labelled with its side count and its area in square pixels, and rocks within twice the floor turn gold as a warning. When a cut lands, a caption appears at the cut site with the arithmetic — parent sides → the two pieces' sides, which law the cut obeyed (n + 4, n + 3, or the true diagonal's n + 2), and the area before and after, conserved. Nothing in the X-Ray changes the game; it only shows the mathematics the game was already doing.

Hiding the chords (T). The dashed chord lines can do your visualizing for you — Steven's note, after a session with them on. The T key (or the CHORDS button beside X-RAY) hides only those full-screen lines: the rock labels, the drift arrows, the ship's circle and the cut captions all stay, and the bullet still cuts along its whole line of travel. The difference is that now you have to hold that line in your head from the moment you fire. Not a harder game, exactly — a quieter one, where the geometry is yours to imagine before it proves itself on the rock.

The flight recorder. Shardstorm now carries the Time Machine — the recorder designed by ChatGPT for the X-Ray Time Machine and first fitted here. The game runs on a fixed tick and a seeded field: every rock, split, bump and spark is drawn from a seed carried inside the recording, and your controls are logged tick by tick. Play a run, then scrub the timeline back, branch from any moment, and fly the alternative while the original runs beside you as a dashed gold ghost. Change one rule flips the recoil from the branch tick onward and replays your original controls exactly — the Recoil Experiment, on the game that asked the question. Verify re-simulates the whole recording from its first tick and compares every state: if the past replays exactly, it says so, with the tick count. The recorder holds sixty seconds; past that the flight continues unrecorded and the panel says so.

Honesty notes. The rocks are convex polygons in a mixed field — triangles up to decagons, irregular and some of them stretched, so no two fields are alike; the split is exact polygon clipping, not a canned animation. Rocks pass through each other — only the ship and the bullets interact with them. Sound: none. This page makes no network requests and runs from a lone local file.

On a touch screen: the recorder below the stage — its buttons and timeline — is fully tappable, and the field is worth watching on its own; but flying the ship takes the arrow keys and Space, so play itself needs a keyboard.

Shardstorm was designed by Steven Powell ("what if the shapes were triangles, hexagons and other polygons that break up?") and built by Muse the same day, 25 November 2026. Its lesson — what the audit measured, including the side-count law checked cut by cut — is beside it: the Shardstorm lesson.
