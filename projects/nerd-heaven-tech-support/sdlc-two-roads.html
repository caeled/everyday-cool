🛤️ Two Roads

Waterfall vs Agile — the same project, two ways of building it

The job

A small town library wants a new website: a catalog search, an events page, and a way to reserve books online. One project, one budget to start — $12,000 and 12 weeks. Two teams take it on. One builds it the Waterfall way, one the Agile way.

Halfway through, the librarian will change her mind about something. She always does — that's not a flaw in the plan, that's real life. Watch what each road does with that moment.

🏁 How it turned out

Waterfall delivered everything on the original list — plus the new mobile reservations — but the change arrived after the design was signed off and the desktop form was already built. Tearing that out and redoing it cost +$6,000 in rework and pushed delivery from week 12 to week 16. The library had nothing usable until the very end.

Agile delivered a working catalog search at week 2, events at week 4, and when the change arrived at week 6 it simply became the top item for the next sprint. Swapping one planned feature for the new one cost about +$800 and no delay — the site was live and being used while it kept growing.

Same project. Same change of mind. Very different bill.

📚 The lesson

Every way of building software follows the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC): figure out what's needed (requirements), plan how it'll work (design), build it (development), check it (testing), put it live (deployment), and keep it running (maintenance). Waterfall and Agile do the same phases — they just arrange them differently. Waterfall does each phase once, in a long line. Agile does all of them over and over, in small loops.

When Waterfall genuinely fits

  • The requirements are fixed and truly known up front — and won't move.
  • The work is regulated: medical devices, avionics, payroll tax engines — places where every step must be documented and approved before the next begins.
  • A contract specifies exactly what will be delivered, and changing it means renegotiating the contract.

When Agile genuinely fits

  • Requirements are uncertain or will change — the customer needs to see something working before they know what they want.
  • Getting something usable early is worth more than getting everything late.
  • The project is exploratory — a new product, a new audience, a first attempt.

The real takeaway: neither road is “the right one.” Waterfall isn't stupid and Agile isn't magic — they're tools, and good builders pick the tool that matches the job. The expensive mistake isn't choosing one or the other; it's choosing one as a religion and forcing every project down a road that doesn't fit it.
