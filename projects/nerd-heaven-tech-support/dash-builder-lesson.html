Dash Builder — the lesson

Steven Powell asked for something special for his son David, who is nine, loves the Web Page Workshop next door, and is obsessed with Geometry Dash. What David wanted was the thing Geometry Dash itself is famous for: not just playing levels, but building them — and then playing what you built. So this bench is a game studio in one page: an editor where you paint blocks and spikes on a grid, a player where a cube runs your level on one button, and an Export that turns your level into a web page of its own. This page is the other half: how the runner works, the numbers it was tuned to, and the receipts from driving it before it shipped.

The whole game is three rules

Geometry Dash (made by RobTop Games) looks frantic, but its core is tiny, and Dash Builder keeps exactly that core and nothing else:

  1. The cube runs by itself at one fixed speed. You never steer and you never slow down. All of your skill lives in when, never in where.
  2. One button jumps. Tap, click, or Space. Hold it and the cube hops again the instant it lands. While it flies, it tumbles — a quarter-turn at a time, snapped square when it lands, the way the original's cube does.
  3. Touching trouble ends the run instantly — a spike anywhere, the side of a block, or a fall out of the world — and you are back at the start before you can blink, with the attempt counter ticked up by one. Landing on a block's top is safe; that one distinction is where level design lives.

Everything else in the studio — the painting, the share codes, the export — exists to serve those three rules. A kid who understands them understands the game, and a kid who builds levels for them is doing real design: every spike they place is a question about timing, and the cube answers it 120 times a second.

The tuned numbers — why the spike timing works

The runner is four constants and one step size. They were chosen together, on paper first, so the jump would have a clean shape in grid squares — and then measured in the shipped simulation, which is where the honest numbers below come from:

ConstantValueWhat it decides
Cell size32 pxOne grid square — the unit everything is measured in
Run speed320 px/s = 10 cells/sHow fast the world comes at you
Gravity3200 px/s²How hard the fall pulls
Jump velocity800 px/s upwardHow hard the launch pushes
Simulation step1/120 s, fixedThe physics never depends on the screen's frame rate
jump height = v² / 2g = 800² / (2 × 3200) = 100 px = 3.125 cells The textbook projectile formula, with the studio's own constants. Air time is 2v/g = 0.5 s, and at 320 px/s that carries the cube exactly 160 px — 5 cells — per jump.

Measured in the shipped simulation, the jump is a hair under the paper numbers, and the lesson prints both because the difference is itself the teaching: stepping time in fixed 1/120 s slices (semi-implicit Euler, the way most small games integrate) gives an apex of 96.67 px = 3.02 cells, an air time of 59 steps = 0.492 s, and a jump distance of exactly 160.0 px = 5.00 cells. The formula designs the game; the simulation is the game; where they differ, the simulation is right and the formula is the map.

Those numbers are why the spike timing works. A spike's killing heart is a small box at its base — 10 px wide, 14 px tall, centred in its cell — so a jump begun two cells before a lone spike sails the cube's underside 65.3 px above that box at the closest pass. One spike is a hop. Two spikes side by side still fit inside a 5-cell jump — the driver cleared them. Three in a row do not: the cube comes down inside the third spike's reach no matter when you leave the ground, and the driver died on the triple exactly as the arithmetic predicts. A level designer who knows "five cells per jump, three spikes is a wall" knows the grammar of every level they will ever build.

A level is just data

Here is the studio's centre, and it is the same secret the Web Page Workshop teaches one door down. A level is a grid of squares, and each square is one character: . for empty, # for a block, ^ for a spike. Read the grid column by column and you have a string; squeeze the runs of identical characters ("eleven dots, a block, eleven dots, a block…") and prefix the width, and the whole level becomes one line — the share code. The starter level, sixty columns of architecture, is 257 characters. David's level fits in a text message.

DB1|60|b.1#b.1#b.1#… The starter level's share code, opening bytes. DB1 names the format, 60 is the width, and each run is a count in base 36 followed by the tile it counts: b. is eleven empty squares, 1# is one block — the ground, arriving column by column.

Because a level is just data, three things that look like magic become bookkeeping. The autosave stores the share code in the browser's localStorage on every painted square. Sharing is copying the code to a friend, whose studio parses the same string back into the identical grid — the proofs below round-trip a painted block and spike through the code and compare the grids cell by cell. And Export writes the code into a complete standalone web page — player included — that plays the level by itself anywhere. The exported page does not contain a copy of the game logic: the studio embeds the source text of its own simulation factory into the file it generates, so the page David emails to Grandma runs literally the same physics as the studio he built the level in. The driver below loaded an exported page on its own, watched it run, and watched it crash on the starter level's first spike — the same death, in the same place, as in the studio.

The proofs — driven, not just read

Before this studio shipped, a test driver loaded the shipped page into a DOM (jsdom, under Node) and used it: it ran the page's own six-proof suite, then drove the editor with real pointer events — picked the Spike tool, painted and dragged on the canvas, erased, stretched the level, loaded a share code through the real Load button — and finally played: pressed Play and stepped the real player until the starter level's first spike killed the run, and loaded an exported page standalone and let it run live. Two stand-ins must be named: jsdom has no canvas 2D renderer, so the driver supplied a no-op drawing context (every proof here is about logic and state, never pixels), and it reported the canvas's layout size so pointer coordinates could land on real cells. Everything else — the simulation, the editor wiring, the serializer, the export — ran exactly as shipped.

The same suite re-runs live on the studio itself, whenever David presses its button.

Honest notes

What this is not: the real Geometry Dash. There is no music (the original's levels are built around songs; these are built around silence and concentration), no icons or ships or saws, no accounts, no online level browser, no stars or demons or ratings. Levels live in this browser, in share codes, and in exported pages — nowhere else, and nothing is uploaded.

The simplifications, named: the cube is one mode only (no ship, no ball, no portals); a spike's hitbox is a small box at its heart rather than the triangle itself, which is the standard forgiving trick and is why a well-timed jump can shave a spike's tip; hitting a block's underside bonks instead of killing; and the physics is a fixed-step semi-implicit Euler integration whose measured jump (96.67 px) sits a hair under the continuous formula (100 px) — printed above, not hidden. None of these change the three rules; all of them are the kind of trade a real game makes and names.
