Raytracer Pipeline Lab — the lesson

Most 3D pictures you see are faked the fast way: triangles are flattened onto the screen and shading tricks guess at the light. A ray tracer does it the honest way — backwards. For every pixel it fires a ray from the camera into the scene and asks, in order: what did this ray hit, is that point in shadow, where does it bounce, and where does it bend? Follow enough bounces and the picture that accumulates is not a guess at the physics of light — it is the physics, run per pixel. This lab is a real one, written in plain JavaScript: primary rays, recursive reflection and refraction, stochastic soft shadows, progressive sampling, and a BVH acceleration tree you can inspect. This page is the other half: the optics the lab runs, and the audit that extracted its shipped code, drove it headlessly, and checked every claim against independent implementations — including the one claim that failed.

Verdict: A real per-pixel ray tracer — intersections exact to about 10⁻¹⁵, Snell refraction and total internal reflection correct, BVH hits identical to brute force — with one permanent-NaN-pixel defect in the studio and sunset skies, and a presentation layer that paints its Cornell "GI," approximates its Fresnel, and proxies its BVH speedup.

The concept

One ray per pixel, then keep asking

The renderer writes into an ImageData buffer, one ray per pixel, built from a camera basis and jittered slightly per sample. Each ray is tested against the scene's spheres, boxes, and floor plane; the nearest hit wins. At the hit point the tracer fires shadow rays toward each light, spawns a reflected ray, and — for glass-like materials — a refracted ray, recursing until the bounce budget runs out. There is no raster or WebGL shortcut anywhere in the render path; the X-Ray and BVH inspector views are 2D projections used only by the inspector panels and never feed the render.

Progressive rendering: the picture converges

One jittered sample per pixel is noisy. So the lab renders in passes — samples per pixel (SPP) — averaging each new sample into an accumulation buffer. Soft shadows come from exactly this: light positions are stochastically jittered per sample, so the penumbra emerges from the accumulation rather than from any blur pass. The SPP counter climbs to a hard cap of 64, and the audit confirmed the cap holds after extra render slices. Convergence is the lab's central promise — which is why the defect in §3 matters: for most pixels the promise holds, and for a few it provably does not.

The BVH: don't test everything against everything

Testing every ray against every primitive is the brute-force way, and it works — slowly. A bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) nests the primitives inside boxes inside bigger boxes, so a ray that misses a big box skips everything inside it in one test. The lab's BVH scene holds 18 primitives, and the inspector lets you watch rays walk the tree. Whether the tree returns the same picture as brute force — and what its speedup readout is actually made of — is audited below.

The concept/math

Intersection: solving for t

sphere: |o + t·d − c|² = r²  ·  box: slab test per axis  ·  nearest t > 0 wins For a sphere the ray equation becomes a quadratic in t; the smallest positive root is the entry point and its gradient is the surface normal. A box is intersected by clipping the ray against three axis-aligned slabs. The floor plane is the degenerate case: in an empty scene the audit's probe returned primIdx = −2, t = 3, normal (0,1,0) — exact.

Reflection, Snell refraction, and the critical angle

r = d − 2(d·n)n  ·  n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂  ·  θc = asin(n₂/n₁) Snell's law sets the bend at a material boundary from the two indices of refraction (IOR): water 1.33, crown glass 1.52, sapphire 1.77, diamond 2.42 — the lab's labels are all correct. Going from dense to rare past the critical angle, no refracted ray exists at all: total internal reflection. For crown glass that angle is 41.14°.

Fresnel: how much reflects vs refracts

exact: R from the Fresnel equations  ·  Schlick: R ≈ R₀ + (1 − R₀)(1 − cos θ)⁵,   R₀ = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))² Both agree head-on: R₀ for glass in air is 4.258%. They part company at grazing angles — and which one the lab actually runs is an audit finding, not a detail (see the bends, below). Dispersion — splitting the refracted ray by wavelength — is also real in the lab, but only on the primary entry ray and only for IOR ≥ 1.85.

Defect: permanent NaN pixels — the picture does not always converge

FALSE — "watch it converge" is not always true. Driving the shipped renderer on the Classic scene (320×200 buffer) produced non-finite values in the accumulation buffer; the final SPP-64 buffer contained 3 non-finite values — one pixel, all channels, permanently dead. The audit isolated the cause by replaying the render deterministically, capturing the exact ray, and tracing its bounce tree:

First poisoned pixel: SPP 19, pixel (148, 118), channel R. The pixel's ray rattles inside the glass sphere to depth 4, and its refracted exit direction comes out of the Snell formula slightly unnormalised: |d| = 1.00008, with dy = 1.0000788 > 1.

The trigger: the studio sky computes Math.pow(1 − Math.max(0, dy), 3.4) → Math.pow(−0.0000788, 3.4) = NaN — a non-integer exponent of a negative base. The sunset sky has the same pattern (Math.pow(1 − h, 4.2)). The obsidian and hallway skies use an integral exponent (5.0) and the Cornell sky is constant — both immune.

Why it never heals: once a NaN enters the accumulation buffer, NaN + x = NaN forever. The pixel renders black after tone mapping no matter how long sampling continues, and the SPP convergence statistic inherits the NaN. The trigger is generic — any reflected, refracted, or jittered ray with dy > 1 reaching a studio or sunset sky — even though the measured frequency was low: 1 pixel in 64,000 at SPP 64 in the test render.

The fix was applied on 25 November 2026, under Steven's sanction (his green light on the whole Bench board): one line at the head of sampleSky clamps dy to [−1, 1] — a range a direction component cannot physically leave, so no valid colour changes. Verified against the shipped function, extracted verbatim: at dy = 1.2 the sunset and studio skies now return finite colours where the original returned NaN in all three channels, and at every in-range dy tested the colours are identical before and after. Hash chain (SHA-256): 47772e8be22a6a15… → 40e2aac2ed35a02e…. Everything else in the exhibit remains byte-for-byte as built.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The Cornell box's "GI" is paint, not bounced light: the red/green colour bleed is a hard-coded analytic tint inside traceRay, keyed to the Cornell sky type with fixed constants depending only on hit x and normal-x, plus an analytic sphere-proximity ambient-occlusion term. No diffuse interreflection is ever traced. It looks like one-bounce global illumination; the mechanism is a tint, and the "Ambient Occlusion & GI" toggle gates both effects together.

"Fresnel" is the Schlick approximation: at 60° incidence the page returns R = 7.25%, which equals Schlick's approximation exactly — but the exact Fresnel equations give 9.25%, so Schlick under-reads by ≈22% relative at that angle. Head-on they agree (4.258%), so the bend only shows at grazing angles. A code comment calls it "Exact Schlick Fresnel" — Schlick is by definition an approximation; the word "Exact" is the bend.

The BVH speedup HUD is a proxy formula, not a measurement: it computes totalPrims / (primTests + nodes×0.22), which evaluates to ≈24.1× averaged over all rays and ≈6.2× over hit rays only — framing-dependent, and inflated relative to the honest 13.79× primitive-test reduction measured above (which is itself not a wall-clock figure). A code comment separately claims "3–5× speedup" for the scene — a third inconsistent number. None of the three is a timed measurement.

Glass shadows are an achromatic fudge, not "colored caustics": measured transmittance through a glass blocker head-on is 0.830 — exactly the page's formula 0.18 + 0.65·|n·d|^2.2. It is a single scalar: no colour, no caustic focusing, despite code-comment language about coloured, partial shadows. (Opaque blocker → 0, no blocker → 1, both correct.)

Smaller print: the BVH preset's copy says "18-orb," but the scene is 16 spheres + 2 boxes. The "Diamond Prism" preset's hero object is a sphere, not a prism. The Mirrors preset's "Infinite 8-bounce tunnel" is hyperbole — maxBounces = 8, and a probe ray chain terminated after 7 segments / 5 hits. The "Throughput M rays/s" telemetry is self-reported from the renderer's own slice statistics and counts all ray types (primary + shadow + secondary); treat it as indicative, not measured.
