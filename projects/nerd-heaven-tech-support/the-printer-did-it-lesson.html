The Printer Did It — the lesson

The Printer Did It is the classic deduction board — a sealed Case File, a house full of suspects, and a notebook — rebuilt on the Tech Bench, where the suspects are machines and the murder weapon is a corrupted driver. Under the jokes it is the same game this room teaches everywhere: Find the Fault at board scale. A suggestion is a test. A pass is data. The notebook is a ledger. This page is the other half of the game: the method, the arithmetic underneath it, one deduction worked in full, and how the game is built. Readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The subject: a whodunit is a diagnosis whose suspects talk back

The Printer is a suspect, not a verdict

Every tech who has ever worked a bench carries a prior: when something in an office dies, it was the printer. The prior is earned — printers have form — and it is exactly the kind of head start this room teaches you to distrust. In this game the Printer is dealt into the same twenty-one cards as everything else: innocent in five cases out of six, guilty in one, and the cards do not care about its reputation. The discipline the game is really teaching is the one from the bench panel: hold the suspects in mind, run the test that discriminates, and let the pattern — not the reputation — close the case. When the Printer is innocent, the game makes a note of it. In triplicate. It insisted.

A suggestion is a test

In Find the Fault, you pay points to run a test and the result divides the suspects still standing. A suggestion here does the same work in a different costume. When you stand in the Kitchen and suggest the Printer, in the Kitchen, by Spilled Coffee, you have built a three-card instrument and pointed it at the table, one player at a time. Each answer divides the world: a player who shows a card has placed that card in a hand — it is not in the Case File. A player who passes has told you something just as hard. And the players who were never asked, because someone earlier in the order showed first, have told you nothing — the game is careful about that distinction, and so is the notebook.

A pass is three facts

Beginners treat a pass as a non-event: nothing happened, move on. The ledger disagrees. A pass on three named cards is three separate facts, each as solid as a shown card: that player does not hold the suspect, does not hold the cause, does not hold the room. Three ✕ marks, bought with one question. This is why the cheapest-looking turns are often the richest: a suggestion nobody can answer has interrogated all three opponents about all three cards, and the answers are all in the notebook. Silence, recorded properly, is evidence.

The notebook is a ledger — and it only writes what the evidence forces

Your notebook on the game page is kept for you, but it is kept honestly: it never writes a hunch. Every ✓, every ✕ and every ★ in it is the conclusion of a rule you could check by hand from the case log — the same discipline as the lab notebook in Find the Fault, which never lets you edit a result after the fact. Human memory quietly revises the past toward the theory it currently likes (“I’m fairly sure Ada showed me the Router…”). The ledger doesn’t. When your notebook puts a ★ in the Case File column, it is not a feeling. It is arithmetic: every other home for that card has been ruled out, in writing, above it.

The math the game runs

The deck is twenty-one cards: 6 suspects, 6 causes, 9 rooms. The Case File takes one of each, so before a card is dealt the case is one of:

6 suspects × 6 causes × 9 rooms = 324 possible cases Measured the way Find the Fault measures doubt, that is log2(324) ≈ 8.34 bits of uncertainty. Every receipt you collect spends some of it. An accusation is a claim that all 8.34 bits are spent.

Your own hand is the first spend, and it is free: five cards you can see are five cards that are not in the file. If your hand happens to hold two suspects, two causes and a room, the case space collapses before the first roll:

(6 − 2) × (6 − 2) × (9 − 1) = 4 × 4 × 8 = 128 cases still standing Every card placed after that — shown to you, deduced, or dealt a ★ — cuts its own type’s factor the same way. The game ends, mathematically, when each factor reaches 1.

One deduction, worked in full

The sharpest rule in the notebook is the one that reads a card nobody showed you. Suppose the case log says:

EventWhat the ledger gets
Linus suggests the Router, in the Attic, by the Midnight Update. Ada shows Linus a card.Ada holds at least one of {Router, Midnight Update, Attic}. Which one is Linus’s receipt, not yours — yet.
Your own handYou hold the Midnight Update. So Ada’s card is not that one.
Two rounds earlier, Grace showed you a card: the Attic.The Attic is in Grace’s hand. So Ada’s card is not that one either.
DeductionAda holds the Router. Two of the three are accounted for; the third has nowhere else to be. The notebook writes the ✓ without ever having seen the card.

That is the whole method in miniature: a show is a receipt, a pass is three receipts, and receipts combine. The second rule of the same kind is pure counting — the file holds exactly one suspect, so the moment five suspects are placed in hands, the sixth is in the file whether or not anyone ever went near it. Neither rule is a guess. Both are the pattern across the gates, drawn on a board instead of a bench.

How it’s built — the coding choices

The Printer Did It is one HTML file with no libraries. It was built by Muse for Everyday Cool and is curated by Steven Powell. Three choices do most of the work.

The core is a pure-function block, so a script can check the game against itself

The cards, the board graph, the deal, the show/pass resolution, the notebook and the houseguests’ brains all live in one sealed block of code, fenced between two marker comments (CORE markers), knowing nothing about the page. The proof that ships with the collection lifts that exact block out of the shipped file and asserts, on the record:

  • Deal integrity — over 500 seeded deals, the Case File always holds exactly one card of each type, all 21 cards are accounted for with no duplicates, and the hands are always 5/5/4/4.
  • Show/pass logic — on scripted hands, the right player shows, the right card is shown, resolution stops at the first shower, and a suggestion nobody can answer produces three passes and nothing else.
  • Inference soundness — across 300 seeded games with 18,000 suggestions resolved, every mark in every player’s notebook was checked against the true deal after every single resolution: a card marked held-by-X was truly held by X, a card marked cannot-be-held truly wasn’t, a ★ truly was in the file. The notebook never once marked a fact that did not follow. That is the game’s central promise — trust the ledger — made literally true of its own code.
  • The case always closes — in 150 full AI-vs-AI games on the shipped rules, every game terminated in a correct accusation, the longest in 33 turns.

The inference rules, stated

The notebook draws conclusions from exactly five rules, and no others: (1) a card shown to you is held by the shower, and therefore by nobody else and not by the file; (2) a pass rules all three named cards out of that player’s hand; (3) a show with two of its three cards ruled out for the shower forces the third into the shower’s hand; (4) a player whose known cards fill their hand holds nothing else — and a player ruled out of every card but their hand’s worth holds all of those; (5) a card nobody can hold is in the Case File, and when every other card of a type is placed, the last one is in the file. Rules are applied until nothing new follows. Anything the rules don’t force stays a blank — the notebook would rather be incomplete than wrong.

The houseguests play with their hands tied — on purpose

Ada, Grace and Linus each keep a private notebook built by the same five rules, fed only with what that player legitimately knows: their own cards, the cards shown to them, the public passes, and the public fact that a show happened. They never see each other’s notebooks and never touch the deal. When suggesting, a houseguest names the cards its own notebook knows least about, in a room it can reach — and when two of the file’s three cards are forced in its notebook, it names a forced card deliberately, because a pass on a card nobody can hold concentrates every answer on the one card it is still hunting. When its notebook forces all three, it accuses. That is not a script pretending to think; it is the same ledger you keep, kept by three other hands.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the board is a graph, not the classic tile floor — nine rooms on a 3×3 grid joined by twelve hallway squares, instead of a 25×25 mansion you cross square by square. Entering a room ends a move, rooms are never passed through, and a corner room takes a second move (or a secret passage) to reach. Squares hold any number of tokens; the classic hallway holds one detective and makes traffic jams a tactic, which this board declines to model. You may stay in a room and suggest there again next turn; the classic game makes you leave and earn your way back. There is one die, not two, and it is honestly random — the die owes you nothing and the notebook says so. The AI houseguests, when they must show a card, show their first matching one; a human shower chooses, and a sharp human’s choice can itself be a tell — the notebook deliberately draws no conclusion from which of two cards you chose to show. Late in a long game, a houseguest whose notebook has two cards forced and the third type down to exactly two candidates may take a stated coin-flip accusation (never before turn 48, and never when it is the last detective standing); the case log labels it a gamble when it happens. In the 150 proof games the deduction engine closed every case before the coin ever came out — the valve exists, and the receipts say it wasn’t needed. Eliminated detectives keep showing cards, as in the classic game. And the suspects are machines: the named suspect’s token is dragged to the room to answer for itself, but no detective is ever dragged — Ada’s dignity is intact, which is more than the Printer’s can say.
