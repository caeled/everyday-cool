🗄️ Storage Tiers: Fast, Cheap, or Patient — Pick Two

Not all data is in a hurry. The file you’re editing right now needs to be instant. Last year’s tax records just need to exist. So back rooms sort storage into tiers:

📖 The Lesson

Servers vs. storage — the cook and the pantry

A server is a computer that does work for other computers: it runs the website, checks the password, sends the email. Storage is where the stuff sits while it waits to be needed. Think of a restaurant: the server is the cook, storage is the pantry. A great cook with an empty pantry serves nothing — and a full pantry with no cook is just a room full of potatoes. Real systems need both, and the RAID explorer above is all about the pantry: how do we arrange the shelves so a dropped jar doesn’t ruin dinner?

What RAID actually buys you

Striping (RAID 0) spreads one file across disks for speed — but lose any disk and the file is confetti. Mirroring (RAID 1) keeps a complete copy of everything on every disk: in our four-disk setup you keep only one disk's worth of space, but a disk can die at lunch and nobody notices. Parity (RAID 5) is the clever middle: it stores a little math on each disk — enough to rebuild a missing disk’s contents, the way you can solve for one missing number if you know the total. RAID 10 does both tricks at once and charges you half your space for the privilege.

RAID is not a backup. RAID protects you from a disk dying. It does nothing at all if you delete the file (RAID faithfully deletes it from every disk), if ransomware scrambles it, or if the whole room floods. A backup is a separate copy, somewhere else, from an earlier time. RAID keeps the shop open today; backups let you rewind to yesterday. Grown-up systems have both.

Why cold storage exists: the rulebook

Some industries are legally required to keep records for years — hospitals keep medical records, banks keep transaction histories, schools and governments keep archives, sometimes for a decade or longer. That’s called a retention rule, and it’s exactly why cold tiers exist: you may almost never read those records, but you must be able to, and paying hot-SSD prices to store ten years of them would be silly. Slow and cheap isn’t a flaw there — it’s the design.
