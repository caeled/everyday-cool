Pack 002 — The Network Card

A plain DOS machine, a network card fitted, and nothing loaded — your job is to bring up TCP/IP the way it was done: packet driver first, then the configuration, then the stack, then the test, one layer at a time. This pack also changes the pack format, at Steven's word: instead of downloading the drive and mounting it yourself, the workshop page puts the machine and the walkthrough on the same page, side by side — read the step, type it at the real prompt, read what the machine answers. The drive below is the same one the workshop boots, for anyone who wants it on their own bench.

The setup — clean, on purpose

Pack 001 was a diagnosis: one fault, planted. This one is a setup, and Steven's call was to build it clean — a correct machine, assembled in the open, so the student's first hour is the bring-up itself. Faults get their own pack later. What is genuinely hard here is not a hidden break; it is the layering. Every program in the chain tells the truth, and the skill is hearing which layer is talking — a skill this console teaches honestly, because its one real limitation (no network card in the emulation) makes each layer speak in turn.

Where the pieces came from

The drive is modelled on Steven's own working DOSBox C: — his words for it were “the basic C I would build.” His reference startup files came with the commission: a CONFIG.SYS with FILES=30, BUFFERS=30, HIMEM and SMARTDRV out of C:\WINDOWS, and an AUTOEXEC.BAT that loads the mouse and ends with win — a Windows-booting machine. The pack's machine is its DOS-first sibling: same economy, pointed at the network instead. The components are the ones Steven fetched for this pack, from the places techs always fetched them:

What the audit drove

The pack was assembled, booted in this console, and worked end to end before the walkthrough was written — the walkthrough quotes these runs, not the manuals. The drive boots to C:\NET>, its AUTOEXEC having set the path and the stack's config pointer and landed in the NET folder; DIR lists the drive's 27 files, sizes matching the assembled bytes. Then the layers, each in its own words:

  • The driver: PNPPD 0x60 prints its 1995 Realtek banner and then, verbatim, “There is no 8019 PnP card on your system !” — captured from the shipped drive. It does not hang and it does not pretend; it probed, found nothing, and said so.
  • The interrogator: PKTTOOL SCAN answers “No packet drivers found - did you load one?” — two witnesses, one story: nothing resident at any interrupt.
  • The stack: DHCP prints “Init: Could not setup packet driver, are the configured interrupts correct? Configured packet driver interrupt: 0x60 — Could not initialize TCP/IP stack.” Read off the console screen — and worth a footnote of its own: the mTCP programs write these complaints to the screen device, not to redirected output. Capture the session to a file and you get the banner only; the diagnosis is on the glass. Period behaviour, measured, and exactly what a student at the real keyboard will see.
  • The test: PING 1.1.1.1 answers with one line — “mTCP: 'IPADDR' must be set.” The address layer, reporting that there is nothing beneath it.
  • The page itself: the shipped workshop page was driven the same way — it boots the pack from its own download, reproduces the driver and scan lines exactly, keeps the stage ticks between visits, and threw zero page errors across every drive.

Steven's Template C: — the starting drive itself

After the pack shipped, Steven sent over the reference itself: the actual C: he builds from, zipped whole — MS-DOS and all. It is uploaded here as a template, in the same spirit as the consoles across the room: the shape and the handwriting ship — and, at Steven's word the same evening, the DOS layer is not a slot at all: it is a working FreeDOS 1.4 base, his own field-tested route (his report: he got most things, even Windows, to work with FreeDOS). Windows remains a slot you fill from your own media. What is his is here verbatim — the root AUTOEXEC.BAT and CONFIG.SYS, the CD-ROM configuration in DOS\ with its banana driver and its rem'd-out port-and-IRQ hunting lines, C.BAT (his directory-spinner: it lists the whole drive, forever — run it when you mean it), and the WINSETUP startup pair, whose first PATH entry is C:\MSBOB. His Windows machine had a Bob layer; the template makes no apology for this. The NET folder rides in working form, and inside it is the form of this lesson Steven asked for alongside the web walkthrough: NETWALK.BAT, the whole bring-up as a narrated batch file that runs the real commands one at a time — CD \NET, then NETWALK, on this template, in Pack 002's drive (it rides there too), or on a real machine.

The template was audited the same way as the pack: booted in this console, it answers with three Illegal command lines — SMARTDRV, MOUSE, WIN — the empty slots announcing themselves, exactly as its README says it will, and lands at a working C:\ prompt; NETWALK then runs end to end, and its narration matches the screen at every stage. The FreeDOS programs were run, not just listed: MEM printed its memory table and CuteMouse installed itself at the PS/2 port, and DIR shows KERNEL.SYS and FreeCom's COMMAND.COM in place. A boot from the FreeDOS kernel itself is the one thing this console cannot prove — a folder mount uses the emulator's shell whoever's DOS sits in the folder — and the template's README says so on its face. (The audit caught one real fault on the way: the walkthrough's first run in the template met PING complaining that MTCPCFG was unset — Steven's AUTOEXEC, unlike the pack's, never sets it. NETWALK now sets the variable itself, the way a careful tech would, and says so in its own comments.)

The layers — and the one that was added later

Card, driver, configuration, stack, address: five layers, and every network fault you will ever meet lives in one of them. Steven, who set this up under Windows when it was new, stated the modern corollary while this pack was being built, and it closes the lesson better than the lesson could:

“There is so much you learned setting that up and getting it to work especially under windows that applies even today. It is amazing how much still applies. Its really the same issues wearing different cloths. The same things pop up be it local, a workgroup. Modern computers networks just have one more layer. The network itself and the control daemons that run them.”

He is right, and this pack contains the proof in miniature: the DHCP client in stage 6 is a supplicant to one of those daemons. In 1993 you typed the address yourself and a wrong one was your own fault, local and diagnosable. Now the machine asks the network who it is, and believes the answer — and when the daemon lies or dies, every correctly-built machine is wrong together, in the same way, for a reason that lives in none of them. That unanimous failure is the genuinely new thing. Everything beneath it is this workshop, unchanged.

The Windows layer

The drive carries the start of it in TRUMPET.TXT: packet driver, then WINPKT, then Trumpet's TCPMAN providing WINSOCK.DLL — and Steven's field warning, in his words: “Trumpet winsock was a doozy great, but things got confused if you had winsock.dll in a couple of places.” Windows loads the first copy it finds, not the right one; the cure was never a settings screen but a census — find every copy, make there be one. It is the configuration-agreement lesson of stage 5 wearing its other cloth: the system obeys the first thing in line, so know what's in line.

The honest limits. This console's emulation fits no network card, so stages 3, 6 and 7 end at the wall the driver names — the workshop teaches up to it and says so, rather than faking a success screen. The same drive in DOSBox-X (which fits an NE2000) or on real hardware completes live: driver resident, DHCP lease written into MTCP.CFG, PING answered. Everything shipped in the pack is free software or our own writing; Trumpet is pointed at, not carried. The audit's keystrokes were injected by script, so the human receipt — a person reading the walkthrough and working the stages at their own keyboard — is, as always, the one that finally counts.

These packs are shareware, in the old sense: if one teaches you something and you want to keep them coming, the tip jar is ko-fi.com/caeled. The same note rides inside every pack as REGISTER.TXT; it is not mentioned anywhere else.

Verdict

Ship it. A real driver, a real stack, a real configuration — assembled in the open, every message earned. Work the pack.
