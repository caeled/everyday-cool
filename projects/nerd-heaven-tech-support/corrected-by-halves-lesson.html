Corrected by Halves — the lesson

Corrected by Halves is ten ops built on one idea: don’t try to get it all done in one move; head the general direction, and correct by halves. The original five — a cable-bus fault hunt, a checksum tree, a TCP congestion game, a Hamming parity bank, and a bisection oscilloscope — were joined, in the Educational Arcade Edition, by five more at Steven Powell’s direction: a Git bisect commit hunt, a quadtree radar, an exponential-backoff arena, a subnet slicer, and a timed Arcade Shift that runs the others as a boss rush. The game was built by Gemini Neon (Google Antigravity) at Steven Powell’s direction, and it is integrated into this room unchanged — the file on this bench is byte-for-byte the file that was delivered. This page is the collection’s half of the bargain: an audit. Everything below was checked against the game’s own code — much of it by playing the shipped file headlessly, through its own buttons and canvas, the rest by extracting its functions verbatim and simulating its bracket rules — and where the game’s in-page Field Guide says more than its code does, the last section of this page says exactly that, in the open.

The one idea in ten costumes

The curator of this room has a stated method for problems, and it is not “follow the map precisely.” It is: head the general direction of the problem, see what looks right and what doesn’t, notice what applies here that might apply there, correct constantly — and you get there by halves instead of full moves. Go Thataway, over in the Map Room, is that philosophy as a walking game: pace and bearing, drift and fix, arrival by successive correction. Corrected by Halves is the same philosophy after it puts on a lab coat. Each of its games hands you a bracket — a span of possibilities with the answer somewhere inside — and one move: split it, ask which half, throw the wrong half away.

The unit of that move is the bit. If the answer is equally likely to be any of N possibilities, your uncertainty is log2(N) bits, and a test that cleanly halves the possibilities earns exactly 1.00 of them. That is the whole economy of the game:

uncertainty = log2(possibilities)  ·  one clean halving = 1 bit 1,024 relays hold log2(1024) = 10 bits of uncertainty, so ten good halvings — never eleven, never fifty — close the case. Every game below is that sentence wearing a different costume: the halves are taken in a cable, in a hash tree, in a send rate, in memory addresses, in a number line — and, in the Arcade Edition, in a commit history, in a map, in a contention window, and in an address block.

Keep one distinction in your pocket for the whole page. Some of the games measure the bits — they compute log2(old span ÷ new span) after every move and will honestly show you a wasteful 0.15-bit probe. Others print “+1.00b” as a scored label for a good move. The receipts section of each game below says which is which.

1 · Line Fault — binary search on a cable bus

The real-world method. A signal trunk has one dead relay among N. You can tap any relay and learn one of three things: CLEAN (signal present — the fault is downstream), DEAD (no signal — the fault is upstream), or a direct hit. That is the oldest move in field troubleshooting — split the run, test the middle, keep the guilty half — and it is binary search with a multimeter. The game’s own probe messages say it in this room’s language: “Relay #512 is CLEAN → Fault is THATAWAY →.”

The math. The game computes par exactly the way the Field Guide prints it: par = ⌈log₂ N⌉. The tiers are 32, 128, and 1,024 relays, so par is 5, 7, and 10, and the probe budget is par + 3 (8, 10, 13). Nothing forces you to tap the midpoint — you may tap anywhere, and the game measures what your tap actually earned: gain = log₂(old span ÷ new span). A midpoint tap earns its 1.00 bit; a timid tap near an end earns a fraction and spends the same budget.

The receipts. The shipped file was played headlessly through its own number input and Probe button, with the fault planted at every position in turn — all 32, all 128, and all 1,024. Midpoint play isolated every fault, and the worst case was exactly par at all three tiers: 5 of 5, 7 of 7, 10 of 10. The Field Guide’s claim — “even 1,024 stations takes at most 10 probes” — is not a slogan; it is the measured worst case. (A sequential scan from relay #1 averages ~512 checks on the big trunk. Halving is not a little faster; it is a different kind of fast.) One grace note: you can also win by pure elimination — when the bracket narrows to a single relay, the game ends even if you never tapped that relay directly.

2 · Checksum Tree — CRC-8, bisected

The real-world method. A frame of 16 or 32 bytes arrived with one flipped bit (two, on the top tier). Re-checking every byte is the linear scan; checking ranges is the halving. The game builds a binary tree over the frame — 31 nodes for 16 leaves — where each node holds the CRC-8 of its byte range. Query a node: if its checksum matches the expected value, the whole subtree is innocent and locks out; if it mismatches, drill into it. When one half comes back clean under a parent already known dirty, the game deduces the sibling is dirty for free — and on the single-fault tiers, a dirty result clears the sibling subtree outright. At the leaf, you flip the one flipped bit back, and the repair is verified twice: the leaf’s CRC must match, and at the end every byte must equal the original frame.

The math, verified. The checksum is a genuine CRC-8 — not a toy hash wearing the name. The code’s crc8() is the bitwise algorithm with polynomial 0x07 (x⁸ + x² + x + 1) and initial value 0x00: the CRC-8/SMBUS (ATM HEC) polynomial. Run the game’s own function over the standard check string "123456789" and it returns 0xF4 — the published CRC-8/SMBUS check value. A CRC with more than one term detects every single-bit error, which is why the hunt can never be fooled by the one fault it plants; the generator still re-rolls (up to 50 attempts) until the corrupted frame’s root checksum differs from the original’s, which is the check that matters on the two-fault tier, where the two faults are deliberately planted in opposite halves so the root is the only node holding both.

The receipts. Playing the shipped tree through its canvas: a perfect descent — one query per level — wins tier 1 at 4 queries against par 4 and tier 2 at 5 against par 5. The descent is not the whole cost, though: opening a leaf is itself a query, so when the first leaf you open is the corrupt byte’s clean twin, the same flawless descent finishes at 5 against par 4. On tier 3, the fully-guaranteed line costs 8 against a par of 7; par is reachable when one fault is opened directly from its quarter instead of probed down to the pair. The star thresholds (full marks at par, a grace band of two more) quietly absorb the difference — but par here is a best-case line, not a guarantee, and this page would rather say so.

3 · TCP Halver — AIMD, with you as the control loop

The real-world method. TCP congestion control is halving applied to a rate. A sender keeps a congestion window (cwnd) — how much it will put on the wire per round trip. It probes upward while the path delivers, and when packets drop it concludes it has found the ceiling and backs off hard: additive increase going up, multiplicative decrease coming down — AIMD. The game turns that into bracket-keeping against a hidden capacity. Your window starts at 4. A round at or under capacity delivers (and pays a 1.35× bonus at 90% utilization or better — a game rule, not a TCP one); a round over capacity drops, pays only a fraction of capacity (25%, or 15% on the satellite tier), and tightens your bracket: the highest clean window and the lowest dropping window become the walls. The signature move is the emerald ½ BISECT button: once both walls exist, jump straight to the bracket’s midpoint. On the higher tiers the capacity itself re-rolls mid-transfer — the “route shift” — and the bracket resets, because the fact you corrected toward is gone.

The math. The tiers are a 128-packet link with a 1,200 KB quota in 42 seconds, a 160-packet backbone (1,750 KB, 48 s, two shifts), and a 256-packet satellite link (2,600 KB, 52 s, two shifts, harsher drop penalty). Rounds tick every 0.62 seconds; capacity is a single hidden integer rolled between 28% and 84% of the tier maximum. Doubling from 4 reaches any capacity in ⌈log₂(cap ÷ 4)⌉ rounds — that is the ×2 button’s job — and after the first drop, each bisection halves the bracket’s width, so the uncertainty about capacity falls by a bit per correction, exactly as it does on the cable. A worked line: clean at 64 (lower wall 64), drop at 128 (upper wall 128), BISECT to 96 — you are now hovering in the zone where the quota fills fastest and drops stay rare, which is precisely what the star thresholds reward (full marks: finish with ≥ 14 s to spare and ≤ 5 drops).

The receipts. This is the game where the Field Guide’s voice and the code part company, and the honest box below has the details. The short version: in this game nothing is automatic. The window never grows on its own between rounds, and a drop never halves it — the log urges “Hit ÷2 HALVE!” and waits for your finger. That is not a defect; it is the design. The game makes you be the control loop, and it grades what a control loop is graded on: quota delivered before the SLA timer, drops kept down. One correction to record in the game’s favour: the expanded edition’s Field Guide now prints the true step sizes — “×2 Slow Start and +2/+8” — where the first edition’s guide quoted a “+1/+4” that matched nothing on the deck. The audit’s sharpest complaint about v1 was fixed at the source; the honest box below carries the amended entry.

4 · Parity Matrix — Hamming’s halving, done in space

The real-world method. Line Fault halves in time: one test, half the world gone, repeat. Hamming codes halve in space: store several parity bits, each covering exactly half the bank, arranged so that the pattern of failing checks is the address of the bad bit, written in binary. This is the error-correcting code inside ECC memory. The game’s banks are textbook Hamming layouts: positions 1–15 (tiers 1–2) or 1–31 (tier 3), with parity bits at positions 1, 2, 4, 8 (and 16), data in every other position, even parity throughout — plus one overall-parity cell at position 0, which makes the physical banks 16 and 32 cells: extended Hamming, the (16, 11) and (32, 26) codes.

The math. Check Pp XORs every position whose index has bit p set, including the parity bit itself. A single flipped bit at position e disturbs exactly the checks whose bit appears in e’s binary form — so the syndrome, the sum of the failing masks, equals e. The game’s own telemetry example is the whole trick: P8 + P2 + P1 failing means cell #11, because 8 + 2 + 1 = 11 = 1011₂. Each check contributes exactly one bit of the address, which is why the Field Guide’s claim holds precisely: 4 checks pinpoint 1 bit in 15; 5 checks pinpoint 1 bit in 31.

The receipts. Every position was planted and played against the shipped code: all 15 positions in the small bank and all 31 in the large one — in every trial the printed syndrome equalled the planted position exactly, and clicking that cell repaired the block. Tier 2 removes the training wheels: the syndrome bits are shown but not summed for you, and the candidate highlighting is off — you add the failing masks yourself. Checks are free and unlimited (there is even a Check All button); the rationed resource is wrong clicks — four mis-flips across the whole tier ends it. The game is not testing whether you can ration questions. It is testing whether you can read the address the halves spell.

5 · Zero Crossing — Bolzano’s bisection

The real-world method. A hidden curve crosses zero somewhere in [a, b], with f(a) < 0 < f(b). Bolzano’s theorem — the intermediate value theorem — is the guarantee: a continuous curve cannot get from negative to positive without crossing. Sample the midpoint, keep the half whose ends still straddle zero, and the bracket halves every probe, forever, with no assumptions about the curve’s shape. The game’s oscilloscope zooms as the bracket narrows, and its par is the honest count: par = ⌈log₂(span ÷ 2·tol)⌉ — 8 probes to pin a root in [0, 100] to ±0.25, 10 for ±0.05 on the cliff tier, 15 for ±0.02 across [0, 1000].

The math, verified. The hidden curves are built from a hyperbolic tangent centred on the root — tiers 1 and 3 add a small sine wobble that fades to zero at the root itself; the cliff tier uses a steep sigmoid (tanh compressed ~14×) plus a gentle linear tilt. The audit extracted the exact function from the shipped file and tested it on a 2,001-point grid across 105 combinations of tier, root position, and wobble phase: zero sign violations — f(x) is negative everywhere left of the root and positive everywhere right of it, and f(a) < 0 < f(b) always. That sign discipline, not the curve’s smoothness, is what bisection feeds on, and it is perfect here. Played through the oscilloscope, midpoint probing locked the root at or under par on every tier tried — 8/8, 10/10 on the cliff, 15/15 and 14/15 on the micro-zoom.

The receipts — and the cliff. At deal time the game genuinely simulates a naive secant solver — the giant-jump extrapolator — from the bracket endpoints, capped at 40 steps, and the debrief quotes its real step count. Run verbatim against the cliff, that solver hit its 40-step cap (the debrief’s “40+ (diverged!)”) for roots at 20% and 35% of the span, needed 36 steps for a root at 80%, 7 at 65% — and once, for a root dead centre at 50%, landed in a single step, because a symmetric cliff makes the first giant jump a perfect one. The Field Guide says the cliffs “fool giant-jump extrapolators.” Measured fairly: they fool them often, sometimes for the full 40 steps — and occasionally the giant jump gets lucky. Bisection’s promise is duller and better: it never gets lucky and never needs to.

6 · Git Bisect — the commit hunter

The real-world method. git bisect is binary search pointed at history. You mark one commit known GOOD and one known BAD, test the commit in the middle, and whichever way it answers, half the suspects walk free. The game deals a line of commits — c0 is a release, labelled known GOOD; HEAD is the prod alert, known BAD — with exactly one first-bad commit planted between them. Test any suspect: CI PASS moves the good wall up, CI FAIL moves the bad wall down, and the case closes when the walls meet — the commit standing between them is the regression. Some commits are unbuildable: testing one returns SKIP, the walls don’t move, and the game’s log gives the working tech’s advice — “Try adjacent commit.”

The math. Par is the same honest count as the cable: par = ⌈log₂(N − 1)⌉ — 5 runs for 32 commits, 6 for 64, 8 for 256. The tiers add two pressures: a CI budget (8, 11, and 12 runs) that counts every test including skips, and, on the upper tiers, 8 and then 18 planted SKIP commits (never at the ends, never on the culprit itself). Information gain is measured here, as on the cable — log2 of the bracket shrink after a good or bad answer. A skip logs +0.05b, which is a label, not a measurement: a skip teaches you a commit can’t be built and buys no ground. The star grade quietly forgives skips — it counts effective runs (runs minus skips) against par — but the budget does not forgive them, and that asymmetry is the game.

The receipts. The audit simulated the shipped bracket rules with a midpoint player who steps aside on skips, exactly as the game advises. On the clean tier, across every one of the 31 possible culprit positions, the worst case was exactly par — 5 runs — and every game was won. On the skip tiers the picture changes honestly: over 1,575 simulated games on the 64-commit tier and 1,530 on the 256-commit tier, with skip placements re-rolled each game, the same player won 87% and 92% of the time; the worst effective runs were par + 1 (7 and 9), and the losses were budget losses — when unbuildable commits cluster near the middle of the bracket, the runs run out before the walls meet. That is not the method failing; it is the method’s one true cost, and real git bisect pays it too — a skip spends a test and buys nothing, and only your patience, not an algorithm, covers the difference. The debrief’s comparison is real arithmetic: walking backwards from HEAD would have cost one CI build per commit to the culprit, and the telemetry’s claim that a 10,000-commit repo needs 14 runs checks out (⌈log2 10000⌉ = 14).

7 · Quadtree Radar — halving in two dimensions

The real-world method. A beacon is hidden on a square grid — 16×16, 64×64, or 256×256 tiles. Drop a radar ping anywhere and the answer is a compass direction: the beacon is north-east of your ping, due west, dead on. Everything on the wrong side of the ping’s row and column is eliminated at once. This is the search pattern a quadtree — the spatial index inside map engines and game worlds, which subdivides a square into four quadrants, recursively — exists to accelerate: quarter the world, keep the guilty quarter, repeat.

The math. Each axis needs log2(S) halvings, and a centred ping performs both axes’ halvings in a single drop, so par is log₂ S — 4 pings for 256 tiles, 6 for 4,096, 8 for 65,536. The bits here are measured, not labelled: every ping’s gain is computed as log2(old area ÷ new area), and a centred ping earns very close to the ideal 2.00. The Field Guide’s headline — “65,536 tiles in 8 drops” — was put to the exhaustive test: every target on the 16×16 grid (256 targets) and every target on the 64×64 grid (4,096 targets) was hunted with centred play, and the worst case was exactly par, 4 and 6; on the 256×256 ocean, 3,000 sampled targets never needed more than 8 pings. Two precisions the lesson owes you. First, the oracle is stronger than a quadrant test: a ping that lands on the beacon’s exact row or column collapses that whole axis to a line in one drop, which no quadrant membership answer would tell you. Second, no tree is ever built — the game’s entire state is one bounding box and the direction answers. What’s simulated is the search a quadtree makes fast, played as bisection in two dimensions; the tree itself is the data structure you’d build when the points have to be stored, not just hunted.

8 · Backoff Arena — halving the crowd

The real-world method. A shared channel, sixteen slots per round, and a crowd of stations all trying to talk. In each slot, every active node transmits with probability p = 1/CW — the contention window — and a slot delivers only when exactly one node picks it: two or more is a collision, none is idle air. Traffic arrives in twelve scripted waves (2 to 32 nodes; on the top tier the count is hidden and you read it off the slots). Between rounds you steer the one dial there is: CW, by ÷2 or ×2 only, between 1 and 64, starting at 4. Collisions everywhere? Double the window and spread the crowd out. Slots going idle? Halve it and let them talk.

The math. Expected deliveries per slot are n · p · (1 − p)n−1, which peaks when p ≈ 1/n — that is, when CW ≈ the number of active nodes — and the peak rate tends to 1/e ≈ 36.8% of slots: slotted ALOHA’s famous ceiling, the same law the debrief quotes. The audit verified the peak against the mode’s own slot model, exactly: at 16 nodes, CW = 16 delivers an expected 6.08 packets per 16-slot frame, against 4.32 at CW = 8 and 4.97 at CW = 32 — the matched window wins, and it isn’t close. (At 16 nodes the model’s true peak is 38.0% of slots; 36.8% is the limit as the crowd grows, which is the number the debrief prints.) The quotas — 56, 54, and 52 delivered packets across 192 slots — sit at roughly three-quarters of what perfect window-keeping earns, so the game is won by tracking the waves, not by luck. One telemetry honesty note: the spark chart’s “CW Match Efficiency” is a grade computed from a formula (1 − log2(ratio) × 0.35), not a measurement — the deliveries themselves are counted honestly, and the grade is labelled as what it is.

The family resemblance — and the difference. This is TCP Halver’s mirror. Both are multiplicative feedback: probe, collide with reality, correct by a factor. But the TCP game corrects your rate against your drops, while the Arena corrects a shared dial against everyone’s collisions and idles — and the correction direction flips with the evidence: ×2 for collisions, ÷2 for silence. Real binary exponential backoff, in Ethernet and Wi-Fi, is per-station and automatic — each station doubles its own window after each collision, draws a random waiting slot inside it, and resets to the minimum after a success. The game keeps one global window, no per-station memory, and no random draw beyond the transmit rolls themselves — and, in this room’s house style, nothing moves until your finger moves it. What survives the simplification is the law the game exists to teach: match the window to the crowd, in powers of two.

9 · Subnet Slicer — the address block, bisected

The real-world method. VLSM — variable-length subnet masking — is halving with an address budget. You hold one block, 10.0.0.0/24: 256 addresses. Departments arrive with host counts, and each must receive the smallest power-of-two block that fits it. The tool is the slice: any free block can be cut exactly in half, prefix +1, and — the buddy system’s saving grace — two free sibling halves can be merged back when you carve in the wrong place. Readers of Route It, over in the Packet Playground, have already sorted mail across this exact 256-address window, traps and all; the Slicer is the same window turned from a sorting test into a construction project.

The math. A block of prefix p holds 2(32 − p) addresses, of which 2(32 − p) − 2 are usable — the first is the network address, the last the broadcast, and the −2 is in the shipped code everywhere a count is printed:

usable = 2(32 − prefix) − 2 /25 = 126 · /26 = 62 · /27 = 30 · /28 = 14 · /29 = 6 · /30 = 2 — the game’s own ruler, verified against its code. The minimum block for h hosts is the smallest power of two whose usable count covers h; the audit ran the game’s own minSizeForHosts() over every host count from 1 to 254 and confirmed it returns exactly that minimum, every time.

The receipts. The department lists are dealt so that an exact, zero-waste plan always exists — and the audit proved it by carving each tier largest-first under the game’s own slice rules: the small office needs 64 + 32 + 16 = 112 of 256; the campus needs 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 4 = 244; the data centre needs 64 + 64 + 32 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 = 220. Every requirement lands in its exact minimum block with room to spare. What the game grades, then, is not survival — there is no way to lose the Slicer — but waste: finish with zero wasted addresses for three stars, with all-but-one department exact for two. Over-allocating a department is allowed (the deck warns you it fits but isn’t tight), and a block too small is refused outright. Two boundaries worth naming: slicing stops at /30, so the 2-host WAN link gets its exact /30 — the classic rule; point-to-point /31 links (RFC 3021, which spend the network and broadcast addresses instead of reserving them) live outside this model. And the telemetry’s “1.00 bit per slice” label is one of the honest ones: each slice literally is one more bit of subnet mask.

10 · Arcade Shift — the boss rush

The real-world method. There isn’t one — this op is the other nine distilled into reflexes. The Shift cycles five quick ops in a fixed rotation — Quick Line Fault, Quick Quadtree, Quick Hamming Syndrome, Quick CIDR Prefix, Quick Git Bisect, then back to the top — each stripped to its essential move. The fault and radar stages grow (32 → 64 relays, 16×16 → 32×32) once you’ve cleared a handful. The Hamming stage is the purest of the set: the syndrome is printed as P8 P4 P2 P1 and you get one decision — click the cell it spells. The CIDR stage is six buttons and one tightest prefix. The Git stage is a 16-commit bracket and your clicking finger.

The math. The clock is the budget: 90, 60, or 45 seconds by tier, and every clear buys time back — +8, +6, or +5 seconds, with the clock capped at the tier’s start time plus 15. A clear scores (150, plus 75 if it was perfect — inside par, or the one-click decode) × your combo multiplier; the combo climbs one step per consecutive clear to ×5, and a miss resets it to ×1 and takes 4 seconds off the clock. Letter grades land at 800 (B), 1,500 (A), and 2,400 (S) points. Fluency is the whole test: the Shift measures whether the halving moves from the other nine ops have become the kind of thing your hands know.

The receipts. The leaderboard is exactly what the Field Guide calls it — local: a three-letter operator tag and the top five scores, kept in the browser’s local storage alongside the career, with your best shift score pinned in the career panel. Nothing leaves the machine. And a bookkeeping precision: the Arcade Shift earns no career stars — the code says so outright, skipping the star recording for this mode, and its debrief shows 🏆 SHIFT COMPLETE where the other ops show stars. Its score feeds the leaderboard and the shift-best line instead. The 81 stars on the career header belong entirely to the nine campaign ops.

How it’s built — the architecture as found

Corrected by Halves is a single HTML file — 185,792 bytes in this expanded edition, one script block, no libraries, and no external references of any kind: no images, fonts, stylesheets, or scripts are fetched, and the only link in the file points back to this room’s index page. It would run off a USB stick in a Faraday cage. The sound effects are synthesized live with Web Audio oscillators — little oscillator chirps, slides, and a four-note win arpeggio — so there are no audio files either.

Ten modules, one router

The script is one strict-mode closure. Inside it, each op is its own module object with the same six-part interface — title, sub, init, draw, onClick, onKey — and a router (MODE_MAP + startMode) swaps the active module and tier. All ten ops draw onto one shared 860×470 canvas (scaled for high-DPI displays, capped at 2×) that is repainted every animation frame; each module keeps its entire game state in one closure object, so a restart is just a fresh init. The shared furniture — telemetry panel, diagnostic log, spark chart of per-probe gains, debrief overlay — is written by helper functions every module calls, which is why ten quite different games report their progress in the same voice.

The career, and what the aids cost

Progress persists in the browser’s local storage under a new key, corrected_by_halves_arcade_v2 — new, note, not migrated: the expanded edition starts a fresh career rather than reading the first edition’s. What it keeps is an ops score, a star map, a five-entry local leaderboard, and a best Arcade Shift score. The star total on the game’s header is real arithmetic — 9 campaign ops × 3 tiers × 3 stars = 81, with the Arcade Shift carrying no stars — and the operator ranks step up at 6 (Field Tech), 16 (Signal Ops), 30 (Bit Surgeon), 48 (Packet Grandmaster, a new rank in this edition), and 68 (Binary Oracle) stars above the starting Apprentice. Stars keep your best result per tier; the score simply accumulates on every win, so it is a lifetime total rather than a best-run record. Two training aids carry a price in the other direction: Line Fault’s ruler and Zero Crossing’s midpoint guide each pay a ×1.25 score bonus when played switched off — the game pays you to take the training wheels off. And a note for auditors: this lesson’s receipts were produced by loading the shipped file headlessly and playing it through its own wiring — its Probe button, its canvas clicks, its Check All button — with planted faults, by extracting functions like crc8() and minSizeForHosts() verbatim from the shipped script and running them against the published values, and by simulating the newer modes’ own bracket rules over thousands of dealt games. The game graded the audit; the audit just held the pencil.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: this game was built by Gemini Neon (Google Antigravity) at Steven Powell’s direction and is integrated unchanged — the original five ops, and the expanded Educational Arcade Edition that added five more, alike. Its in-page Field Guide is its own voice — enthusiastic, and mostly accurate. This box is where the collection’s audit differs from that voice, precisely and without mockery: Gemini built a real thing, and the job here is to say exactly what it is.

TCP’s automation is you. Congestion control, in the Field Guide’s telling, “ramps up your send rate until packets drop, then cuts the window in half.” In the code, nothing ramps and nothing cuts on its own: every change to the window is a button press (the audit verified that each cwnd assignment in the shipped script sits inside a button handler), and after a drop the game logs “Hit ÷2 HALVE!” and waits for your finger. One v1 entry is hereby amended: the first edition’s guide quoted a “+1/+4 Additive Increase” that matched nothing on the deck — the expanded edition’s guide prints the true +2 and +8, and the correction is the game’s own. What still matches neither protocol named: real TCP Reno adds roughly one segment per round trip and halves its threshold on loss, while CUBIC grows on a cubic time curve and backs off by ×0.7, not ÷2. The ½ BISECT button, the 1.35× peak-utilization bonus, the fraction-of-capacity paid on drop rounds, and a link capacity that re-rolls mid-transfer are likewise game rules wearing TCP’s clothes. As a simulator of TCP’s arithmetic it is a simplification; as a game about being the control loop, it is the honest article.

There is no seed. The header offers “↻ New Seed” and the debrief offers “↻ Replay Seed,” but the word seed appears nowhere in the code: there is no seed value and no seeded generator — every deal is fresh Math.random(), and both buttons simply restart the current game with a new random deal. In particular, Replay Seed cannot replay the deal you just played; the puzzle you lost to is gone. (The collection’s other seeded games, like Pass It On, can replay a deal exactly — that is the difference a real seed makes.)

Checksum par is a best case, and the loss line is softer than it looks. Par counts the tree descent; opening the final leaf is itself a charged query, so a flawless descent lands on par or par + 1 depending on which twin leaf you open first (measured: 4 vs 5 on tier 1; the guaranteed tier-3 line is 8 against par 7). The game actually ends only when queries pass budget + 4 — 13, 15, and 17 queries on the three tiers — and that overrun check runs on internal-node queries. Nothing on screen states either number.

The repair bay shows its hand. When a corrupt leaf is selected, the deck prints the XOR difference between the received byte and the expected byte — the exact bit to flip is named before you flip it, and wrong flips cost nothing. Real repair never hands you the pristine byte to diff against; the tree hunt is the honest part of that game, and the last step is a guided one. Similarly, “Merkle” names the tree’s shape rather than its arithmetic: each node’s value is a CRC-8 recomputed over its raw byte range, not a hash of its children’s hashes, and the game holds the expected checksum for every node in advance, where real Merkle verification carries a trusted root and a proof path.

The overall parity cell is a bystander. Position 0’s overall-parity bit is computed, drawn, and labelled P0 — and never checked, never clickable, and never the flipped bit: errors are planted only in positions 1–15 / 1–31. In a real SECDED code that bit is what distinguishes a single error from a double one; this game plants no double errors for it to catch, so the syndrome does all the work alone.

“Monotonic” is the code comment’s word, not the curve’s. Zero Crossing’s steep tier is strictly monotonic, but tiers 1 and 3 are not: the sine wobble makes the curve locally decrease in its flat tails (largest measured dip ≈ 0.004, always far from the root). What holds perfectly — verified at every grid point tested — is the property bisection actually needs: one crossing, with the sign of f(x) always agreeing with which side of the root x is on. Two smaller flourishes in the same game: a probe landing within ±tol of the hidden root wins instantly, a check no real instrument can make (real instruments don’t know the root); and tier 3’s “50,000× Micro-Zoom” name is span ÷ tol, while the win triggers at bracket width 2·tol — a zoom of at least 25,000×, with your actual zoom printed in the debrief.

Not every “bit” on the telemetry is measured. Line Fault, Zero Crossing, Git Bisect, and Quadtree Radar compute the real quantity — log₂ of the span or area shrink — per move. Checksum Tree and Parity Matrix print scored labels instead (1.00 for a useful query or a mask, 0.25 for a query whose parent wasn’t known, 0.6 for a clean leaf). For parity the label happens to be exactly right — each mask’s answer genuinely is one bit of the address — but in the tree the numbers are grades, not information. The Arcade Edition adds two more dialects: a Git skip logs +0.05b, a consolation label for a test that bought no ground at all, and Backoff Arena’s spark chart isn’t in bits at all — it plots an efficiency grade from a formula, and says so on its label.

Git’s skip tiers tax the budget, not the method. The Field Guide promises you can find the regression “even working around unbuildable SKIP commits,” and you can — the star grade even forgives the skipped runs. But the CI budget forgives nothing: every test counts, skips included, and the audit’s midpoint player — playing exactly as the game advises — still lost 13% of the 64-commit tier’s games and 8% of the 256-commit tier’s to budget exhaustion when skips clustered near mid-bracket, and needed par + 1 effective runs in the worst cases. The guide doesn’t state the odds; now they’re stated.

“Quadtree” names the pattern, not a data structure. No tree is built, stored, or traversed in the Radar’s code — the state is a single bounding box, narrowed by a compass oracle that also reveals exact row/column alignment, which collapses an axis in one ping and is more than a quadrant test would say. The 2D halving itself is genuine and its bits are measured; the name points at the structure that makes this search fast over stored points in real systems.

Backoff’s window is global, and its stations have no memory. Real binary exponential backoff lives inside each station: double your own window per collision, draw a random slot within it, reset on success. The Arena keeps one window for the whole channel, stations re-roll fresh every slot with probability 1/CW, and the traffic waves are fixed tables written into the tiers — the crowd never actually reacts to your corrections between rounds, because the next wave was always going to be what it is. The physics that matters — matched window wins, in powers of two — is the model’s own, and verified. Also, doubling CW halves each node’s transmit probability; the guide’s “cuts collision density in half” is the loose version of that sentence, and the 36.8% ceiling it quotes is the many-stations limit of 1/e — at the game’s own crowd sizes the true peak runs a little higher (38.0% at 16 nodes).

The Slicer cannot be lost, and its world ends at /30. Every department list is dealt so an exact fit exists (proven above), over-allocation is accepted with a warning, and the finish screen always opens with a win — the stars grade your waste, nothing grades your survival. Slicing also stops at /30 by design: the model reserves network and broadcast addresses in every block, so the /31 point-to-point links of RFC 3021, which spend those two addresses, are outside it. Within those fences, the arithmetic is exact.

The Shift’s rotation is a cycle, not a shuffle. The Arcade Shift deals its five quick ops in a fixed order — Fault, Quadtree, Hamming, Subnet, Git — and repeats it; “mixing,” in the Field Guide’s word, means the rotation, not randomness. A player who learns the order knows what’s next, which the audit counts as a feature wearing a simplification’s clothes: it’s a rhythm game’s honesty, not a slot machine’s.
