The Vectrex Console — the lesson

The 1982 vector machine — and, not incidentally, the first console Steven ever played: a Tempest variation, and his first Asteroids, on a glowing tube sometime in the eighties. This one earned its gate, its audit, and one posture decision made in the open.

▶ Open The Vectrex Console

Where this one came from

Steven, the morning after the Atari pair landed (25 November 2026): "what about a console like the Vectrex… Vectrex was the first console I ever played a game on BTW, it was a tempest variation and my first Asteroids." The first Asteroids, on a Vectrex, is Mine Storm — the game built into the machine. So this console was commissioned with its conclusion already written, if the engineering allowed it: the idle screen should be that game. The gate ran first, at his word — "Gate the Vectrex first, then build both" — and this lesson is its receipt, the build's receipts, and the audit's.

The gate — three roads, one pass

  • EmulatorJS — FAIL, evidence-closed. Our other racks are EmulatorJS 4.2.3, so it was checked first and hardest: the npm registry carries no @emulatorjs/core-vecx at any version (404), the official CDN answers 404 for every plausible vecx payload name on both the stable and nightly channels, and the upstream README never mentions the Vectrex. There is no vecx core in that distribution to adopt. (Claims elsewhere that "EmulatorJS supports Vectrex" trace to an old, different, deprecated project of the same name.)
  • JSVecX — PASS, proven end-to-end. A pure-JavaScript port of the VecX emulator. The gate drove a real cartridge to its title screen twice — over HTTP, and again from a lone file:// document with identical frame statistics — because JSVecX is classic scripts end to end: no fetch of its own code, no workers, no WebAssembly. It is the only engine on this shelf that survives Steven's actual environment — a double-clicked local file — without a server of any kind.
  • libretro vecx — parts proven, assembly unproven; kept as the upgrade path. The core binaries are real, downloadable, and carry the one feature JSVecX lacks — save states. But no shippable frontend pairing was proven in the gate, so it stays on the shelf: if save states ever become a must for this console, that is the road to re-open.
  • The Internet Archive / MAME — also-ran. MAME's vectrex driver needs the system ROM set, the Archive's file servers send no CORS headers (measured 24 November 2026), and our vendored MAME 2003-Plus core is arcade-only. Documented, not pursued — the same failure shape as the Apple IIe gate.

The exec ROM — the posture, in the open

JSVecX carries the Vectrex's own 8 KB exec ROM as data inside fastromdata.js — decode it and the title strings sit there in plain sight: MINE…STORM. That firmware is not decoration. Cartridges call its routines, and with an empty slot it boots the game stored in it. Shipping the engine unmodified ships that ROM.

“The machine's own firmware rides inside the unmodified engine, stated on the page — the Apple IIe posture, with a rights holder who said yes in writing thirty years ago.”

Steven ruled on it explicitly — "Build both — Vectrex on JSVecX with the BIOS posture stated openly, IIe-style" — and the shape is exactly the Apple IIe precedent he accepted the day before: firmware inside an upstream build we do not modify, disclosed here and on the console page rather than footnoted. This console's case has one fact the IIe's never did: Jay Smith / Smith Engineering, the Vectrex's rights holder, put a policy in writing in the mid-1990s permitting non-commercial reproduction and distribution of Vectrex software. Everyday Cool is a personal, non-commercial shelf; Mine Storm idles on the console under that permission, and this paragraph is its citation. Nothing else about the rule changes: cartridges are still the visitor's own, read locally, never bundled, never uploaded.

What JSVecX is

VecX was written by Valavan Manohararajah — a clean, small emulator of the Vectrex: a Motorola 6809 CPU, the VIA interface chip, and an AY-3-8910 sound chip driving an analog vector generator. JSVecX is raz0red's hand port of it to JavaScript (2010–2019, finished and stable code — the opposite of a moving target). The port keeps the machine's shape: vectors are computed as analog beam movements and rasterised into a 330×410 framebuffer each frame, with per-vector intensity — the phosphor brightness steps are in the data, not a filter we added. Sound comes from the AY chip emulation; the page's glow and overlay tints are furniture laid over the tube, and the engine draws the identical lines whether they are on or off.

The license question, in plain words

JSVecX is GPL version 3. The repository's own LICENSE.md is the GPLv3 text, and it rides in the rack verbatim. (The Emulation Wiki labels the port "GPLv2"; the repo's text governs, and the discrepancy is recorded here so nobody has to re-derive it.) GPLv3 is the same family as our EmulatorJS frontend — ship it unmodified, carry the license, credit the authors — and the rack does all three. jQuery (MIT) rides along because the engine calls it for exactly two status writes; it is vendored as upstream ships it rather than stubbed out, so the engine set stays byte-honest.

The audit — Mine Storm first, then a cartridge

Driven headlessly against the shipped page, through the page's own controls and slot — never by calling the engine behind the page's back:

  • The idle screen is Mine Storm. With no cartridge anywhere, the page boots to the built-in game's title card — MINE STORM / © GCE 1982, 396 lit pixels — and its attract cycle runs through the PLAYER 1 card (139 lit). Steven's first Asteroids is the console's idle screen, as commissioned. Measured, screenshotted.
  • Button 1 starts it. A key press on A at the title advanced the machine — title (396) → PLAYER 1 card (139, held ≈5.6 s; that hold is the game's own pacing, timed in the audit) → the live playfield at ≈1,983 lit pixels, animating across 13 distinct frame counts. Measured.
  • A cartridge through the page's own slot. E-SRG was handed to the page by its drag-and-drop path: the title frame rendered at 1,937 lit pixels, 1,887 of them near-full brightness, in 3 distinct intensity levels — the phosphor steps are the machine's, not a rendering accident. The marquee and the slot both name the cartridge and its size. Measured.
  • Eject is honest. Ejecting returned the machine to the Mine Storm title (396 lit) and restored the marquee. Measured.
  • The furniture works. The glow slider rewrote the tube's filter (blur 0.99px, brightness 1.214, drop-shadow halo at 90); the overlay picker laid the Mine Storm tint (rgba(56,196,178,0.15)) over the screen. Both are page-side; the engine's frame underneath is untouched. Measured.
  • And as a lone file. The same page opened as a file:// document — scripts off the disk, cartridge loaded through the same drop path — produced the identical frame: 1,937 lit, 1,887 bright, 3 levels; eject back to 396. Measured.
  • Requests: 10–12 per session, every one same-origin or a local file; zero external. Logged.
  • One bug the audit caught in our own page. The engine writes an FPS line into the element with id status every second (vecx.js:859) — and the page's first draft had put its own status line on that very id, so the machine overwrote the page's words with frame rates. Found by reading the audit's own status capture; fixed by giving the engine its own small readout line (it sits under the tube controls, labelled as the engine's) and moving the page's line to its own id. Fixed and re-driven.

The audit's ground. Drives ran headlessly against a plain local server and against the lone file — Steven's two real environments. Sound is the one sense this audit cannot certify: the AY chip's code is upstream's and the engine starts it on the first key press, but headless Chrome has no ears. Steven's first game with the sound up is the audio receipt, and his first cartridge from his own library is the field receipt — both still his to give.

Honest notes

  • No save states — none. There is no serialize or save code anywhere in JSVecX's source (searched, not assumed). No state code, no hand-off file, and no Baton Box passenger for this console: the baton here isn't even a file. A game is played the 1982 way, in one sitting. If that ever becomes a must, the libretro vecx road from the gate is the upgrade path, with its own frontend work to prove.
  • The stick is digital. The real Vectrex stick is an analog, self-centring lever; the keyboard gives full deflection or none (arrows, with L/P/'/; as alternates in the engine's own map). Games that steer by degrees will feel broader here. Named, not hidden.
  • The overlays are tints, not the printed plastic. The original overlays were screen-printed with different colour zones for different parts of each game's screen. Ours are single-hue washes in their spirit — the page says so — because reproducing the original artwork would mean shipping the original artwork.
  • The tube is 330×410, and the page scales it. The engine rasterises into a fixed framebuffer; the size stops scale that picture up, which softens the lines slightly. On a vector machine that softness reads as phosphor bloom rather than pixel blur, and the glow slider leans into it deliberately — but the native picture is the Standard size, and the purist can play there.
  • Mine Storm's PLAYER 1 card holds ≈5.6 seconds between the title and the playfield in this emulation (timed across two audit runs). It looks like a hang the first time you meet it; it is the game's own pacing, and the page leaves it exactly as the machine deals it.
  • Sound, once more: the emulation wiki flags this port's sound as its weak spot. Our audit could not measure audio headlessly, so the claim is carried here as a warning, not a verdict — Steven's ears will settle it.
