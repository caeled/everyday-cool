What you are looking at

A rocket that wants to hover at the dashed target line, and a PID controller — proportional, integral, derivative — deciding its thrust twenty times a second. The rocket is a point mass: ÿ = u − g − (c/m)·ẏ, with g = 9.81 (in arbitrary "metres"), mass m = 1, light drag c = 0.2, and thrust acceleration u clamped to 0…40. The loop runs at a fixed step dt = 0.05 s — a 20 Hz controller, like a real flight controller — integrated with semi-implicit Euler.

How to play

Start with the P only preset. The rocket hovers, but below the target line — that gap is the gravity tax, and no amount of waiting removes it. Press PID (or raise Ki) and watch the gap die: the integral term remembers the error and pays the tax. Press PD and step the setpoint: the derivative term brakes the approach and the overshoot calms down. Then raise Kp with some Ki in and keep going — the loop rings harder and harder until it shakes itself apart. Poke kicks the rocket downward (Δv = −3 m/s). Gust adds a sinusoidal shove, ±1.5 m/s² at 0.7 rad/s, fed in as a disturbance acceleration.

The three terms

P — proportional: thrust = Kp × error. Push harder the further away you are. Alone, it must hold a permanent error to pay for gravity: at hover, thrust must equal g, and P thrust only exists when error does — ess = g/Kp. I — integral: thrust grows by Ki × ∫error dt. It remembers every second the rocket sat low and keeps adding thrust until the debt is paid — the only term that can hold thrust with zero error. D — derivative: here it acts on measurement, not error: thrust includes −Kd × ẏ, a brake proportional to climb speed, so a setpoint jump does not kick it and it starts braking before the target arrives.

Stated choices

Thrust is clamped to 0…40 m/s² every step (hover needs 9.81, so there is headroom, but a big error can still pin it). Anti-windup is conditional integration: when the thrust is saturated and the error would push it deeper into saturation, the integral is not allowed to grow that step. The derivative is on measurement — with velocity measured directly it is exactly −Kd·ẏ, no differencing of a noisy error signal. There is no sensor noise and no sensor delay in this bench; the only delay is the one sample step itself — and that step is enough to own the stability boundary (see the proofs and the lesson).

Reading the numbers

Error now is setpoint − altitude. Steady-state error (measured) is the mean error over the trailing 5 seconds of sim time; the theory line next to it is g/Kp with Ki = 0, and 0 whenever Ki > 0. Last overshoot is measured from the most recent setpoint change: how far past the new equilibrium the rocket travelled, as a percentage of the equilibrium's own move. The scope traces altitude (cyan) and thrust (amber, scaled so the hover value 9.81 sits mid-scale). If the rocket reaches the ground the bench clamps it there (altitude 0, downward velocity zeroed) — the core itself has no ground; that clamp is the room, not the physics.

Honest bends

The rocket is a point mass: no attitude, no tilt, no thrust lag, no noise. The instability in the proofs is real but it belongs to the sampled loop at dt = 0.05 s — an ideal continuous PD loop on this plant does not go unstable with Kp at all. That is not a defect of the bench; it is the bench's main lesson. Every real digital controller lives in the sampled world, and the sampled world has a cliff. The full story is on the lesson page.

The Feedback Bench — teach a rocket to hover, one correction at a time

Feedback is the oldest trick in this building: sense the error, apply a fraction of the correction, sense again. This bench is that loop as an instrument — a hovering rocket, three gains, and a gravity bill that only one of the three terms can pay. Corrected by halves, twenty times a second.

Controls

Presets set the gains, nothing else: P only is Kp 4, Ki 0, Kd 0 — watch it hover low. PD adds Kd 1. PID adds Ki 1 and the error dies. Then take Kp up by hand and find the cliff the proofs below measure.
