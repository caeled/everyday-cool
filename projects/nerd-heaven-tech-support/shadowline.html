Shadowline — fly the height you can't see

Steven's third original from the same afternoon, after the Vectrex conversation turned to Zaxxon: "another favorite. You had to THINK in 3d." The isometric view never shows your height — that was always the game's real subject. So you fly by two clues: your shadow, crawling on the ground below you, and an altimeter whose bands match the gaps in the walls ahead. Underneath, the world is honest 3D — every wall, tower and barrier is a real box with a real height, and the projection is the only lie, the deliberate one. Press T and the altimeter goes away: shadow only, the way 1982 made you fly.

Reading the sky: your shadow sits on the ground directly below the ship — the gap between ship and shadow is your altitude, 3 screen pixels per unit of height, exactly. The altimeter at the right edge is marked in the same bands the wall gaps use: when a wall is coming, its gap band lights up on the bar, so one glance tells you whether you're at gap height. Walls must be threaded through their gap; towers must be cleared; barriers hopped. Fuel burns the whole flight — shoot the ground drums to take more aboard. Run dry and the ship settles, and the ground is not soft.

The game. An isometric fortress run in the form of Sega's Zaxxon (1982) — the game Steven named for making you think in 3D. Your ship flies forward on its own, down a corridor of walls, towers, barriers, turrets and fuel drums. You slide, climb, dive, and shoot. Three ships; a sector every 900 units of distance, each a little faster and a little denser than the last; an extra ship every 10,000 points.

The honest 3D. The world is measured in units: the corridor runs 96 units wide (lateral −48 to +48), the sky is 46 units tall, and the ship's collision box is 8 units wide and 7 tall. Every obstacle is a box in that world. A wall spans the corridor with one gap band 13 units tall; you pass only if the ship's whole 7-unit height fits inside the band. A tower is a box of a stated height at a stated lane; a barrier the same, lower and wider. Collision is decided in world units, never in screen pixels — the isometric projection (3 px per unit; the ground sheared so ahead is up and to the right) is a drawing, not the physics. Gap centres are always placed so the climb or dive they demand is comfortably inside what the ship can do in the distance available — no wall is a trap; every crash is earned.

The instruments. Two, inherited from 1982. The shadow: the ship's silhouette drawn on the ground plane beneath it — its vertical separation from the ship is exactly your altitude times 3 px, no perspective tricks. The altimeter: the bar at the right edge, 0 to 46, with the next wall's gap band highlighted on it while that wall approaches. Learn either and you can fly; learn the shadow and you can fly with the instruments gone.

Shadow only (T). Steven's standing option, born in Shardstorm the same day: the game may show you the answer, but it must let you refuse it. T (or the INSTRUMENTS button) takes the altimeter away — and with it, if the X-Ray is on, the profile strip and the numeric altitude readout. What remains is the shadow, the way the original left you. Nothing else changes: the boxes are the same boxes.

The X-Ray layer (F2). A dashed drop-line from ship to shadow, your altitude in numbers, every obstacle's true height labelled, and along the bottom a profile strip: a side elevation of the next 170 units — ship, walls with their gaps, towers — the view Zaxxon never gave anyone. It is drawn from the same world state the collisions use, so it cannot disagree with the game.

Scoring. Distance pays 1 point per 4 units flown. Threading a wall pays 100, plus a precision bonus of up to 60 for crossing at the band's centre, plus 40 if you threaded it with less than 2.5 units of clearance — nerve money. Ground turrets pay 100, fuel drums 50 and 22 units of fuel. Fuel burns at 2.2 per second; a drum's fuel is capped at a full tank of 100.

Honesty notes. The ship is a drawn vector jet, not a sprite; there is no sound. This page makes no network requests and runs from a lone local file.
