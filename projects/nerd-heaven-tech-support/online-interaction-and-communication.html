Online Interaction and Communication, as Told in the Age of the BBS

Rules, etiquette, and rules of engagement — a guide to productive, if not always fully polite, online communication. By Steven Powell, November 2026.

Introduction: The Room With a Door

Before the feed, there was the board.

A bulletin board system was a computer in somebody's house, attached to one phone line. You dialed it. If somebody else was on, you got a busy signal and you waited your turn like a person. When you got in, you were a guest — in a literal room, on a literal machine, under the eye of a sysop who owned the place and could end your account with one keystroke and no appeals court.

Everything about online manners that is worth knowing was worked out there, and on Usenet beside it, by people who had never met and would mostly never meet. It was worked out under conditions the modern internet abolished: the room was small, the regulars knew each other, memory was long, and your name — even a handle — was worth something because you had to live under it tomorrow.

This is that code, written down. It is not a code of politeness. Some of the best communication I ever saw online was blunt to the edge of rude, and some of the worst damage was done in perfect, courteous sentences. The code is about something else: whether the communication produces anything — understanding, correction, a decision, a person who stays — or whether it just burns time and people.

Productive, if not always fully polite. That is the whole standard.

Part One: Entering the Room

Read before you write. Every board had a culture already in motion when you arrived: running arguments, old jokes, wounds you could not see. The newcomer who posted first and read later stepped on all of them in his first three messages. The boards had a word for the correct order of operations — lurk. It was not shyness. It was reconnaissance. You read a room before you speak in it for the same reason you look before you step off a curb.

Everybody was new once. This is the first law, and the boards enforced it unevenly but the good ones enforced it absolutely. The person asking the question you answered two years ago is not stupid; they are new, which is a temporary condition that every expert in the room once shared. Answer the beginner's question like it matters, because to them it does. Hand them the map instead of mocking them for not having it. And the standards do not move — welcoming is not lowering the bar, it is holding the door.

You are a guest until you are a regular. A board was somebody's house. You did not rearrange the furniture on your first visit. You learned where things were, who the sysop was, what the room was for, and you earned your standing in small deposits: a good answer, a file shared, a thread carried well. Standing was never claimed. It accrued, or it didn't.

Your handle is your name. A handle was not anonymity — it was the opposite. It was the name your reputation lived under, in a room small enough to remember. You could burn it, once. Then you were the story the regulars told the next newcomer.

Part Two: The Talk

Quote what you are answering. Bandwidth was precious, so you quoted the line you were replying to — a few lines, trimmed — and answered under it. The practice survived because it is not really about bandwidth. Quoting is proof of being read. It says: I heard the actual words, not the words I expected. Most online argument, then and now, is two people answering posts the other one never wrote. The quote is the discipline that prevents it. Read the post. Quote the line. Answer that.

Say the thing in the subject line. A message cost somebody a download and a minute of a shared phone line. "Question" was not a subject. "RE: stuff" was not a subject. The subject line was a promise about what was inside, and breaking it taxed everybody. The modern version taxes attention instead of baud, and attention is the scarcer resource now.

Signal is a duty, not a talent. Every post either raised the room's signal or lowered it. A me-too, a content-free agreement, a joke that had already been made twice in the thread — these were not crimes, they were noise, and noise was understood to be a cost imposed on everyone who came after. Before posting, the regular asked the only question that mattered: does the room know more, or feel better, or decide something, because this exists? If not, the post stayed in the drafts of the mind, where most posts belong.

Correction is a gift; treat it like one. On a good board, being corrected in public was not humiliation — it was the room working as designed. The corrected party said thank you and updated, and his standing rose, because everyone watching learned he could be trusted to repair. Reward correction, not completion. The poster who never errs is not the best poster in the room; he is the one whose errors nobody bothers to fix anymore, which is the room quietly giving up on him.

Do not shout, and do not whisper either. All capitals was shouting, and reserved for the genuinely deaf moment. But the opposite failure existed too: the hedge-padded, apology-wrapped post that never quite said anything. The boards preferred a plain sentence, plainly owned. Say it like you will still be here tomorrow — because on a board, you would be.

Part Three: Rules of Engagement

Disagreement was not a malfunction of the board. It was half the reason the board existed. The code never asked anyone to agree. It governed how.

Attack the argument; the person is out of bounds. Not because people are fragile — board regulars were not fragile — but because it is inefficient. The moment a fight becomes about the person, it stops producing anything except heat. The argument is the only part of your opponent you are licensed to dismantle, and it is also the only part worth dismantling. Everything else is arson for warmth.

Bring receipts. The board ran on a simple evidentiary standard: claims wanted support. Quote the manual. Post the log. Name the source. A position stated with its evidence could be tested; a position stated with volume could only be endured. And when your receipts lost — when the other side's evidence was simply better — the code had a move for that too. It is in Part Four, and it is the most important move in this book.

A flame with a purpose is a tool; cruelty for sport is noise. The age produced an art form now mostly lost: the flame that taught. It was hot, it was funny, it was aimed at a position or a piece of work, and the target — this is the part nobody believes anymore — often thanked the flamer, because the burn came with the fix inside it. What the code despised was not heat. It was cruelty with no payload: the dunk, the pile-on, the mockery of the newcomer. That was not strength. In a small room, everyone could tell the difference between a surgeon and a vandal, whatever the temperature of the language.

The pile-on is a confession. When six regulars descend on one poster, the room has not won an argument; it has admitted it could not win it singly. Headcount is cheap. Judgment is scarce. One good answer outweighs forty agreements, and one good correction outweighs forty dunks.

Appeal to the sysop, not the mob. Every room needs a referee who is not a participant. The sysop's rulings could be wrong, and the good ones reversed themselves in public when they were — see Part Two, on correction — but the existence of an appeal that was not "get more people angry" kept fights bounded. The moment a dispute's only court is the crowd, the dispute never ends; it just recruits.

Escalate in order. The ladder ran: answer the argument → answer it harder → take it to private mail → take it to the sysop → leave the thread. Skipping rungs was itself a violation, not of politeness but of engineering: every skipped rung multiplied the audience for a problem that had a smaller solution. Most fights that scar a community are two-person problems performed for two hundred.

Part Four: As You Wish

This is the heart of the code, and it comes whole from the old Usenet lesson the author published as "You Win." It is quoted and built from that piece throughout, because the piece is the doctrine and the doctrine should be heard in its own voice.

The trick, as it was taught. You take it to a point. You let them have the range they want. And then you give in with a "you win" — and the "you win" does all the rest of the work, because they keep swinging at a guy who has already left the ring, and the more they swing, the more obvious it gets that nobody is watching except them. It is a good trick. It works every time.

It is a spotlight, not a concession. That is the part the untrained ear gets wrong, and it is the whole of the doctrine. The "you win" isn't a concession. It's a spotlight. You hand them the stage, walk off it, and let the audience watch them perform for the walls. "As you wish" — the bow you give on your way out of a fight that is not worth your soul — is not retreat either. It is the oldest dampening method in online life: let the fools demonstrate their foolishness while the rest sit back and laugh. A killfile only hid a fool from you. The "you win" does something better. It shows the fool to everyone, at full volume, alone on a stage he thought was a battlefield.

Know what a fight costs — and count the cost of the trick, too. Every engagement is paid for in the only currencies that do not refill: your time, your temper, and your standing in the room. The veteran asks first whether the fight can change anything, teach anyone, or protect someone; if it cannot, it is not a fight, it is a leak. But the original piece is honest about the deeper cost, and the honesty is part of the doctrine: the trick terrifies, because you shouldn't need it. A room that requires a technique for surviving its shouters is already spending its regulars' strength on weather instead of conversation. Use the bow. And say out loud that needing it is the symptom.

Don't feed it. Don't leave. Here is where the doctrine is most often misread, so it gets its own heading. The assignment of the quiet majority was never to abandon the room to the loud. It was: don't feed it, don't leave — just keep having the real conversation somewhere the noise can't follow. On Usenet, the conversations didn't die. They moved — first to moderated groups, then to mailing lists, then to places the shouters hadn't discovered yet — and each migration left the soapbox crowd standing in an emptier room, talking to each other about how nobody understood them. The bow is for a fight; it ends an engagement. The doctrine is for a room; it keeps one. The shouters are not the majority. They never were. They just post a hundred times more, so the room looks loud. The regulars' work is to be visibly, stubbornly still there when the swinging stops.

Do not chase the last word — it was never the last post. The last word is the most expensive word in any thread and the least valuable: the audience has already decided, the opponent will never concede it, and the room remembers the chase long after it forgets the point. The original piece closes the matter for good: the shouters can have all the algorithm's rope they want; the rest of us get the conversations — and the last word was always ours anyway. The last word is not a post. It is the conversation still going on after the shouting stops.

When you do leave a thread, leave it clean. Threads outlive their fighters, and the archive is a reader too. No parting accusation, no scorched summary of everyone's failures. If a real point was buried in the fight, leave it standing, plainly stated, for the reader who arrives next month. The opposite of the bow is not staying to fight. It is staying to smolder — present, silent, poisoning. The code has no respect for it at all.

The reverse side: hold the door. The bow is only half the doctrine, because before the exit there is the entrance, and the same code governs both. The person who can leave a fight gracefully is the person who remembers arriving as a stranger. So: hold the door for the one who just walked in, knowing nothing, needing everything, hoping the room is not going to eat them alive. Welcoming is not agreement, and it is not surrender. It is a door held open by somebody still standing in it.

The door and the bow are one move, seen from two ends of a visit. You enter like a guest. You fight only the fights that produce something, at full strength and inside the rules. You refuse the fights that only consume — not by fleeing them, but by spotlighting them and staying for the real conversation. As you wish is not giving up. It is declining to pay.

You win. Let the fools demonstrate it, while the rest of us sit back and watch.

Part Five: What the Feed Forgot

The boards died, and the code nearly died with them — not because it stopped working, but because the conditions that enforced it were demolished on purpose.

There is no door. A board had one line and a login screen; you arrived somewhere, as someone, into a room with edges. The feed has no edges and no arrival. Nobody holds a door that does not exist, and nobody is a guest in a place with no host. Half the code — everything in Part One — assumes a room. The feed's first casualty was the room itself.

There is no sysop. The referee was replaced by an engagement metric, and an engagement metric is a sysop who profits from the fight and therefore never stops one. Appeals go to a machine that cannot be corrected in public because it never speaks in public. Part Three's ladder has no top rung anymore; escalation now runs answer → dunk → screenshot → swarm, forever, because forever is what pays.

Memory inverted. The board remembered your standing — the accumulated weight of how you had behaved — and forgot your worst post by spring. The feed forgets your standing instantly and remembers your worst post forever, stripped of the thread that explained it. A reputation system that keeps only the failures teaches everyone the same lesson: never correct, never concede, never bow. Which is why nobody does. The old system rewarded correction, visibly, in front of the room. The new one optimizes for completion — for the post that lands, not the poster who repairs.

What the code is protecting. It was never the platform. It is the one venue in human history with no door and no dress code — where a field tech in rural Ohio, a guy who fixes computers, gets to argue with the people designing the future his child will live in. The salons of Paris had a door. The coffeehouses of London had a door. This room, at its best, has neither: you just show up with your brain. That has never existed before, and it is not guaranteed to keep existing. That is what the regulars are for.

So carry the room with you. The code was never really about the technology. It was a set of decisions, and decisions are portable. Read before you write, in any medium. Quote what you are answering. Make the beginner's question matter. Attack arguments and bring receipts. Reward the person who corrects himself — be that person, in public, where it counts. Fight the fights that produce something, at full strength; spotlight the ones that only consume; and do not leave the room to the shouters — the majority's oldest assignment is to stay, and keep the real conversation going where the noise can't follow. Hold doors. The feed will not reward any of this. The boards would have, and the people worth talking to still do.

Judgment is scarce and headcount is cheap, in every age. The age of the BBS simply made it impossible to pretend otherwise.

The Rules, as Posted on the Board

Every board's login screen carried some version of this list. This is the whole book, in the form it would have taken at 2400 baud.

 1.  READ THE ROOM BEFORE YOU POST IN IT.
 2.  EVERYBODY WAS NEW ONCE. HOLD THE DOOR.
 3.  QUOTE WHAT YOU ARE ANSWERING.
 4.  THE SUBJECT LINE IS A PROMISE.
 5.  SIGNAL IS A DUTY. NOISE IS A TAX ON EVERYONE.
 6.  CORRECTION IS A GIFT. SAY THANK YOU AND UPDATE.
 7.  ATTACK THE ARGUMENT. THE PERSON IS OUT OF BOUNDS.
 8.  BRING RECEIPTS.
 9.  A FLAME WITH A PURPOSE IS A TOOL.
     CRUELTY FOR SPORT IS JUST NOISE.
10.  SIX ON ONE IS A CONFESSION, NOT A VICTORY.
11.  APPEAL TO THE SYSOP, NOT THE MOB.
12.  ESCALATE IN ORDER. MOST FIGHTS ARE TWO-PERSON
     PROBLEMS PERFORMED FOR TWO HUNDRED.
13.  THE LAST WORD COSTS THE MOST AND BUYS NOTHING.
14.  WHEN THE FIGHT IS NOT WORTH YOUR SOUL:
     BOW. "AS YOU WISH." WALK OFF THE STAGE.
15.  LEAVE THE ROOM BETTER THAN THE FIGHT FOUND IT.
