    04 / HELP

    How Satellite Watch works.

    Where the orbits come from

    CelesTrak, every two hours

    Each satellite's orbit is a "two-line element set" published by CelesTrak from US Space Force tracking. The page asks for a family at most once every two hours (that is how often the data changes, and CelesTrak asks for no more). If it cannot connect, it uses the copy saved last time, then a snapshot packed with this room, so it works offline — the age of the data is always shown.

    Fresh, saved, or packed — and it says which

    How it works out the position

    The SGP4 maths

    From those numbers, the same SGP4 maths that satellite trackers everywhere use works out where each satellite is this second, and where it goes next — that is the blue ground-track line. Orbits change (engines fire, air drags low satellites down), so data older than about two weeks can be far off.

    Good for days, not months

    Seeing one with your eyes

    Sunlit satellite, dark sky

    You can see a satellite when it is lit by the Sun but your sky is dark — the hour or two after sunset and before sunrise. Passes marked 👀 are those. The ISS is the brightest; high passes (above 40°) are easiest. Starlink trains just after a launch look like a line of moving stars.

    Look up at dusk

    Lots of dots

    Ten thousand Starlinks

    Starlink has more than ten thousand satellites. To keep the page smooth, big families show an even sample of 3,000 at first; the Show all button draws every one (slower on older computers).

    Smooth first, everything on request

    Privacy

    Your spot stays here

    Find me and the ZIP box are only used to work out passes over you, in this tab. Nothing about you is stored or sent: orbit data comes from CelesTrak with no information about you, and a typed place is looked up on OpenStreetMap's Nominatim. If your device cannot say where it is, a free service (geojs.io or ipwho.is) gives a town-level guess from your internet address.

    No accounts, no trackers
