Catch the Pass — the lesson

Catch the Pass is an umpire game about the oldest skill in satellite watching: knowing what a pass will do before it does it. The bulletin gives you the rise and set. You call the two things it leaves out — how high the satellite climbs, and when it gets there — and the sky rules on your call. This page is the other half of the game: what a pass really is, why the peak is the whole story, what the fair line means in degrees you can measure with your own hand, and how the game is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button.

What a pass is

A satellite in low orbit circles the Earth in about ninety minutes, but you can only see it during a pass that meets two conditions at once: the satellite is still lit by the Sun, and your sky is dark. High above you, night comes later — the satellite flies in daylight long after your ground has gone dark. That is why satellite watching lives in the twilight windows: the hour or two after sunset and before sunrise. A pass outside those windows is really happening; it is just invisible, a sunlit bird over a sunlit observer or a dark one over a dark one.

During a visible pass the satellite rises over one horizon, climbs to a highest point — the peak, or culmination — and sinks to the opposite horizon, all in a few minutes. It does not blink (blinking lights are aircraft) and it moves at a steady, stately pace. Mid-pass it may fade out in seconds: that is the satellite sliding into the Earth's shadow, not clouds. For the real sky, Heavens-Above has published pass predictions for decades, and Wikipedia's satellite watching page is a good plain-language start. This room's main page does the genuine prediction work — see Satellite Watch itself.

Why peak elevation rules everything

One number decides what kind of pass you get: the peak elevation, the highest angle the satellite reaches above your horizon. It rules three things at once.

  • Length. A high pass crosses more sky, so it lasts longer. A satellite that peaks at 80° can be up for seven minutes or more; one that grazes at 15° is gone in under three.
  • Brightness. At its peak the satellite is as close to you as it gets on that pass — a few hundred kilometres overhead instead of two thousand away at the horizon — and brightness falls off fast with distance. (The game does not score brightness; see the honest box below.)
  • Clarity. Low in the sky you are looking through the thickest part of the atmosphere: haze, dust, light pollution, and every rooftop and tree. High passes climb out of all of it. Round 4 of the game, the haze round, is this fact with a score attached.

The game's whole duration model is that first bullet, written as one line:

pass length (seconds) = 100 + 4.4 × peak height (degrees) The game's own formula, quoted from its code. A 15° graze lasts 166 s; a 40° pass, 276 s; a 78° station pass, 443 s. Real passes vary with the orbit, but the ordering never does: higher is longer.

The two calls, and the fair line

Before each pass you call peak height (5–90°) and time to peak — how long after rising the satellite culminates. That second call is the sleeper: the peak is not always at half time. A pass can climb fast and coast down slowly, or the reverse, so time-to-peak is a real prediction, not half of the duration.

The umpire's line tightens as the night goes on. These are the game's exact tolerances:

Both calls must be inside the line for a FAIR ruling — miss either one and it is a foul, with the miss named ("wide by 9°", "late by 52 s"). Points run from 100 for a perfect call down: 6 points per degree of peak error, 0.8 per second of timing error, floored at zero.

What "wide by 6°" means, measured with your hand

Degrees are abstract until you hold them up against the sky. The classic observer's ruler is your own hand at arm's length: a fist is about 10° across, three fingers together about 5°, a thumb about 2°, a pinky fingertip about 1°. So a call that is wide by 6° missed the true peak by a little more than three fingers — close enough to see you were nearly right, far enough that the umpire has no choice. The round 1 line, ±8°, is a bit under a fist; the finale's ±5° is three fingers. When you watch a real pass, hold the fist up: it is the same instrument the game is grading.

Worked example — from the game's own proof run

The game's first pass is fixed by its seed, so the proof can quote it exactly. The ISS rises at 19:42 in the SSW, sweeps 238° of sky, and sets in the ENE, 453 seconds (7:33) later. The truth the umpire holds: peak 80.3°, reached 210 seconds (3:30) after rising — note the peak comes before half time; this pass climbs fast and coasts down.

  • Call 76° at 3:20: peak error 4.3°, timing error 10.4 s — both inside the ±8° / ±45 s line. CALLED IT FAIR, 66 points.
  • Call 71° at 3:20: timing still fine, but the peak error is 9.3° — 1.3° past the line. FOUL — wide by 9°, 36 points. One fist instead of three fingers too many, and the ruling flips.
  • Call 80° at half time (3:47): the height is nearly perfect, but assuming the peak sits at half time leaves you about 16 s late — still fair in round 1, and a foul by the finale's ±30 s line if the pass leans the other way. Time-to-peak is a call, not a default.

How it's built

Every pass is drawn by a seeded random generator (mulberry32, seed 20261121 plus a per-round offset), so a pass is dealt once and can be replayed exactly — the proof suite plays against the same sky you do. The arc is analytic, two pieces joined at the peak: a quarter-sine climbing, a quarter-cosine descending, which is what lets the peak sit anywhere along the pass. Azimuth sweeps at a steady rate from rise to set. Scoring is a pair of absolute differences against the tolerances above — there is no hidden judgement in the umpire. Before the game shipped, a headless proof checked that a perfect caller scores zero error and FAIR on all six rounds, that a call off by exactly 4° is scored exactly 4° (and one off by 12° is a foul), that five hundred generated passes keep their peaks inside (0°, 90°] with arcs that start and end exactly on the horizon, and that the same seed always deals the same sky.

Simplifications, named

Not real orbital mechanics: the game invents its passes from a seeded generator. Real prediction starts from two-line element sets and the SGP4 propagator — which is exactly what this room's main page (Satellite Watch) runs on, from live CelesTrak data. Play here for the calling skill; go there for tonight's truth.

No brightness: real visibility turns on magnitude — how bright the satellite actually gets — which depends on its size, surface, and the Sun's angle. The game scores geometry only and never claims a pass would have been seeable.

Idealized arcs: real passes are not perfect sine-and-cosine arcs, their azimuth does not sweep at a perfectly steady rate, and the peak of a real pass is shaped by the orbit's geometry, not drawn from a range. The shapes here are honest teaching shapes, not ephemerides.

Compressed time: every pass plays in about nine seconds on screen. Only the clock is squeezed; the geometry, tolerances, and rulings are the model's real numbers.
