Putting it all
together: A Survival Guide.

Every other room in this collection teaches one thing at a time. This room is where they all get used at once, outdoors, with dirt on them: the chemistry makes the soap, the electronics makes the battery, the sky gives the direction, and the body knowledge does the first aid. It is written the way Scouts learn it — and every single card carries two lines: a safety line, and an honesty line that says what the tip can and cannot do. A guide that grades its own advice is a guide you can trust with the strong stuff.

First aid on this page follows the American Red Cross and Scouts BSA — the references are named in the Help section and in Sources & limitations. This page teaches and reminds; it is not a first-aid course, and in a real emergency the first step is always to call 911 (or your local emergency number).

01 / FIRE FROM LITTLE

Fire from little.

A fire is three things holding hands: heat, fuel, and air. Every method below is just a way of arranging the meeting. Gather more wood than you think you need before you strike anything — fires die of errands. Tinder is the fine, dry, fluffy stuff that catches first (dry grass, shredded bark, wood shavings, a cotton ball worked open); kindling is pencil-thin sticks; fuel is everything bigger. Work up in sizes, never down.

Three fire lays

Teepee · lean-to · log cabin

Teepee: stand kindling sticks in a cone over a ball of tinder, like the poles of a tent, leaving a door on the side the wind comes from. It draws air up through the middle and flames climb fast — the quickest lay for getting a young fire going. Lean-to: push one long stick into the ground at an angle over the tinder and lean smaller sticks against it like a roof; it shelters the tinder from wind and drips while it catches. Log cabin: lay two sticks parallel on either side of the tinder, two more across them at right angles, and build up a little square tower, topping it with the smallest kindling. It burns down slowly and evenly into a bed of coals — the cook's lay, and the one Smokey Bear's own guide teaches.

Safety: Build only where fires are allowed, on cleared ground inside a ring of stones or an existing fire pit, at least 15 feet from tents, brush, and low branches — with a bucket of water and a shovel already standing by, not fetched later.

Honestly: The lay matters less than the tinder. A perfect log cabin over damp tinder fails; a messy teepee over dry tinder works. Spend your care on the smallest wood, not the architecture.

The ferro rod

Sparks on demand, thousands of them

A ferrocerium rod is a stick of metal alloy that sheds burning sparks — around 3,000 °C — when a steel edge scrapes down it hard. Hold the rod still, tip close to the tinder, and pull the scraper (or the back of a knife) toward you, so the spark shower lands in the tinder and your hand moves away from the pile, not through it. It works wet, it works cold, and one rod is good for thousands of strikes, which is why it lives in so many Scout kits.

Safety: A knife used as a scraper is still a knife — spine toward the work, edge away from fingers, and a grown-up's call on who strikes. Sparks are real fire the instant they land; nothing flammable behind the tinder.

Honestly: The rod is the easy part. Ferro rods fail beginners on tinder, not on sparks — if your tinder won't take a spark from a rod, it wouldn't have taken a match either. Fluff it finer and drier.

Flint & steel

The old way: a spark you have to catch

Strike a sharp edge of flint (or quartz, or chert) down the face of a high-carbon steel striker and it shaves off a sliver of steel that burns in the air — a much smaller, cooler spark than a ferro rod's. Too small to light tinder directly, so the old-timers caught it on char cloth: cotton cloth baked in a sealed tin until it turned black, which takes a spark and holds it as a creeping ember. Nurse that ember in a tinder bundle, blowing gently, until it bursts into flame. It is slow, fiddly, and deeply satisfying.

Safety: Making char cloth means a tin of cloth smouldering in a fire — a grown-up job, done outdoors, and the tin stays shut and untouched until it is stone cold. It looks harmless long before it is.

Honestly: This is a demonstration skill, not a plan. Nobody should ever need flint and steel while carrying a ferro rod and matches — learn it to understand what a spark is, and because it connects your hands to about five thousand years of campers.

Friction fire: the bow drill

Rubbing two sticks together — for real

A bow drill spins a wooden spindle against a fireboard using a bow and string. The friction grinds out wood dust and heats it until a tiny pile of dust becomes a coal, which goes into the tinder bundle. The kit has five parts — bow, spindle, fireboard, handhold, and the tinder bundle — and every one of them has to be right: dry, soft wood (willow, cedar, basswood, yucca stalk), a spindle that spins true, steady pressure, and long smooth strokes that use the whole bow.

Safety: This is an endurance event done kneeling over a blade-free but very real fire-starting — take breaks, watch your posture, and keep the ember work over bare ground. The coal you make is a fire; treat it like one from the first second.

Honestly: The bow drill is genuinely hard, and this room will not pretend otherwise. Most people fail for hours or days before their first coal — damp wood, a wobbling spindle, strokes too short. That is the lesson the skill teaches: it works, it has worked for millennia, and it will make you respect the match in your pocket like nothing else. Practice it at home, for fun, never as your only plan.

Putting it out — dead out

The second half of every fire

Smokey Bear's method has four beats: drown, stir, drown, feel. Pour on lots of water until the hissing stops — all the embers, not just the red ones. Stir the ashes and dirt with a shovel, scraping sticks and logs so no hidden ember stays smouldering. Drown again until everything is cool. Then feel: hover the back of your hand above the ashes. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave. Dirt or sand alone does not put a fire out — it can bury a live ember that wakes up hours later in the wind.

Safety: A fire is watched by a person the whole time it lives — never left alone, never left to "burn itself out" while you sleep. If a fire ever escapes the ring, call 911 first and fight it only if you can do so safely.

Honestly: Most campfire harm doesn't happen at lighting or at cooking — it happens at leaving. The drown-stir-drown-feel routine takes five minutes and is the difference between a fire you had and a fire you caused.

02 / WATER

Water you can trust.

Outdoor water has two separate problems, and they need two separate fixes. The first problem is stuff — grit, leaves, murk, the things that make water look and taste like a creek. The second problem is germs — bacteria, viruses, and parasites far too small to see, in water that can look perfectly clear. A filter handles the first problem. Only heat (or proper disinfection) handles the second. This room teaches both, in that order, and never lets you confuse them.

Filtering does NOT make water safe.

A layered filter clears grit and improves taste. The germs sail straight through it. Boiling is the step that makes water safe: a full rolling boil for 1 minute — 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, where water boils cooler. Filter first if the water is murky (germs hide in the murk), then boil. Never let the filter talk you out of the boil.

Build the layered filter

A bottle, four layers, ten minutes

Cut the bottom off a plastic bottle and turn it upside down into a cup — that funnel shape is the whole machine. Line the neck with cloth (a bandana or coffee filter works). Then stack, from the neck upward: a layer of gravel, a layer of sand, a layer of crushed charcoal from a cold fire (rinse it first), and a final cloth on top to catch the big pieces. Pour creek water in slowly. Each layer stops smaller stuff than the one above it: the cloth takes leaves, the charcoal grabs some of the tastes and smells, the sand strains the fine murk, the gravel keeps it all from washing through. The first cupful runs cloudy — throw it back through until it runs clearer.

Safety: Treat every drop that comes out of this filter as untreated water, because it is. Keep the filter cup and the drinking cup as two different cups, and never let them swap jobs.

Honestly: Charcoal in a bottle is not an activated-carbon purifier, whatever the internet says. It makes water taste better — which is genuinely useful, because water that tastes fine is water people will actually boil and drink.

Boil it — the step that counts

One minute rolling, three at altitude

Bring the water to a full rolling boil — big bubbles that don't stop when you stir — and keep it there for 1 minute. Above 6,500 feet (about 2,000 m), water boils at a lower temperature, so give it 3 minutes. That's the CDC's guidance, and it kills the bacteria, viruses, and parasites that cause waterborne illness. Let it cool, and store it in a clean, covered container. Boiled water tastes flat because the dissolved air boiled out of it; shaking it in a bottle or pouring it back and forth between two clean cups puts the air — and the taste — back.

Safety: Boiling handles germs. It does nothing at all about chemicals — fuel, pesticides, mine runoff. Water you suspect is chemically contaminated is not a boiling problem; it's a find-different-water problem.

Honestly: One honest minute at a rolling boil is the whole trick. The ten-minute boils in old books were written for fuel-rich kitchens and doubtful thermometers; modern guidance is one minute, three at altitude, and it is enough.

If you truly cannot boil

The backup plans, ranked

Sometimes there is no fire and no stove. The honest ranking: a real purifier or purification tablets from your kit, used exactly as their instructions say, beat everything improvised. Chemical disinfection (the CDC's emergency page, linked on the shelf below, gives the careful household-bleach method) works on most germs but is fussy about cloudy water and weak against one tough parasite, Cryptosporidium — filter cloudy water first and follow the instructions to the letter. Sunlight in a clear bottle (the SODIS method) is the last resort: real, studied, and slow — six hours of strong sun, and it fails in cloudy weather and murky water.

Safety: Bleach for water is a measured medicine dose, never a glug — if you can't measure it and time it, don't drink it. When in doubt, wait for fire.

Honestly: Every method on this card is a compromise wearing a costume. Fire plus a pot is the only plan on this page with no asterisks, which is why Section 01 comes first in this room.

03 / SOAP & OIL

Soap from ash and fat.

Soap is one of humanity's oldest chemical tricks, and it is pure Everyday Chemistry wearing an apron: take fat, take a strong alkali, and introduce them. The alkali historically came from wood ashes — which is why soap belonged to fire-keeping households for thousands of years, and why it belongs in this room, right after the fire section.

Rendering: oil from fat

Low heat, patience, a strainer

Rendering means melting the usable fat out of fatty trimmings and leaving the cracklings and water behind. Cut the fat small, put it in a heavy pot with a splash of water so it doesn't scorch at the start, and hold it at the gentlest heat you have — a slow oven or the edge of the fire, never a roaring one. Over an hour or more the fat melts clear. Strain it through cloth into a clean jar and let it set. What you have is tallow or lard: cooking oil, candle stock, leather dressing — and soap waiting to happen. The same low-and-slow idea renders oil from oily fish and from some nuts and seeds, in smaller, harder-won amounts.

Safety: Hot fat burns worse than hot water and keeps burning on skin. Heavy pot, long spoon, no water splashes into hot fat ever, and a grown-up owns the pot from first melt to final pour.

Honestly: Rendering is slow, smelly work that fills a kitchen with eau de farmyard. It is also the difference between throwing away half an animal and using nearly all of it — the old households weren't being quaint, they were being thorough.

Wood-ash lye

The alkali half of soap

Rain water (or any soft water) trickled slowly through a barrel of hardwood ashes comes out the bottom as lye water — the ashes give up their potassium, and the water turns slippery and alkaline. The old test was whether it could float a fresh egg with a coin-sized patch showing. Ash lye is weaker and less predictable than the measured lye a modern soapmaker buys, which is exactly why frontier soap was often soft, made in a pot rather than bars, and judged batch by batch.

Safety: Lye is caustic — it burns skin and eyes, full stop. Gloves and eye protection, a grown-up does every step, it is stored labelled and out of children's reach, and vinegar is not a first-aid plan for a lye burn: flood with clean water and get help. No shortcuts, no exceptions, no "just this once."

Honestly: This is the one card in the room where "close enough" doesn't exist. If the grown-ups aren't fully in charge and fully geared, the correct survival skill is skipping the soap and washing with water and friction, which works better than pride.

Saponification: what soap is

The chemistry, in plain words

Stir lye into melted fat and the alkali takes the fat molecules apart and rebuilds them into two new things: soap and glycerin. That rebuilding is called saponification, and once it finishes there is no fat and no lye left — only soap, which is why finished soap can't burn you. The soap then works because each of its molecules has a split personality: one end loves water, the other end loves grease, so it surrounds dirt and grease and lets water carry them away. New soap is harsh and unfinished; bars rest — cure — for about four weeks, getting milder and harder, before they touch skin.

Safety: The mixture is caustic until saponification finishes, and the curing clock is part of the recipe, not a suggestion. Test a finished, cured bar the way soapmakers do (a tiny touch, or a pH strip) before it washes anybody.

Honestly: Camp soap from ashes is a fine teacher and a mediocre product — soft, variable, sometimes harsh. It teaches the real chemistry better than any diagram, which is its job in this room. The Chemistry room has the atoms-and-molecules version of this same story.

04 / BATTERIES FROM WHAT'S AROUND

Electricity from the pantry.

Two different metals in something sour make a battery — that is the whole secret, and it is the same trade the fruit battery in Everyday Electronics makes with an orange. Outdoors and in the kitchen, the parts are whatever is at hand: a lemon, a potato, a jar of vinegar, a copper coin, a galvanized nail.

One cell: copper, zinc, and sour

About nine-tenths of a volt

Push a copper piece (a coin, a bit of bare copper wire) and a galvanized nail — galvanized means coated in zinc — into a lemon or a potato, not touching each other. The acid in the juice reacts with the two metals differently: electrons pile up at the zinc, and if you give them a wire to travel through, they flow to the copper. Measure across the two metals with a multimeter and you'll see roughly 0.9 volts from one lemon or potato. That voltage is real, and it is also nearly the whole story — because the current is a trickle.

Safety: A fruit or potato that has hosted a battery is for looking, not eating — the metals leave traces in it. Wash hands after building, and let a grown-up push in the nails; galvanized nails are sharp.

Honestly: A single cell will not light an LED, and that is not the build failing — a red LED needs about 2 volts before it lights at all. One cell is a voltage lesson. Lighting something takes the next card.

Series: the pushes add up

Three or four cells, one glow

Wire cells nose-to-tail — copper of the first to nail of the second, copper of the second to nail of the third — and their voltages add: three lemons give about 2.7 volts, four about 3.6. Now the free ends can light a red LED: the free copper end to the LED's longer leg, the free nail end to the shorter. Expect a gentle glow in a dim room, not a torch beam. This adding-up is called series, and it is the same reason the batteries in a flashlight sit in a row.

Safety: Series adds voltage but these cells stay gentle — the danger on this card is only the standing ones: sharp nails, and battery fruit never goes back in the fruit bowl.

Honestly: Real electricity, tiny current — enough for an LED and an education, not for charging a phone, running a radio, or any of the other things video titles promise. A lemon battery powering a phone is a trick of editing; a lemon battery powering an LED is physics, and physics is better.

Why this is in a survival guide

The principle travels; the lemon doesn't

Nobody survives on a potato battery. What survives is the understanding: electricity is chemistry you can arrange, two metals and something sour, anywhere on Earth. That understanding is what the Electronics room builds into circuits, radios, and amplifiers — and it started, for a lot of working scientists, as a kid staring at a faint LED glow that four lemons made possible.

Safety: If you ever meet this principle at real-battery scale — a car battery, a swollen phone battery — the rules change completely and the Electronics room's safety lines apply: real batteries can burn, and damaged ones are grown-up, gloves-on problems.

Honestly: The honest output of four lemons is a dim LED and a bright idea. This room counts that as a fair trade and says so, because the next room over turns the bright idea into a working radio.

05 / DIRECTION

North, five ways — graded.

Every direction clue in this section carries a grade, because clues are not equal and pretending they are gets people lost. Learn them in grade order. And notice what makes the A-grade clue work: it only works if you know the sky — which is why this section leans on Tonight's Sky, where the same stars are a clock and a calendar.

The North Star — grade A

The clue that doesn't move

Find the Big Dipper — the saucepan shape, up most of the year from most of the northern hemisphere. The two stars at the front edge of its cup are the pointer stars: draw a line from the bottom pointer through the top one and keep going about five times the distance between them, and you land on Polaris, the North Star. Polaris sits almost exactly over the North Pole, so it hangs in the north all night while every other star wheels around it. Face it and you're facing north; its height above the horizon even roughly equals your latitude. Our banner up top has the Dipper drawn in — that's it, doing its job.

Safety: Star-walking is for standing still. Get your bearing, pick a landmark on the ground in that line — a tree, a rock, a rooftop — and walk to the landmark, not to the star, eyes on the ground in between.

Honestly: Grade A, with the conditions printed on it: northern hemisphere only (there is no bright south pole star), and you need a reasonably clear night. Within those conditions it has never once been wrong.

The phone compass — grade A−

A real instrument, already in your pocket

A phone's compass is not a picture of a compass — inside the phone is a magnetometer, a small hardware sensor that feels the Earth's magnetic field directly, the same physics that swings a needle. That is why it works with no cellular signal, no Wi-Fi, and no data: airplane mode in a dead zone is fine, because what it reads is the planet, not the network. Open the compass, hold the phone flat and still for a few seconds, and the bearing it gives you is a real bearing.

Safety: Use it the way you use the North Star: standing still. Take the bearing, pick a landmark on the ground in that line, and walk to the landmark — a phone screen is a poor thing to watch while crossing rough ground, and every minute it stays lit is battery spent.

Honestly: Grade A−, and the minus is printed on it. It points at magnetic north, not true north — in northwestern Ohio the difference is roughly 7° west, close enough to walk by and worth knowing. Magnets, speakers, and car bodies can bend its reading, so take your bearings away from the car. And its real limit is the battery, not the network: a dead phone is a rock — which is why it shares this section with clues that need no battery at all.

The shadow stick — grade B

North from sunshine and patience

Push a straight stick upright into flat ground in the sun. Mark the tip of its shadow with a pebble. Wait at least fifteen minutes — longer is better — and mark the new shadow tip. The line from the first pebble to the second runs roughly west to east (shadows walk west to east as the sun crosses the sky). Stand with your left foot on the first mark and your right foot on the second, in the northern hemisphere, and you are facing roughly north. It works in any hemisphere with the feet swapped — and Tonight's Sky explains the sun-path that makes it tick.

Safety: This method costs time on purpose. If someone is hurt or weather is coming, "wait fifteen minutes for a bearing" is the wrong tool — use the emergency plan, not the astronomy.

Honestly: Grade B: real geometry, approximate answer. It gives you a north good enough to walk a direction, not good enough to survey a fence line — and on a cloudy day it gives you nothing at all.

The satellite dish — grade B

The moss story's town cousin — with real science under it

Moss is a clue read off the living world; this is the same kind of clue read off the built one. Television satellites sit in geostationary orbit above the equator, parked over one spot on Earth, so a fixed TV dish anywhere in North America has to aim roughly south to see them — that is not folklore, it is orbital geometry, repeated on every rooftop on the street. Read the dishes, find south, and north is simply behind you: the whole compass rose falls out of one rooftop. Where it shines is the country and the aftermath. Out in farm country the dishes are thick on the roofs — cable lines never reached those lanes, so satellite was the television, and a whole township leans one way. And after a disaster, when the street signs are gone and the landmarks with them, the dishes tend to still be standing: bolted to sturdy poles and rooftops, still pointing south over the neighborhood. It even covers the sky clues' blind spot — after a storm it is usually overcast, no North Star and no shadow to stick — but the dishes never needed the sky to be visible. They only needed it once, on the day somebody aimed them. It sounds like a joke until you try it; it has been used, for real, by the writer of this page.

And a dish the storm has already taken is more than a clue — it is a parts shelf. Its cable run and wiring are copper wire of exactly the kind this Guide's pantry battery drinks up, and that the Electronics room's crystal radio bench is built around: weatherproofed, already the right stuff, free for the gathering where it lies. The standing dishes give you south; the wrecked one on the ground keeps working for you afterward, as wire.

Safety: Read dishes from the ground, where you already are. A rooftop is never a direction tool — nobody climbs anything for a bearing — and a quadrant read off the rooftops gets confirmed by a stronger clue before anyone walks a long way on it. Salvage follows the same ownership rule: it is for the ones the storm already took. A dish on a standing house is somebody's property and possibly live equipment — hands off, whatever you need the wire for. And wreckage is worked like wreckage: gloves on, sharp edges everywhere, and a look at what is underneath before you pull anything free.

Honestly: Grade B — and the grade measures usefulness for the job, because that is this section's standard. The job is navigation, and the writer of this page, who has navigated by this clue repeatedly and successfully, states the principle the grade rests on: "Navigation is not a precision thing. It is a 'go thataway' — and you get there." A heading is not a survey line; it is a direction you keep correcting against landmarks as you walk, and a clue that reliably hands you the south half of the sky has done its work. The limits stay printed, because that is this room's rule: "roughly" is doing real work — depending on where you stand and which satellite a dish serves, it can lean well east or west of due south (from Ohio, dishes lean southwest), so it hands you the southern quadrant, not a bearing. And mind the exception that proves the rule: a dish on a motor, or one aimed for radio astronomy — Tonight's Sky has a salvaged dish being aimed at the hydrogen line — points where its work is, not south. The plain fixed TV dish is the clue; anything unusual is not. The salvage has a caveat of its own: not all coax is solid copper. Much of it is copper-clad steel — a skin of copper over a steel core — under an aluminum braid, so the wire you harvest varies from dish to dish. A magnet finds the steel hiding in it: solid copper ignores a magnet, and the clad stuff jumps to one. Test the wire like everything else in this room before you count on it.

Moss on trees — grade D

The weakest clue on the page

The campfire saying goes: moss grows on the north side of trees, because the north side is shadier. Here is what is actually true: moss grows where it stays damp and shaded. On a lone tree in open country, that is often the north side, because the sun dries the south face. But in a real forest, shade comes from other trees, slopes, and streams — so moss grows on whichever side stays wet, which can be any side, or all of them, in a cheerful green coat all the way around the trunk.

Safety: Never navigate by moss alone, and never let it overrule a stronger clue. If the moss and the stars disagree, the moss is wrong — it is a hint to double-check, not an answer.

Honestly: Grade D, and the D is the lesson: this room would rather teach you to grade a clue than to trust a rhyme. Moss tells you where the water lingers. Sometimes that's north. Sometimes it's just a wet tree.

06 / READING SIGNS

Reading signs.

Direction finds north. This section reads the rest of the world's mail. The built and grown world around you is always reporting on itself — what is failing, what is running, what is buried under your feet — in small signs that cost nothing to read once you have learned the patterns. These are an operator's clues, collected the way the direction clues were: each with the pattern that makes it trustworthy and the limits printed on it. A sign is a hint with a pattern, not a verdict. Learn the patterns and the street will talk to you for free, forever.

The flickering streetlight

A lamp reading out its own death notice

The orange streetlights — high-pressure sodium lamps — die in a particular way: they cycle. The lamp strikes, warms up to full brightness over a few minutes, drops out, sits dark while it cools, and then restrikes and does it all again, on a period of minutes, regular as a metronome. A failing ballast flickers instead — rapid and uneven, a light that cannot hold its nerve. Either way the lamp is telling you something true: it is at the end of its life, and that corner will be dark soon. On a strained grid — a heat wave, the days after a storm — a street full of cycling lights is the grid clearing its throat, and worth noticing for that too.

Safety: A dying light over your walking route is a promise that will not be kept: carry your own light, and do not plan a night route around a corner whose lamp has started cycling.

Honestly: The rhythm is the tell. One flicker can be a loose connection, a branch worrying the line, a truck going past. And the newer white LED streetlights fail differently — their drivers flicker, or they dim, or they simply go out and stay out. Read the pattern over minutes, not from a single glance.

Lights that breathe like clockwork

A voltmeter you read from the porch

When a heavy motor starts — a well pump, an air-conditioning compressor, shop equipment — its first gulp of current is enormous, and the voltage on everything sharing its circuit or transformer sags for a moment. When the motor stops, the voltage recovers. So if something big nearby is cycling on and off, the house lights breathe at that machine's duty cycle: dimming at every start, brightening at every stop, regular enough to time with a watch. What it reports is something big running on a cycle, somewhere near. At home it is a diagnosis you get for free: a well pump that should start every ten minutes but is starting every minute is short-cycling — a real fault, quietly wearing the pump out — and the lights told you without anyone opening a panel.

Safety: Gentle breathing is information. Hard dimming with buzzing, one outlet flickering while the others hold, or a panel that feels warm is a fault, not a sign — that is electrician territory, and the inside of a panel is not a place for curiosity.

Honestly: A gentle, aimless wandering of brightness can just be the utility's voltage drifting, which it does all day. The clockwork regularity — same period, same depth, again and again — is what points at one cycling load. Count the period before you name the culprit.

Darker grass

A soil test growing in public

A patch or strip of grass that is noticeably darker, lusher, and faster-growing than everything around it is reporting nitrogen-rich soil underneath. Grass does that over a septic leach field, where the treated water feeds it all season; where animals habitually go; where a bag of fertilizer once spilled. What it tells you is genuinely useful in both directions: it draws you a map of where the septic field lies — ground you should not dig, not park anything heavy on, and keep at a sensible distance from the well — and it marks the fed ground, if you are choosing where to plant something hungry.

Safety: A septic field is not a garden bed. Hands get washed after digging anywhere near one, and food gardens keep their distance from it — what feeds the grass is not what you want feeding the tomatoes.

Honestly: Dark alone is not proof: moisture, shade, and a different species of grass all darken a patch. The nitrogen signature is the three together — dark and lush and fast, in a shape that matches something underneath. Like moss in the direction section, it is a hint to check, not a verdict.

The street is labeled

A locate crew's handwriting — and the code is public

The colored paint lines and little flags that appear on pavement and grass are a locate crew marking what is buried, before somebody digs. The colors are not decoration; they are a code — the Uniform Color Code — and anyone can learn it: red is electric power, yellow is gas and oil, orange is communications (phone, cable, fiber), blue is drinking water, green is sewer and drain, white is where the digging is proposed, and pink is temporary survey marks. Read a painted street and you know what runs under that exact spot — and that a dig is coming.

Safety: Paint on the ground means somebody plans to dig, and it exists for one reason: digging blind is how gas lines get hit. It is why you call 811 — free, a few days ahead — before your own post hole, fence, or tree goes in. The marks on someone else's street are the system working; get your own before you dig.

Honestly: Marks are a locate, not an X-ray. They are approximate — the real line can run a forearm's width or more to either side of the paint — they fade and get mowed over, and they only cover the utilities' own lines: the wire out to your garage and your sprinkler pipes are yours, and nobody paints those.

Snow melts in a pattern

Winter's version of the dark-grass card

The dark-grass card reads the ground in green; winter lets you read it in white. After a snowfall or a hard frost, the places that melt first are reporting heat from below: snow gone in a neat rectangle over a septic tank, a bare stripe along a buried warm pipe, a roof patch that clears while the rest stays white, where insulation is missing and house heat is escaping straight through it. A row of icicles hanging off one roof in a row of clean ones tells the same story from the other side — leaking heat melts the snowpack, and the meltwater refreezes at the cold edge. What it reports: where buried things run, and which houses are heating the outdoors all winter.

Safety: Read melt patterns from the ground, like everything in this section. A melt stripe is a hint about what is buried, not permission to dig or drive stakes on it — and icicles over a doorway are a falling hazard, not a decoration.

Honestly: Sun and wind melt unevenly too — a south-facing slope and a wind-scoured ridge go bare early for free. The buried-heat signature is a shape with edges: a rectangle, a straight stripe, one roof in a snowy row. Pattern, not patchiness.

The rotten-egg smell

An alarm somebody installed on purpose

Natural gas, as it comes out of the ground, has no smell at all. The rotten-egg stink is mercaptan, a chemical the gas company adds on purpose, in tiny amounts, so that your nose becomes the leak detector. That makes this the one sign in the section you do not interpret — you obey it. It has a second, quieter face: a round patch of dead grass or dying plants in an otherwise healthy lawn can be an underground leak poisoning the roots, the gas crowding the air out of the soil. Treat the circle seriously even when there is no smell at all — an outdoor leak may never reach your nose.

Safety: If you smell gas: do not flip switches, do not light anything, do not use a phone anywhere near it. Get everyone out and away, and call the gas company or 911 from outside, or from a neighbour's house. Nothing in the building is worth going back for. And the dead-grass circle gets reported, not investigated — never dig it up to check; checking is the gas company's job, with their instruments.

Honestly: The smell can also be a stove burner left on unlit, a pilot light out, or genuinely rotten eggs in a drain — and the rule does not care. You do not grade this sign; you act on it and let the utility do the grading. A false alarm is the cheap outcome, every time.

A streetlight burning at noon

The light is fine. Its eye has failed.

A streetlight blazing away in broad daylight is usually not a broken lamp at all. On top of the fixture sits a small sensor — a photocell — whose whole job is telling the light when it is dark. When the photocell fails, or gets covered over, the light believes it is midnight at noon, and burns. It is a small, charming, true sign, and it teaches the biggest lesson on this shelf: every automatic thing has a sensor, and sensors are what fail. The machine is fine; its information about the world is wrong.

Safety: A light that never sleeps burns its life away fast, so report it — most poles carry the utility's number — while it is merely wrong, instead of after it has died and left the corner dark. The same failure on your own porch light is worth checking before you replace a bulb that was never the problem.

Honestly: Once in a while a daytime light is deliberate — a crew testing after a repair, a fixture running on a timer. One noon sighting is a note; the same light burning at noon every day for a week is a failed eye.

Water running on a dry day

A leak uphill, announcing itself

A gutter, a street, a ditch running wet — or icy — in weather that did not make it is water escaping from somewhere uphill: a water main or a service line letting go underground, often days before it erupts through the pavement. The reading skill is to follow it uphill to where it starts, clearest and highest. In a disaster this sign does double duty, because it is how you find water and trouble with the same eyes: a broken main is a contamination risk and a road undermined — and it is also, undeniably, where the water is.

Safety: A main break can wash the ground out from under a street that still looks whole — never wade or drive into a flooded stretch whose bottom you cannot see, and report running water to the water utility. And break water is untreated water, whatever it looks like: the water rule (Section 02) applies to it in full.

Honestly: It could be a sump pump doing its legal job, a neighbour's drainage, a hydrant a crew just flushed. Follow it uphill before you conclude: pump water comes in pulses and stops; a leak runs at three in the morning, with nobody home and nothing cycling.

The pole that hums wrong

Equipment talking in its sleep — and then not

Transformers hum. A low, steady hum from the can on the pole is a working sound, as normal as a refrigerator's. Trouble sounds different: the hum grows loud or changes pitch, a hard buzzing or crackling sets in — worse in wet weather — or blue flashes flicker at night. That is arcing: electricity jumping a gap it was never meant to cross, insulation failing, equipment in the act of breaking. The pole has been reporting its health all along; the wrong hum is it reporting a fever.

Safety: Enjoy this one entirely from a distance. Never touch a pole, the thin ground wire running down it, or anything hanging from the lines — and a downed line can energize the ground around it, so keep a bus-length back and report it. Treat everything near a fallen line as live until the utility says otherwise, because it is.

Honestly: Some hum variation is just load and weather — the loudest hum of the year, on the hottest evening, is a transformer working hard, not necessarily dying. Crackle, flashes, and smoke are the unmissable ones. Between the two, the rule matches the gas card: report it, and let the utility grade it.

Reading an outage's shape

The shape is the diagnosis

When the lights go out, the shape of the darkness is the first report. One dark house on a lit street: the problem is that house's — a tripped breaker, a failed service drop, its own equipment. A whole street dark: the problem is the grid's — a fuse, a transformer, a feeder. And traffic lights out intersection after intersection maps the outage's footprint better than any news report: the boundary between the lit blocks and the dark ones is roughly where the trouble lives. This is the clockwork card's big sibling — dimming was the grid whispering; darkness is it stating, in a shape you can read from the porch.

Safety: Two rules ride on every outage. A generator belongs outside, well away from windows — a dark house with a generator in the garage is a carbon-monoxide emergency waiting for a closed door. And a dark traffic light is a four-way stop, whatever the other drivers think. If your house is the dark one and you find a damaged meter, a scorched smell, or a downed service line, that is the utility and 911, not the breaker panel.

Honestly: A dark house might just be an empty house, and a dark street might be a planned outage the utility announced last week. The shape is a first read — strongest at the hours when that street is normally lit and lived-in, and always worth confirming before you repeat it as news.

07 / THE REMEDIES SHELF

What the woods offer — and what it doesn't.

Read the shelf's rule first: the medicine shelf is the last resort, not the first. First aid (next section) and real help come first, always. What lives here is traditional knowledge — the things people reached for when there was nothing else — and each card is written in three honest parts: the traditional use, what the evidence actually says, and the limits. A remedy with its limits printed on it is a tool. A remedy without them is a story.

Banana slug slime

The numb patch

Traditional use: The banana slug — the big yellow slug of the Pacific Northwest coast forests — has been handled for generations with the knowledge that its slime numbs: Indigenous peoples of the coast are recorded letting slugs' slime soothe nettle stings and sore spots, and Northwest kids have tested the numb tongue-tip ever since.

What the evidence says: The numbing is real. Researchers who studied banana slug mucus found it contains compounds with a mild anesthetic, pain-dulling effect — the slug makes its own gentle novocaine, probably to discourage predators. "Mild" is the load-bearing word: it takes the edge off a sting; it does nothing for a sprain, a burn, or anything deeper than skin.

Limits: A curiosity and a comfort for a nettle sting when nothing better is at hand — nothing more.

Safety: This is a look-and-learn card, not a lick-a-slug card. Wild slugs and snails can carry parasites — including the rat lungworm, which is a genuinely nasty infection — and slug slime on hands goes nowhere near mouths, eyes, or open cuts. Admire the chemistry, wash your hands after any handling, and let the slug go back to its log.

Honestly: The evidence here is "studied compounds, mild effect" — interesting and true, and about three strength-levels below anything in a first-aid kit. The slug is a science lesson with a numb patch, and that's a fine thing to be.

Willow bark

Aspirin's ancestor

Traditional use: Willow bark tea is one of the oldest recorded remedies on Earth — ancient Egyptian and Greek texts describe it for pain and fever, and Hippocrates recommended it. If you have ever heard "tree bark for a headache," this is the bark they meant.

What the evidence says: Willow bark contains salicin, which your body converts into salicylic acid — the chemical family that chemists later refined into aspirin. This one is not folklore with a lab coat on: the active ingredient is identified, measured, and genuinely pain-relieving, which is exactly why it carries aspirin's cautions too.

Limits: Bark is an unmeasured dose — the salicin in a cup of tea varies with the tree, the season, and the brewing. A pharmacy tablet is the same idea, measured. The bark is for understanding, and for true nothing-else situations.

Safety: Same family as aspirin means same warnings: not for children or teenagers with fever or a viral illness (the Reye's syndrome risk that keeps aspirin away from kids), not for anyone allergic to aspirin, and not for anyone on blood thinners or with a bleeding problem. When in doubt: don't.

Honestly: The strongest evidence on this shelf — and notice that the stronger the evidence, the longer the safety line. That's not a coincidence; that's how real medicine behaves, and this shelf grades itself accordingly.

Broadleaf plantain leaf

The sting-and-bite poultice

Traditional use: Not the banana — Plantago major, the flat, ribbed-leaf weed in every lawn and trail edge on several continents. Chewed or crushed into a poultice and pressed on bee stings, bites, and nettle rash, it is probably the most widely used folk first-aid plant in the world, carried everywhere European settlers went and adopted everywhere it landed.

What the evidence says: Plantain leaf contains compounds (including allantoin and aucubin) with plausible soothing and anti-inflammatory action, and it has a long, consistent record of taking the fire out of minor stings. The formal evidence is modest — small studies, lots of tradition, no dramatic trials.

Limits: Minor stings and bites only. A sting with any sign of allergic reaction — swelling spreading, trouble breathing, dizziness — is not a plantain situation; it's a first-aid-section, call-for-help situation.

Safety: Identification is the whole safety rule: learn plantain's ribbed, parallel-veined leaves with an adult who knows them, use only leaves from ground that hasn't been sprayed, rinse the leaf, and skip it entirely if you're not sure what you're holding. Some people's skin dislikes new plants — test on a small patch first.

Honestly: "Modest evidence, low risk, long tradition" — plantain is the honest middle of this shelf: probably helps a little, very unlikely to hurt when identified right, and never a reason to skip real care for a real reaction.

Oak bark

The astringent — not the headache bark

Traditional use: Oak bark, simmered into a strong tea, was the household astringent: dabbed on weepy skin and minor scrapes, gargled for sore gums. If someone in your family swears a bark fixed a headache, they are remembering willow (the card above) wearing oak's coat — the two get confused constantly, and telling them apart is a tidy little lesson in why names matter.

What the evidence says: Oak bark is loaded with tannins — the puckering compounds in strong tea and acorns. Tannins genuinely tighten and dry tissue surfaces, which is a real, physical, astringent effect you can feel in one sip of over-steeped tea. European herbal regulators have accepted oak bark for minor skin inflammation and mouth-and-gum irritation on the strength of that long traditional use.

Limits: Surface uses only, short-term. It does nothing for pain inside the body, and it was never the headache remedy — that's willow's card, with willow's warnings.

Safety: Tannins are strong medicine in the literal sense: oak preparations don't belong on broken or large areas of skin, aren't for drinking regularly, and aren't for small children. External, brief, and sure of your identification — or not at all.

Honestly: A real effect (astringency) attached to a modest job (minor surface irritation). The oak card earns its place on the shelf mostly by teaching the difference between "does something" and "does what you need" — the most useful distinction in all of first aid.

08 / FIRST AID — THE ANCHOR

First aid: help until help arrives.

This is the section the rest of the room exists to support. The steps below follow the American Red Cross and Scouts BSA practice, and the references are on the shelf at the bottom of the page. Two rules stand over every card: check the scene is safe before you touch anyone, and call 911 early — calling is a first-aid skill, not an admission of defeat. These cards are memory-joggers for skills learned properly, with hands, from instructors. The Everyday Body room explains the machinery these cards are protecting.

Severe bleeding

Pressure first. Always pressure first.

  1. Call 911 (or send someone specific: "You — call 911").
  2. Find the bleeding and press on it: a dressing, a cloth, a t-shirt, your hands — firm, steady, direct pressure, and don't lift to peek. Blood soaking through? Add another pad on top; never remove the first.
  3. How much blood is life-threatening? The Red Cross measure: about half a soda can's worth — less in a small child — or blood that flows continuously or spurts.
  4. For life-threatening bleeding on an arm or leg, a tourniquet (if you have one and know how) goes 2–3 inches above the wound, never on a joint, tightened until the bleeding stops. Note the time. Never loosen it — that is a hospital job.
  5. Keep the person warm and still, and stay with them until help takes over.

Safety: Gloves if you have them; if you don't and the bleeding is life-threatening, help anyway and wash thoroughly after. Press around — never pull out — anything embedded in a wound.

Honestly: Direct pressure handles nearly every bleed you will ever meet. The tourniquet is for the rare one that is trying to kill in under five minutes — knowing the difference is the skill, and pressure is always step one either way.

Burns

Cool water. Nothing else on it.

  1. Stop the burning: get the person away from the source; smother flames or remove them from it.
  2. Cool the burn under cool running water — lots of it, until the pain eases. (Red Cross wording: cool, not cold.)
  3. Cover loosely with a sterile dressing or clean cloth.
  4. Serious burns — large, deep, on the face, hands, feet, or joints, or on a small child — are a 911 call.

Safety: No ice (it damages the skin further), no butter, no ointments, no home remedies on a fresh burn — the remedies shelf stays on the shelf for this one. Don't peel off anything stuck to the burn.

Honestly: Cooling with water is not the old-fashioned option; it is the entire modern treatment for a minor burn, and everything else people reach for makes the injury worse or dirtier. Boring and correct beats traditional and harmful.

Sprains

RICE: rest, ice, compression, elevation

  1. Rest it — stop using the joint; a sprained ankle that gets walked on becomes a worse sprained ankle.
  2. Ice — cold pack, cooler ice, snow, or cold stream water — about 20 minutes at a time, with a cloth between ice and skin.
  3. Compression — a snug elastic bandage, firm but never numb-making; loosen it if fingers or toes tingle, pale, or cool.
  4. Elevation — prop it above heart level when you can.

Safety: If the limb looks bent wrong, can't bear any weight, or the pain is severe and focused on one bony point, treat it as a possible fracture: splint it as it lies and get real help instead of walking it out.

Honestly: The Scouts' own kit advice skips instant ice packs — heavy, and good for only about 15 minutes. A cooler, a stream, or snow does the job better. RICE manages a sprain; it does not grade one, and a bad "sprain" that isn't improving in a day or two deserves an X-ray.

Choking

Coughing first, then 5 and 5

  1. If the person can cough, speak, or breathe, encourage the cough and stay close — a working cough beats anything you can do.
  2. If they can't — silent, clutching their throat, weak cough — call 911 and start the Red Cross sequence: 5 back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, leaning them forward.
  3. Then 5 abdominal thrusts: fist above the navel, pull in and up, sharp and separate.
  4. Alternate 5 and 5 until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive — then it's CPR, and the 911 call matters most of all.

Safety: This sequence is for adults and children over 1 year. Babies get back blows and chest thrusts instead — never abdominal thrusts — and that, like CPR, is a learn-it-in-a-class skill, not a learn-it-from-a-webpage one.

Honestly: A webpage can hold the order of the steps; it cannot put the force in your hands. The sequence is here so that, in the moment, your memory has something to stand on — the class is where the skill actually comes from.

Heat exhaustion vs heat stroke

One is a warning. One is the emergency.

Heat exhaustion looks like: heavy sweating, pale and cool or clammy skin, nausea, dizziness, headache, a fast weak pulse. Care: stop, move to shade or air conditioning, loosen clothing, cool wet cloths, small sips of water. If they vomit, get worse, or aren't clearly better within about 30 minutes, treat it as the emergency below.

Heat stroke looks like: skin hot and red — and often dry, the sweating stopped — a very high body temperature, confusion, slurred speech, or unresponsiveness. This is the body failing to cool itself, and it kills.

  1. Call 911 immediately.
  2. Cool them fast, by any means you have: cold water immersion is best; otherwise soak the whole body with water and fan, or cold wet cloths over the whole body, changed constantly.
  3. Do not give fluids to someone confused or unresponsive.

Safety: With heat stroke, cooling is not comfort care — it is the treatment, and minutes count. Cool first, transport second, whenever help is on the way and you can keep cooling.

Honestly: The names sound like points on one scale, which makes heat stroke easy to underestimate. Learn the tell instead: sweating that stops in the heat, plus a mind that isn't working right, is never "just tired."

Hypothermia

Cold, handled gently

Signs: shivering (which eventually, ominously, stops), fumbling hands, slurred speech, drowsiness, confusion — the "umbles": stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. Care: get them out of the wind and wet; remove wet clothing; insulate them from the ground and wrap them, head included, in dry blankets or a sleeping bag; warm the center of the body first. Warm, sweet drinks are fine if the person is fully awake and can swallow normally. Body-to-body warming in a shared sleeping bag is a real, standard technique.

Safety: Handle a hypothermic person gently — a cold heart is an irritable heart, so no rough jostling, no marching them around, no rubbing cold limbs, and no alcohol, ever. Moderate or worse cases are a 911 call; rewarming someone who has stopped shivering is a hospital job.

Honestly: The trap in hypothermia is that the person often insists they are fine — the condition attacks the judgment you'd use to notice it. Believe the signs over the person, including when the person is you.

CPR: learn it in a class

The one card that refuses to teach

This card will not teach you CPR, on purpose. CPR is compressions at the right depth, at the right rate, in the right place — a physical skill that lives in your arms, not your eyes, and a webpage that "taught" it in a paragraph would be lying to you in the room's own honesty language. What this card can do: tell you that if an adult collapses and isn't breathing normally, the steps are call 911, send for an AED, and push hard and fast in the center of the chest — and that hands-only CPR from an untrained bystander saves lives compared to standing by. Then it points you at the people who teach it properly, in a few hours, with practice manikins and an instructor's hands correcting yours: the American Red Cross CPR/AED classes.

Safety: In the real moment: an unresponsive person who isn't breathing gets the 911 call first and compressions second — imperfect CPR beats perfect hesitation, every study agrees.

Honestly: Scouts BSA agrees with this card: the First Aid merit badge requires demonstrating CPR on an approved training device, in person, to a counselor. Some skills are certified by hands or not at all, and this room respects the difference.

The kit

Build it, know it, check it

Scouting's kit wisdom in one line: a kit you built yourself beats a kit you bought, because you know what's in it and where — and if you buy one, empty it out and repack it before it ever goes on a trail. Tick things off as you pack yours. The count is kept only on this page, nowhere else.

0 of 18 packed.

Safety: Check the kit before every outing: medicines expire, ointments get used, bandages get damp. A kit with last year's leftovers is a story about a kit. Clean wounds with clean drinking water — Scouting's current word is to skip the hydrogen peroxide; it kills germs and living tissue alike and slows healing.

Honestly: The kit is the least important part of first aid. It is a box of supplies for skills — the skills are the point, the kit just keeps them from depending on a torn t-shirt. (A torn t-shirt, pressed hard, is still a fine dressing. See card one.)

Survival writing that hides its weak tips among its strong ones gets people hurt; this room would rather be trusted about the strong ones because it was straight about the weak ones.

Safety says how it hurts. Honesty says how well it works.

Fire, honestly

Skills in grade order, like the clues

Dead out and cold out, every time

The water rule

The biggest type on the page, on purpose

Filtering and making safe are different jobs. The layered filter removes grit, murk, and some tastes; germs pass through it untouched, so filtered water is still untreated water. Boiling — a full rolling boil for 1 minute, 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, per the CDC — is the step that makes water safe, and it does nothing about chemical contamination. If a source in this room ever seems to blur that line, the big callout box in Section 02 is the room's official position.

Filter for clear. Boil for safe.

Last resort, labelled as one
