# Sources & limitations — The Survival Guide

This room teaches safety-critical skills, so it names exactly what it stands on. The first-aid and safety content was written against the following references, and the Links Out shelf ships only links that were **opened and read live on 19 November 2026** — all six passed; none were dropped.

## References used (the room's authorities)

- **American Red Cross — Life-threatening bleeding** — https://production.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external — direct pressure first; the life-threatening measure (about half a soda can of blood, or continuous flow/spurting); tourniquet and wound packing for limb bleeding; call 911; care for shock. Opened and read in full, 2026-11-19.
- **American Red Cross — How to apply a tourniquet** (published Nov 27, 2024) — https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/articles/how-to-apply-a-tourniquet — placement 2–3 inches above the wound, never on a joint; tighten until bleeding stops; never loosen or remove; a second tourniquet above the first if needed; use direct pressure while preparing. Opened and read in full, 2026-11-19.
- **American Red Cross — first-aid guidance generally** (take-a-class first-aid pages and reference answers) — burns cooled with cool running water, **no ice**; the choking sequence for a conscious child or adult of **5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts**, repeated; the CHECK–CALL–CARE emergency order. Verified against Red Cross material, 2026-11-19.
- **Scouting America — First Aid merit badge** — https://www.scouting.org/merit-badges/First-Aid/ — kit contents and kit practice: build or fully repack your own kit; bandages, moleskin, tweezers, antiseptic towelettes, antibiotic ointment, ibuprofen, antihistamines, and a first-aid manual belong in it; instant ice packs are heavy and good for ~15 minutes (coolers, streams, and snow do better); clean wounds with clean drinking water and **skip hydrogen peroxide** (it damages tissue and slows healing); keep the kit current. Opened and read in full, 2026-11-19. The same page confirms CPR must be demonstrated on an approved training device — the basis for this room's "learn it in a class" card.
- **Smokey Bear (USDA Forest Service) — Campfire safety** — https://smokeybear.com/campfire-safety — check fire restrictions and weather; 15 feet from tents, vegetation, and low branches; clear a 10-foot diameter; the teepee, lean-to, and log cabin lays; never use flammable liquids; keep the fire small; extinguish by **drown, stir, drown, feel** — "if it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave." Opened and read in full, 2026-11-19.
- **CDC — water disinfection guidance** — https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/page/water-disinfection — boiling is the surest method: a full rolling boil for **1 minute**, **3 minutes above 6,500 feet**; the measured household-bleach method for when boiling is impossible; chemical methods are weak against *Cryptosporidium*, so filtering cloudy water first matters. Verified against CDC guidance, 2026-11-19.

## The remedies shelf — sourcing note

The remedy cards state their evidence level on the card, and that is deliberate: banana slug mucus has been studied in laboratories (mild anesthetic compounds identified); willow bark's salicin is established pharmacology (the aspirin family, with the aspirin cautions — including the Reye's syndrome warning that keeps it from children and teens with fever or viral illness); broadleaf plantain's evidence is modest and mostly traditional; oak bark's astringent tannins are real, with long traditional use for minor skin and gum irritation. None of the four is dosed or prescribed here, and the shelf's standing rule — first aid and real help first — governs all of them.

## Knots — sources (added 20 November 2026)

The Knots section's strength figures are **knot efficiency**: the breaking strength of the knotted rope as a percentage of the same rope unknotted, from published break tests. Every figure on a card is an "about" range spanning the sources below, because the sources genuinely disagree — rope fibre and construction, wet or dry, how the knot was dressed, and how fast it was loaded all move the number. The cards plan on the low end.

- **The general frame — Wikipedia, "Knot" (Strength)** — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot — knotted rope almost always fails at or near the knot; the efficiency of common knots ranges between **40% and 80%** of the rope's original strength; precise values are hard to pin down for the reasons above; prudent users allow a large safety margin, and working load limits carry safety factors up to 15:1 for critical applications. Read 2026-11-20. The section's "roughly half to two-thirds" planning figure sits inside this range, on its cautious side.
- **Richards, Dave (Technical Director, Cordage Institute), "Knot Break Strength vs. Rope Break Strength"** — https://www.mountainguides.com/pdf/prodeals/Knot_Breaking_Strength_vs_Rope_Break_Strength.pdf — five breaks per knot per rope, all knots tied by one person, in 12.5 mm static life-safety rope, 10.5 mm dynamic climbing rope, and 7 mm accessory cord. Mean efficiencies across the three ropes: **bowline about 63–67%**; **figure-eight (end) about 69–75%**; **figure-eight (bight) about 70–78%**; **sheet bend about 50–61%**; **double sheet bend about 53–60%**. Also the source for the sheet bend's behaviour card: in the dynamic-rope tests the sheet bend **slipped and pulled out without backup knots**, and the double sheet bend slipped heavily — the basis for the card's "use the double, leave real tails" safety line. Read in full 2026-11-20.
- **McKently, John, "Rescue Knot Efficiency Revisited" (International Technical Rescue Symposium, 2014), reporting the CMC Rescue tests of 1987** — https://verticalsection.caves.org/nh/59/Rescue%20Knot%20Efficiency%20Revisited.pdf — in ½″ nylon rescue rope: **figure-eight loop 80%** efficiency, **figure-eight bend 81%**, **bowline 67%**. The same report quotes Hudson & Vines, *High Angle Rescue Techniques*: bowline 73–74%, figure-eight 78–80%. These are the high ends of the bowline and figure-eight ranges quoted on the cards. Read 2026-11-20.
- **Jacobson, Cliff, *Knots for the Outdoors* — the classic breaking-strength chart** (figures derived from Plymouth Cordage, 1946, and tests by Scovell, Miller, Dent, Trumpler, and Day, as reported in Day, *The Art of Knotting and Splicing*, 1970) — **square (reef) knot 45%**, **two half hitches 75%**, **clove hitch 75%**, **bowline 60%**. The sole source for the square knot, two half hitches, and clove hitch figures — the cards say "the classic chart" when they quote them, because no modern test series in the sources above includes those three. Read via the book's online edition, 2026-11-20.
- **Tying steps** follow the standard published forms as taught in the Scout handbook tradition (the square knot, bowline, figure-eight, taut-line hitch, clove hitch, two half hitches, and sheet bend are the classic Scout knots; the square lashing is the standard campcraft lashing). No new technique is introduced; where a knot has variants, the card teaches the common form and says what it leaves out.
- **Step-through diagrams (added 20 November 2026):** original schematic line drawings in the room's diagram style, one stage per numbered step on each card, staged to the card's own text. The staging follows the same standard published sequences as the tying steps above, cross-checked against **Animated Knots by Grog** — the bowline (https://www.animatedknots.com/bowlineknot: small loop, end up through it, around behind the standing part, back down, pull the standing part to set) and the sheet bend (https://www.animatedknots.com/sheet-bend-knot: bight in the thicker rope, thinner end up through it, behind both legs, tucked under itself, both tails finishing on the same side; the double taken twice around) — read 2026-11-20. The drawings are schematics, not photographs or traced frames: rope paths and over/under crossings follow the sequence, simplified to stay legible at card width, and the section intro says on the page that a diagram is not a teacher.
- **Rope figures:** true Type III "550" paracord's ~550 lb minimum breaking strength is definitional (it is what the 550 names); the ~110 lb working load is the commonly quoted manufacturer guideline (about a fifth of breaking strength), quoted on the card as a maker's rule of thumb, not a standard. The trucker's hitch's 3-to-1 is the ideal three-segment figure from Everyday Engineering's pulley bench; the card's "nearer 2-to-1 in the field" is the friction correction, stated as an estimate, not a measurement.

## Limitations

- **This room is education, not certification.** First-aid steps are simplified memory-joggers of Red Cross / Scouts BSA practice as of November 2026; guidance changes, and hands-on classes (the Red Cross class finder is linked) are the real qualification. In an emergency, call 911 (or the local emergency number) — no page outranks a dispatcher.
- **Numbers kept conservative:** the bleeding card uses the Red Cross's own "half a soda can" measure; the boiling times are the CDC's; tourniquet placement is the Red Cross's 2–3 inches. Where authorities phrase things slightly differently, the room follows the Red Cross wording and says so.
- **Identification is a skill this page cannot give.** The plant cards assume identification learned with a knowledgeable adult; the room says so on every plant card.
- **Knot figures are ranges, not ratings.** The efficiency percentages in the Knots section come from the test series named above, in the ropes those series used. A different rope — older, wetter, slicker, thinner — moves them, generally downward. They are for choosing the right knot and respecting the cost, never for computing a load a person depends on; the section's standing rule (no climbing, no life support) has no exceptions.
