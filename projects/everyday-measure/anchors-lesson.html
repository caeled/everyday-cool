Anchors — the lesson

The game asks you to estimate sixteen real things — a mouse, a Moon, a pyramid — with nothing but a shelf of reference sizes and your own judgement. This page is the method it is teaching: what an anchor is, why estimation is scored in ratios, how to chain small certainties into enormous ones, and exactly how the game itself is built.

▶ Play Anchors

What an anchor is

An anchor is a measurement you carry. Not a number you memorised for a test — a size attached to a thing you can hold, stand next to, or pour. A nickel is 5 grams, exactly, because the US Mint makes it so; once you know that, every small mass in the world has a handle: it is some number of nickels. A doorway is about 2 metres; a giraffe is about 5.5 of your metres stood up; a bathtub is about 300 litres. The room this game lives in, Everyday Measure's Feel It bench, puts it plainly: nobody feels a number; everybody feels a thing.

Anchors turn an impossible question — how much does a 747 weigh? — into an arithmetic question: how many elephants is that? Thirty, as it happens. You will never hold a 747. You might stand next to an elephant. The anchor is the bridge between the sizes you know in your body and the sizes you will only ever meet as numbers.

Why the score is a ratio

Most games score the gap: truth minus estimate. Estimation can't. Miss a mouse (20 g) by 10 grams and you are 50% wrong; miss a car (1,860 kg) by 10 grams and you are right to five decimal places. The same gap is a blunder in one world and perfection in the other, because estimation error is multiplicative — it scales with the thing being estimated. So Anchors scores the factor:

factor = max(estimate ÷ truth, truth ÷ estimate)  ·  points = round( 100 × (1 − log₁₀(factor) ÷ 2) )

The factor is symmetric — twice too big and half as big are the same size of wrong — and the points slide down the logarithm, because equal ratios of error should cost equal points:

FactorBandPointsWhat it means
×1Whisker100Dead on.
×1.15Whisker97Within a whisker — the band's edge.
×1.5Close91Half again, or two-thirds. A good working estimate.
×2Same neighbourhood85Double or half. Wrong, but recognisably about this thing.
×10Right order of magnitude50The power of ten is right. In Fermi work, that is a result, not a failure.
×100Different universe0Two powers of ten out. The estimate describes something else.

That last-but-one row is the honest heart of the game: the order of magnitude is the first truth. An estimate of the Moon's distance that lands within ×10 has the right number of digits and most of the physics; it will steer a real decision correctly far more often than a precise answer to the wrong question.

Fermi estimation: why the method works

The technique is named for Enrico Fermi, who famously estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb test by dropping scraps of paper and watching how far the blast wave pushed them — and who set his students problems like how many piano tuners are in Chicago? The method: break the unknown into factors you can anchor, estimate each one, and multiply. (Wikipedia's Fermi problem page tells the piano-tuner story in full, and its order of magnitude page is the ratio idea written as mathematics.)

Why doesn't the error explode as you chain factors? Because your errors don't all lean the same way. One anchor you overestimate, the next you underestimate, and in the multiplication the pushes partly cancel — in log space, the errors add like a random walk, growing far more slowly than the count of steps. A chain of five factors, each good to ×1.5, usually lands within about ×2–3 of the truth, not ×7.6. That is why the game's proof matters: a scripted estimator using nothing but the game's own anchor chains lands inside the ×2 band on every challenge. The chains suffice. The game is winnable by the method it teaches.

Chaining anchors: the unreachable scales

No anchor is a pyramid. But anchors compose: a pyramid is some number of elephants, an elephant is some number of tonnes, a tonne is a thousand litres of water, and a litre of water is two hundred nickels — and nickels you can hold. Round 4 of the game makes you build the estimate exactly that way: pick an anchor, pick a count, multiply.

Two worked chains, from the game's own cards:

The Olympic pool, in bathtubs

The pool's truth is 2,500,000 L (the World Aquatics (formerly FINA) minimum, from the room's comparison shelf). A full bathtub is about 300 L. Chain: 8,000 tubs × 300 L = 2,400,000 L — a factor of 2,500,000 ÷ 2,400,000 ≈ ×1.04 from the truth. A Whisker, worth 99 points, built from two numbers a person can actually picture: a tub, and "about eight thousand of them."

The Great Pyramid, in elephants

The pyramid's stone is estimated at about 5,750,000 tonnes. An elephant is about 6 tonnes of animal. 5,750,000 ÷ 6 ≈ 958,333 elephants — call it a million elephants and you are at a factor of ×1.04 again. Absurd picture, sound arithmetic. That is not a bug in the method; it is the method. The picture is how a human mind holds a number that size at all.

Worked examples from the game

The mouse and the nickels

Truth: 20 g. The nickel anchor is 5 g, so the game reports the truth as about 4 nickels — computed as 20 ÷ 5, not written by hand. Say you estimated 50 g: your factor is 50 ÷ 20 = ×2.5, the band is Right order of magnitude, and the points are round(100 × (1 − log₁₀(2.5) ÷ 2)) = round(100 × 0.801) = 80. A 30-gram miss on a 20-gram mouse still earns 80 — because proportionally, you were in the mouse's world.

The pencil and the hand

Truth: 19 cm. The hand-width anchor comes from the room itself: a metre is a yard plus about 3.4 inches, and that difference is "roughly the width of your hand across the knuckles" — so a hand's width is about 8.6 cm. 19 ÷ 8.6 ≈ 2.2 hand-widths. Two hands laid along a pencil and a bit: that is a measurement you can make in a classroom with no ruler in the building.

The 747 and the elephants

Truth: about 180 tonnes, empty. 180 ÷ 6 = 30 elephants exactly. Some estimates are famous because the anchor makes them unforgettable — nobody who has pictured thirty elephants parked in a hangar ever again wonders what a 747 weighs, even roughly. Roughly is the point.

Your stride is an anchor

The most personal anchor is your own pace. A pace is a length you carry everywhere, and like any instrument it must be calibrated: walk a known distance — a football field, 100 paces counted — and your stride becomes a number you own. After that, every field, trail, and parking lot you ever cross is measurable with your legs. The Map Room's game Go Thataway is built entirely on that one anchor: navigate by bearing and pace count, correct at landmarks, arrive. Estimation and navigation are the same skill wearing different boots — know your unit, chain it honestly, and correct as you go.

How it's built

One HTML file, no libraries, no network. The game keeps three things as data: the ANCHORS table (name, family, value in the family's base unit, source), the CHALLENGES table (prompt, stored truth, the unit you answer in, a designated anchor, and a chain count), and one pure scoring function. Everything you read after an estimate is computed from those tables at the moment you lock in:

· The factor and points come from scoreEstimate(truth, estimate) — the same function the headless proof exercises.
· The "truth in anchors" sentence is truth ÷ anchor value, computed — it is not text anyone typed per card, so it cannot drift from the stored truth.
· The anchor shelf beside the game is rendered from the same ANCHORS table the scoring uses. The shelf and the judge read one list; they cannot disagree.
· Round 4's estimates are anchor value × your count, scored by the identical function — a built estimate and a typed estimate face the same judge.

Before this page shipped, a Node proof ran against the game's own core: it checked the scoring formula at the band edges on both sides (over and under), recomputed every card's anchor expression from the stored truth, replayed every designated chain to confirm it lands within ×2, and audited every truth for presence, positivity, and unit family. The proof caught one real wart on its first run — a mathematically exact ×1.5 against the pencil's 0.19 m truth landed a hair above the Close line in binary floating point — so the band edges now carry a billionth of tolerance. No player gets failed by the representation.

Simplifications, named

  • Anchor values are rounded. A paperclip is roughly a gram — the room's own word — and a real one varies with size. The nickel (exactly 5.000 g) and the football field (exactly 100 yd) are the shelf's only true exacts.
  • Challenge truths are typical values, not measurements of a specific thing. A real elephant varies — the room gives 5,000–6,500 kg — phones run 150–220 g, blue whales are "around" 150 tonnes, and the Moon's distance breathes in and out around its average. The game scores you against the stated typical value and says so on the card.
  • The paper anchor is American. 4.5 g is a US letter sheet in standard 20 lb bond; an A4 sheet at the common 80 g/m² weighs a whisker under 5 g — a nickel, near enough.
  • The doorway is rounded down. A standard door leaf is about 2.03 m; the anchor carries 2 m, because an anchor you must look up is no anchor at all.
  • Scoring happens in the stated unit only. The game converts your answer exactly, so a slip between grams and kilograms is yours — the room's trap cards exist precisely because that slip, at industrial scale, has cost a spacecraft.
  • No partial credit for method. The game scores the number you lock in, not the chain you were thinking of. The lesson is where the method lives.
