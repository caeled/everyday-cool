Everyday
Measure.

America measures in inches, pounds, and gallons. Most of the world measures in metres, kilograms, and litres — and the second system is built on one staircase of tens that a five-year-old can climb. This room teaches that staircase, converts anything you throw at it with the working shown, and tells the stories of the two famous times mixing the systems up broke something expensive.

01 / THE PREFIX LADDER

One staircase. Every measurement.

The whole metric system is a single idea: take a unit — a metre, a litre, a gram — and resize it by tens, hundreds, and thousands, naming each size with a prefix. The prefix is the number. Kilo means a thousand of them, so a kilogram is a thousand grams and a kilometre is a thousand metres. Centi means a hundredth, so a centimetre is a hundredth of a metre. Learn ten words and you can read any metric measurement ever printed.

Press a step to start from it, then choose where to climb to. The ladder shows every step of the climb. Notice the jump near the bottom: from milli the staircase leaps straight to micro — the powers in between exist, but they have no everyday prefix names, so the ladder honestly skips them.

The same ladder fits every base unit: millimetres, millilitres, and milligrams are all “a thousandth of one.” One staircase, three climbs.

02 / THE CONVERTER BENCHES

Convert anything. See the working.

A converter that just prints an answer teaches nothing. These benches show their arithmetic: every conversion starts from the legal definition of the unit — and those definitions are exact, because since 1893 the inch and the pound have been defined from the metre and the kilogram (the story is in the history section below). Type a number, pick two units, and watch it happen.

Temperature

Temperature is the odd one out: it is not a ×10 system. Fahrenheit and Celsius are two different rulers laid over the same heat — they agree at exactly one point, −40, and nowhere else. There is no prefix for temperature because there is nothing to multiply: just a formula, shown above.

03 / FEEL IT

Sizes your hands already know.

Nobody feels a number; everybody feels a thing. These anchors hang metric sizes on objects an American already knows. Each one is a checked fact, not a slogan.

A nickel is 5 grams

EXACT — US MINT SPECIFICATION

The US five-cent coin is manufactured to weigh exactly 5.000 grams. A pocketful of nickels is a set of gram weights: four nickels, 20 grams; twenty nickels, 100 grams.

5 g — the coin in your pocket is a metric weight.

A paperclip is about a gram

APPROXIMATE — CLOSE ENOUGH TO FEEL

An ordinary small paperclip weighs roughly one gram. So a gram is “a paperclip's worth” — and a kilogram is a thousand paperclips, which is why the kilo is the one you shop with.

1 g ≈ one paperclip · 1 kg ≈ 1,000 of them.

A litre is a quart plus a splash

EXACT — 1 L = 1.057 US QUARTS

A litre is just 5.7% bigger than a US quart. Anything sold by the quart — milk, motor oil, ice cream — is, near enough, a litre wearing an American label.

1 litre = 1.057 quarts. Close cousins, not twins.

A kilogram is 2.2 pounds

EXACT — 1 KG = 2.20462 LB

Halve a kilogram weight and you are near a pound; double a pound and you are near a kilo. A 150 lb person is 68 kg — divide pounds by 2.2 and you are within a whisker.

Pounds ÷ 2.2 ≈ kilograms. The benches above do it exactly.

A metre is a yard plus a hand's width

EXACT — 1 M = 39.3701 IN

A yard is 36 inches; a metre is 39.37. The difference — about 3.4 inches — is roughly the width of your hand across the knuckles. Yardstick plus a hand: that's a metre.

1 metre = 1 yard + ~3.4 inches.

100 on a Canadian speedometer is 62

EXACT — 100 KM/H = 62.1 MPH

Drive into Canada and the signs jump to 100 — that's kilometres per hour, and it's 62 mph, ordinary highway speed. The metric number looks alarming; the speed is the same.

100 km/h = 62.1 mph. Same road, bigger-looking number.

The 2-litre bottle is the anchor

EVERYDAY — YOU ALREADY OWN ONE

Soda taught America the litre without a lesson: the 2-litre bottle. Two of them are 4 litres — a splash over a US gallon (3.785 L). A 500 mL water bottle is half a litre, about 17 fl oz.

2 bottles ≈ 1 gallon + a splash · 500 mL ≈ 17 fl oz.

05 / TRAP CARDS

Same word. Different thing.

The dangerous differences between the systems aren't metric at all — they're the places where two units share a name and not a size. These are the traps that catch travellers, shoppers, and (in the stories below) space agencies.

Three “tons” walk into a bar

TRAP — TON, TONNE, LONG TON

NameEqualsIn kilograms
US (short) ton2,000 lb907.18 kg
Metric tonne (t)1,000 kg1,000 kg
Long (Imperial) ton2,240 lb1,016.05 kg

A tonne is about 10% heavier than a US ton. When a European spec sheet says a crane lifts 5 tonnes, it means 11,023 lb — not the 10,000 lb an American ear hears.

Tonne = 1,000 kg · US ton = 2,000 lb · long ton = 2,240 lb.

The gallon that isn't

TRAP — US vs IMPERIAL GALLON

The US gallon is 3.785 litres. The Imperial gallon — the British one — is 4.546 litres, about 20% bigger. They share a name because both descend from old English measures; America kept the smaller wine gallon, Britain redefined its gallon in 1824, and the two have differed ever since. A car rated 40 mpg in Britain is doing about 33 mpg in American gallons.

US gallon = 3.785 L · Imperial gallon = 4.546 L. The converter bench above carries both.

A fluid ounce is not an ounce

TRAP — VOLUME vs WEIGHT

One measures space, the other measures weight; they share a name and nothing else. For water they nearly match — a US pint of water weighs about a pound, which is where the rhyme “a pint's a pound” comes from — but pour honey or oil instead and the two part company at once.

Fl oz fills a space · oz sits on a scale. Same word, different job.

06 / WHEN IT BROKE

Two systems. Two famous wrecks.

Mixing units isn't a schoolroom embarrassment. Done at industrial scale, it has destroyed a spacecraft and nearly destroyed an airliner. Both investigations found the same root cause: two teams, two systems, and a number passed between them with its unit left unsaid.

The Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999

NASA · $327.6 MILLION MISSION, LOST

One team's software reported the thrusters' impulse in pound-force seconds. The navigation team expected newton seconds. A pound-force is about 4.45 newtons, so every course correction was quietly wrong by that factor. On 23 September 1999 the orbiter dipped far too low into the Martian atmosphere and was destroyed. The mission's total cost is commonly cited at $327.6 million.

The maths was right. The unit was wrong. Nobody wrote it on the number.

The Gimli Glider, 1983

AIR CANADA FLIGHT 143 · NO FATALITIES

Canada was mid-switch to metric. With the fuel gauges broken, the crew calculated the 767's fuel load by hand — and used the pounds conversion (1.77) instead of the kilogram one (0.8). The jet took off from Montreal with roughly half the fuel it needed, ran dry at 41,000 feet, and glided to a dead-stick landing at a former air base in Gimli, Manitoba. Everyone survived.

22,300 was the right number — in the wrong unit. It needed kilograms; it got pounds.

07 / THE SHORT HONEST HISTORY

America's units are metric underneath.

1790s — France builds a system from the Earth. Revolutionary France defines the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, and the kilogram as the mass of a litre of water. Units from nature, in tens, for everyone.

1875 — America signs. The United States is a founding signatory of the Treaty of the Meter, and receives its official kilogram and metre standards like every other member.

1893 — the Mendenhall Order. The US defines its customary units from the metric standards: the yard is defined as 3600/3937 of a metre, the pound from the kilogram. From this order onward, American units are legally metric underneath — and they still are.

1959 — the exact numbers. The English-speaking countries agree the inch is exactly 2.54 cm and the pound exactly 0.45359237 kg. Those are the definitions the benches on this page use; they are not approximations.

1975 — conversion made voluntary. The Metric Conversion Act encourages, but does not require, the switch. America stays dual: metric in science, medicine, the military, and the soda aisle; customary on the road signs and in the kitchen. This room exists because “dual” means every American is a part-time converter — and converting is a learnable skill, not a talent.

10 / HELP

Help — how Everyday Measure works.

The prefix ladder

Press any step of the staircase to make it your starting step, type a quantity, pick a base unit (metres, litres, or grams), and choose a step to climb to. The bench shows the climb in two stages — into the base unit, then out to the target prefix — because that is all any metric conversion ever is. The ladder carries nine prefixes plus the base unit; the jump from milli to micro skips powers that have no everyday names.

The converter benches

Four benches: length, weight, volume, and temperature. Each starts from the legal definition of the unit you picked, converts through the base unit, and prints the arithmetic as numbered steps — nothing is hidden inside the machine. Temperature is the honest exception: it uses a formula, not a factor, and the bench prints the formula with your number substituted in.

The comparison shelf

Pick a thing and read it in both systems at once. The second reading is computed by the same code as the converter benches, so the two sides always agree. Things that vary in real life — elephants, cars, bathtubs — are marked approximate, with the honest range or reason in the note under the pans.

The trap cards

Three name-collisions that cause real trouble: the metric tonne vs the US ton vs the long ton; the US gallon vs the Imperial gallon (the volume bench carries both, labelled); and the fluid ounce, which measures volume, vs the ounce, which measures weight. If a number ever crosses the Atlantic, check which gallon and which ton it travelled with.

Honest limits — exact vs approximate

The conversion factors are exact by law: 2.54 cm to the inch and 0.45359237 kg to the pound are definitions, not measurements. The real-world things are approximate: elephants and bathtubs vary, and the page marks every one of them. Displayed results are rounded to six decimal places at most; the tests in this folder re-check the arithmetic against the definitions on every run.

Works offline?

Yes. The ladder, the benches, the comparison shelf, and this Help all run from the files in this folder — including inside the desktop app. Only the Read deeper shelf needs the internet, because those pages live on other people's sites.
