Hill Climb — the lesson

Hill Climb — Suspension Lab (the game calls itself 2D Hill-Climb — Suspension Trials) is a drive-as-far-as-you-can game: procedurally generated hills, three little rigs, a tank of fuel, and one way to die — landing on the roll cage. Underneath the cartoon is a real spring-and-damper simulation: every wheel is its own small physics body, hung from the chassis on a simulated strut that pushes with Hooke's law and resists with a damper, exactly the pair of forces that decides whether a real wheel follows a bump or bounces off it. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples from its own numbers, and how it is built.

The physics

The spring: pushed, it pushes back — proportionally

Each wheel hangs below the chassis on a strut. Squeeze that strut and it pushes back with a force proportional to the squeeze: twice the compression, twice the push. That is Hooke's law, and it is the whole personality of a spring — it has no opinion about how fast you squeezed it, only about how far. The stiffness is the constant k, and each rig has its own: the Dune Buggy's struts are k = 420, the Monster 4x4's are 370, the Moon Rover's are 290 (in the game's own units). The Rover's longer, softer struts are why it floats over the rocks while the Buggy skitters.

The damper: it resists the speed of the squeeze

A spring alone is a trampoline: it stores every bump and hands the energy straight back, so the car would pogo down the road forever. The second half of a real shock absorber is the damper — think of a plunger being forced through oil. It pushes back in proportion to how fast the strut is shortening or lengthening, and it doesn't care at all how far it has moved. Hit a bump and the damper speaks first, hard, in the first instant — before the spring has squeezed enough to say much. Cruise over gentle swells and the damper barely whispers, letting the spring do the holding. Together: the spring carries the weight, the damper spends the bump's energy as heat instead of returning it as a bounce. The game's damper constants are c = 26 (Buggy), 22 (Monster), 20 (Rover), and the tuning sliders in the game let you feel both failures — too little damping and the car wallows and bounces off crests; too much and the wheel can't move out of a bump's way, so the chassis takes the hit instead.

Why the pair makes a wheel follow the ground

A wheel only does its jobs — driving, braking, holding the car up — while it is touching the ground with weight on it. A bump throws the wheel upward; if the suspension is a stiff spring with no damper, the wheel leaves the crest still travelling up and lands late and hard. If it is all damper, the wheel can't extend fast enough into the dip on the far side. The spring–damper pair, tuned near a sweet spot the math section names, keeps the tyre pressed to the dirt through both — and in this game you can watch it work: the drawn coil between chassis and wheel changes colour as it compresses, cyan to amber to red.

The engine's push — a constant shove with a ceiling

The game's card on the Physics Game Table credits it with "Engine Torque Curves," and here the lesson has to correct the card: there is no torque curve in the code. A real engine's twisting force rises and fades with revs; this game's engine delivers a constant push whenever the gas is held — a fixed tangential acceleration applied at each driven wheel: 920 units/s² for the Buggy (rear wheel only), 1,180 for the Monster and 880 for the Rover (both wheels; they are all-wheel drive). What it has instead of a fading top end is a hard ceiling: the push switches off once the wheel's speed along the ground reaches the rig's maxSpd — 680, 620, and 640 units/s respectively — so the rigs top out abruptly rather than running out of breath. The only thing in the game that genuinely curves is the engine note, whose pitch climbs with the rear wheel's spin. The math section below converts the push into force and torque the way a mechanic would, but the honest headline is: constant shove, brick-wall ceiling.

Traction: the budget the game doesn't charge — and what it charges instead

A real tyre has a grip budget: the ground can push the tyre sideways or along its roll with at most μ × (the weight pressing it down) of force — μ, the friction coefficient, is roughly 0.7–1 for rubber on dirt. Ask for more than the budget and the wheel spins or slides. This game has no μ anywhere in it. A wheel that is touching the ground grips perfectly, at any slope, at any load. So what limits a climb here? Three honest substitutes. First, contact: an airborne wheel gets no drive at all — hold the gas in the air and the wheels just spin up uselessly (26 rad/s² of spin, waiting). Second, the speed ceiling above. Third, geometry: the drive also pitches the chassis backward, and a climb steep enough becomes a backflip onto the roll cage, which is fatal. The worked examples put numbers on what a friction budget would demand — it turns out the game's one-driven-wheel Buggy would need racing-slick grip on a real 30° climb, which is the entire reason real hill-climb rigs drive every wheel they have.

Weight transfer: load goes where the squeeze is

The two struts are independent, so the car's weight is always divided the way the compressions say it is — nothing enforces a 50/50 split. Accelerate and the chassis pitches back (the game even adds a helping torque from the gas pedal, 42% of its in-air strength while grounded), the rear strut compresses, the front extends: load moves onto the rear wheel. That is why the rear-driven Buggy can climb at all — its one driven wheel is precisely the one being loaded. Park on a slope and the downhill wheel carries more for the same reason. Brake, and the nose dives and the front strut takes the load. Weight transfer isn't scripted anywhere in the code; it falls out of two springs and a pitching body, the way it falls out of a real car.

The math the game runs

Every substep, for each wheel, the code projects the wheel's position and velocity onto the strut's axis and applies one combined force. Stated the way the code states it (fAlong = -kSpring * stretch - cDamp * vAlong, where stretch is the strut's length minus its rest length):

Fspring = −k · x Hooke's law. x is how far the strut is stretched from its rest length (negative when compressed). Buggy: k = 420. Standing still on flat ground, each strut carries half the chassis weight — 1.6 × 640 / 2 = 512 units of force — so each spring sits compressed by 512 / 420 ≈ 1.2 units of its 26-unit rest drop. These springs are stiff: nearly all the travel is saved for bumps, not spent on standing.
Fdamper = −c · v The damper law. v is the speed at which the strut is shortening or lengthening. Buggy: c = 26, so a wheel being shoved upward at 100 units/s meets a damper force of 26 × 100 = 2,600 — five times the force its spring exerts just holding the car up. At a gentle 20 units/s of squeeze speed the damper already matches that standing spring force. Speed, not distance, is what the damper reads.
f = (1 / 2π) · √(k / m) The natural bounce frequency of one corner: how fast that corner would bob if you pushed the car down and let go. One corner's share of the chassis is m = 1.6 / 2 = 0.8. Buggy: √(420 / 0.8) / 2π ≈ 3.65 Hz. Monster ≈ 3.42 Hz, Rover ≈ 3.03 Hz. A real family car rides near 1–1.5 Hz; these are stiff little machines, partly because the game's gravity is fierce — at its own odometer scale (14 px = 1 m) the Rolling Hills gravity of 640 px/s² is ≈ 45.7 m/s², nearly five Earth gravities. Paired with the dampers, the corners sit at roughly two-thirds of critical damping (ζ ≈ 0.71 Buggy, 0.64 Monster, 0.66 Rover) — one overshoot, then settle, which is exactly the lively-but-controlled feel the game has.
F = τ / r   →   τ = F · r Torque and the contact patch. An engine's torque τ becomes a push at the ground by dividing by the wheel's radius r. The game works the other way — it applies its "torque" number as an acceleration to the driven wheel (mass 0.42) — so the patch force is 0.42 × 920 ≈ 386 for the Buggy, and the equivalent axle torque is 386 × 20 ≈ 7,728 unit·px. The Monster, with two driven wheels and bigger tyres: 0.42 × 1,180 ≈ 496 per wheel, τ ≈ 496 × 28 ≈ 13,877. Same force, longer lever (bigger wheel) — more torque, spent on turning a heavier rig.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Example 1: the bump stop is fifteen times stronger than standing still

From Example math above, a parked Buggy's strut is compressed about 1.2 units and pushes with 512. Now land it badly: the strut slams to its bump stop, where the code clamps travel at a strut length of 8 — a compression of 26 − 8 = 18 units from rest. Spring force: 420 × 18 = 7,560, roughly fifteen times the standing load, before the damper's contribution is even counted. That enormous reserve is why a landing that looks impossible just bottoms out with a red coil and drives on — and why the failure the game actually punishes is never "the spring broke" but the roof arriving first.

Example 2: a 30° climb, and the grip the game lets you cheat

On the Rolling Hills (gravity 640), a 30° slope pulls the car back along the ground with 640 × sin 30° = 320 units/s² of deceleration. The Buggy's engine delivers 920 units/s² to its rear wheel — nearly three times the slope's pull — so in this game the climb is never about power; it is about the 680 units/s ceiling and keeping the cage off the dirt. Now charge the traction budget the game waives. The whole rig weighs 2.44 × 640 ≈ 1,562; holding it on the 30° slope takes ≈ 781 of tangential force, and the ground presses each wheel with ≈ 1,562 × cos 30° / 2 ≈ 676. Through one driven wheel, the Buggy would need μ ≈ 781 / 676 ≈ 1.15 — more grip than dirt offers. Split across the Monster's two driven wheels, each needs μ ≈ 0.58 — ordinary dirt territory. The game lets the Buggy cheat that budget; real hills don't, and the difference between those two numbers is the whole engineering case for all-wheel drive.

Example 3: tuning the sliders is tuning the physics

The Suspension Tuning panel multiplies the coded constants live — spring stiffness ×0.45–1.9, damping ×0.3–2.0, torque ×0.6–1.6 — so the formulas above become knobs. Drop the Buggy's spring slider to 0.45 and k becomes 189: the standing sag more than doubles (512 / 189 ≈ 2.7 units) and the corner frequency falls to √(189 / 0.8) / 2π ≈ 2.45 Hz — visibly wallowier. Push damping to ×2.0 and the Buggy's ζ climbs past 1.4: overdamped, no bounce at all, but the wheel now returns from a bump so slowly that the next bump arrives before the tyre has — the wallowing failure from the other direction. The stock tune, ζ ≈ 0.7, is the compromise real suspension engineers also land on.

How it's built — the coding choices

The wheels are bodies, not raycasts

Many 2D driving games fake suspension with a ray cast from the chassis to the ground. This one doesn't: each wheel is a genuine little physics body with its own position, velocity, and mass (0.42), simulated separately from the chassis (mass 1.6, with a rotational inertia of 1,850). The strut between them is computed fresh each substep — the wheel's offset from its chassis anchor is projected onto the chassis's down-axis for the spring–damper force, and onto the sideways axis for a much stiffer locating spring (2,400 stiffness, 85 damping) that keeps the wheel under its arch without being a rigid rod. Forces are applied equally and oppositely to wheel and chassis, with the chassis side also producing torque about its centre — which is where the pitch, the dive, and the weight transfer come from. Bump stops clamp the travel (strut length between 8 and restDrop + 26) so the spring can never invert or stretch to infinity. The whole step runs eight substeps per frame with the frame time clamped at 0.032 s — stiff springs (and 2,400 is very stiff) demand small steps, or the simulation pumps in energy that isn't in the physics.

The terrain is a function, not a picture

Each biome's ground is an analytic height function — layered sine waves for the hills and the Moon, a sawtooth ramp profile for the canyon's jumps, a higher-frequency stack plus a steady downward trend for the trials — flat for the first 260 units so you always start on a pad. The physics samples the exact function at each wheel; the ground normal comes from a finite difference (sample 3 units either side). When a wheel penetrates, it is pushed out along that normal and its inward velocity is nearly annihilated (reflected at 1.08×, i.e. about 8% bounce — a thud, not a trampoline), with rolling resistance bleeding about a fifth of the wheel's speed per second. There is no track to run out of: pickups are generated ahead of the car forever.

Flips, the roof, and a spring you weren't told about

Airborne rotation accumulates, and a flip is declared at 1.82π radians — about 328°, generous on purpose, since a game about flips should pay for nearly-there. Air time over 1.05 s pays a bonus. Death is a single point test: the roll cage's top point, 30 units above the chassis centre, checked against the terrain each substep. And the driver's bobble head is a third spring–damper system (k = 45, c = 6, bobbing at ≈ 1.07 Hz — right in a real car's ride band), driven by the chassis's rotation: pure theatre, running on the same two formulas as the suspension. Once you see the pattern, you see it everywhere in the file.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there is no engine torque curve — the drive is a constant acceleration with a hard top-speed cutout, despite the Game Table card's wording. There is no friction model: no μ, no slip, no traction cap — a grounded wheel grips perfectly, and its in-air spin-up has no effect on landing. The wheels are point masses (all three rigs share the same wheel mass, 0.42, whatever their tyre size) and the tyres never deform. The struts act only along the chassis's down-axis — no camber, no steering, no lateral tyre forces; the world is a 2D side view and the car cannot leave its lane. Gravity is a per-biome constant: the "Lunar Low-G" of 265 px/s² is floaty only next to the hills — at the game's own metre scale it is ≈ 18.9 m/s², about twice Earth's gravity, not the Moon's 1.62. Fuel burns by the clock (2.5/s driving, 0.75/s idling), not by work done; nitro is a flat 560 units/s² push along the chassis nose, not extra wheel torque. The odometer's 14 px = 1 m and the HUD's speed × 0.22 "km/h" are theatre scales, chosen to feel right. None of this is hidden in the game — it is simply what a one-file game can afford, and now it is named.
