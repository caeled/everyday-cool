Star Forge — the lesson

Star Forge is a gravity sandbox with a score: drag back like a slingshot, launch a body — asteroid, planet, or star — into a living system, and see how many years the system survives before something is ejected or everything collapses into one. What makes it different from Gravity Golf down the hall is that nothing here rides a rail: every body pulls every other body, the star wobbles under its planets, and the only thing between your system and chaos is the quality of your launches. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, the audit that checked the physics against the code, and the receipts — including a famous three-star braid flown for four hundred of its own periods through the game's own engine.

Star Forge was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

The physics

Every mass pulls every other mass — including the star

The game's own lesson line, printed over the canvas, is the whole thesis: gravity is mutual — the star moves too — and stability is earned by the launch, not granted. The code keeps that promise literally. Each step, the engine walks every pair of bodies once, computes the pull between them, and applies it to both: the planet accelerates toward the star and the star accelerates toward the planet, by exactly the amount Newton's third law demands. There are no anchored suns, no scripted orbits, no test particles. Load the Starter and watch the crosshair at the centre of mass: Sol visibly wobbles around it, dragged in a small circle by its one planet. That wobble is the receipt that the gravity here is a conversation, not a decree.

The figure-eight: three stars chasing each other down one track

The Figure-Eight preset loads the most famous orbit in the three-body problem: three equal masses that chase each other along a single figure-eight curve, each star taking its turn through the middle. Cristopher Moore found the orbit numerically in 1993; Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery proved it exists in 2000, and it opened the modern study of choreographies — periodic dances where every body follows the same path, offset in time. It is also a genuinely hard test of an integrator: the orbit is a knife-edge arrangement that a leaky or sloppy simulation smears into a merger or an ejection within a few dozen periods. The preset uses the canonical Chenciner–Montgomery starting positions and velocities, scaled to the game's units by the similarity rules of gravity itself (lengths ×190 world units, velocities ×√(G·m/L)) — and the audit below flew it, at the game's own timestep, for 400 full periods. It was still braided when the audit stopped counting.

Why the integrator is symplectic — and why naive Euler would lie to you

The engine steps time with velocity Verlet: a half-kick of velocity, a drift of position, a fresh computation of every pull at the new positions, then the second half-kick. That ordering makes it a symplectic integrator — it doesn't conserve energy exactly, but its energy error oscillates inside a fixed band forever instead of leaking in one direction. The naive alternative (plain Euler: step position, step velocity, repeat) gains a little energy every orbit, so simulated planets spiral slowly outward and a "stable" system is an artifact of the arithmetic. The audit measured the difference in kind, below: over 500 years — 500 full orbits of the Starter's planet — Star Forge's total energy drifted by about a hundred-billionth of a display unit. The books don't just balance; they stay balanced.

Softening: the named trade

Pure Newtonian gravity has a singularity: as two bodies approach, the pull rockets toward infinity, and any fixed-step simulation eventually feeds a body a velocity it can't cash. Star Forge uses the standard astronomer's trade, Plummer softening: the force law's denominator is (r² + ε²)3/2 with ε = 4 world units (0.027 AU). Beyond a few ε, the force is Newton's to many decimal places; inside ε, the pull rounds off to a peak and falls toward zero at the centre instead of exploding — close passes slingshot cleanly. The page says this out loud, twice (the integrator note under the title, and the "What this leaves out" card), and the energy readout uses the matching softened potential, so the bookkeeping and the force are the same physics. The trade is real, though: inside ~4 units, gravity here is gentler than Newton's, and the figure-eight's true period in this softened world runs about 0.13% long — a number the audit caught, below.

The math the game runs

One pairwise formula runs the whole forge. For every pair, each body feels:

ai += G · mj · (rj − ri) / (r² + ε²)3/2,   ε = 4 wu G is not the textbook constant pasted in — it is calibrated: G = 4π²·AU³/M☉ ≈ 133,240 in game units, chosen so Kepler's third law comes out in round numbers: a circular orbit at 1 AU (150 world units) around a 1 M☉ star (1000 mass units) takes exactly 1.00 simulated year. Time steps are fixed at dt = 0.0005 yr — 2,000 steps per simulated year — whatever the speed button says; the speed controls (pause, 0.25×, 1×, 2×, 4×) only change how many fixed steps each real second buys.

The Stable Orbit Helper runs the same physics backwards. For a body placed a distance d from the system's centre of mass, a circular orbit under the softened force needs the centripetal pull to equal the softened gravity of everything already there:

vcirc = √[ G · (Msystem + mnew) · d² / (d² + ε²)3/2 ] Note the mass in that formula: the total system mass plus the new body's own mass — not just the central star's. The helper treats all the existing mass as if it sat at the centre of mass (exact when one body dominates, a good suggestion otherwise), points the velocity tangentially, adds the centre of mass's own motion, and flips the direction if your drag is already aiming the other way. The audit flew it: a planet placed at 225 units from a lone star with exactly the helper's velocity held its separation between 225.0000 and 225.0003 units for twelve years. The ghost arrow is not a hint. It's the answer, drawn.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

Star Forge arrived with a commission: a list of things it was supposed to be. The audit lifted the game's own physics functions out of the shipped file — same constants, same pairwise loop, same Verlet step, same merger rule — and ran them headlessly. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

That last line is the honest subtlety: if you freeze the simulation at exact multiples of the textbook period, the stars are increasingly out of place — but they're out of place along the track, because the softened system's real period is 1.437 years, not 1.435. The dance persists; only the metronome differs. An integrator that was leaking would not give you a saturated offset and a stable braid at year 574. It would give you a merger by year 50.

The HUD's energy readout is real arithmetic, not set dressing: kinetic energy summed over every body, potential energy over every pair with the same softened law the force uses, and a BOUND / UNBOUND pill that simply reports the sign. Negative total energy, bound system — the same test the physics itself obeys.

Receipt 3: mergers conserve what they claim to

Two bodies on a collision course — a planet (m = 15) and an asteroid (m = 1) — carried total momentum (1540, 510) into the step that merged them, and the merged body carried (1540, 510) out, to floating-point precision. Mass came out exactly 16; the new radius was 7.4658 units, exactly ∛(7.2³ + 3.5³) — volume conserved, as the honesty card says. Every merger is written into the Observatory Log with the classes and the combined mass, including the special case the log calls an ignition: feed enough mass to a body to cross the star threshold and the log says so.

How it's built — the coding choices

One pairwise loop, Newton's third law doing the bookkeeping

The acceleration pass visits each pair once and applies the pull to both bodies in the same breath — half the arithmetic of the naive double loop, and momentum conservation built into the structure rather than hoped for: the force that speeds body A toward B slows B toward A by the matching amount, in the same line of code. The merger test's exact momentum receipt is this loop's signature.

Fixed steps, honest speed

The display runs on an accumulator: real time is converted into a debt of fixed 0.0005-year steps, and the engine pays the debt exactly, step by step. Speed buttons change the exchange rate (at 1×, a simulated year takes four real seconds), never the step size — so a system that survives at 1× survives identically at 4×, and the score can't be gamed by a fast machine. A per-frame cap on steps means a stalled tab slows the forge down rather than fast-forwarding it through a catastrophe you didn't see.

The score is a rule, and the rule is drawn on the sky

Your lifetime runs while at least two bodies remain inside a dashed ring drawn around the system's centre of mass: the ejection horizon, 8.0 AU (1,200 units) out, labelled in the world where you can watch bodies approach it. Collapse — mergers reducing you to a single body — ends the run too. The best lifetime is kept in your browser (one localStorage key, star_forge_best_lifetime_v1) and nothing else is stored. Presets are built with the same care as the physics: the toy Solar System gives each planet the circular speed for the mass enclosed inside its orbit, and the Binary's two stars split their shared orbital velocity in inverse proportion to their masses, so both presets start in balance instead of in trouble.

Trails are evidence, kept on a budget

Every body lays a trail of past positions, sampled every 2.6 units of travel and capped (320 points for a planet, 280 for a star, 180 for an asteroid), drawn with the oldest points faintest — so what you see behind a body is its recent history, fading at the rate it deserves. When a system starts to go wrong, the trails show it before the telemetry does: a braid loosening, an orbit precessing, a planet's ellipse fattening toward the horizon. The forge's real instrument is the picture.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate — the audit checked it line by line: the world is 2D; G and the scale are toys (calibrated toys — 1 AU around 1 M☉ really does take 1.00 yr); softening is ε = 4 units with the (r² + ε²)−3/2 law the card prints; mergers are perfectly sticky, conserving mass, momentum, and volume; there is no relativity and there are no tides. The card even confesses that mergers lose kinetic energy "to heat/flash" — the code tracks no heat, but the energy readout does drop at a merger by exactly the amount momentum conservation costs, and the energy tooltip says plainly that E is conserved only between mergers and launches. All of that holds.

Where the words bend: the score panel says your lifetime runs while "≥ 2 bodies remain bound inside 8.0 AU." The code's rule is blunter: the run ends the moment any one body crosses the horizon — the audit ended a run at 0.23 years by flinging a single asteroid out while the star and its planet sat bound and untouched — and "bound" is never actually tested per body; inside the ring is the whole test. Read the rule as "the whole system stays home," not "a bound pair survives." Relatedly, once a run has ended the forge keeps simulating and you can keep launching, but the score stays ended until you press Restart or pick a preset; only a cleared canvas restarts the clock on its own.

The helper's name oversells it slightly: the button says "Stable Orbit Helper." What it computes is exactly the circular velocity at your placement point — verified above to four decimal places around a lone star — but it treats all the system's mass as concentrated at the centre of mass. Around one dominant star that's the truth; in a spread-out cluster it's a good first guess that the other bodies will immediately start arguing with. The arrow's own label in the sky is the honest one: v_circ.

Small print: preset bodies carry their own masses — the Starter's Terra is m = 25, not the palette's 15; the palette numbers (asteroid 1, planet 15, star 1000) describe bodies you launch. The Chaos Cluster is dealt by an unseeded random generator, so no two deals match and its log line can't be replayed. And the figure-eight, for all its stamina, flies as a close cousin of the textbook orbit — same braid, 0.13% longer period — because softening is part of this world's physics, not an error in it.
