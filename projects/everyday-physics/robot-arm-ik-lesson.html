Kinematics — the lesson

Give a chain of rigid links a set of joint angles and the tip's position is arithmetic — no choices, no argument. Run it backwards — put the tip here — and the problem turns inside out: there may be two postures that reach, or a family of them, or none, and at a few unlucky postures the mathematics itself goes quiet. This exhibit puts three different inverse-kinematics solvers — FABRIK, damped least squares, and CCD — on the same planar arm, with joint limits, servo slew limits, obstacles, and a live manipulability readout, so you can watch each one's character. This page is the other half: the physics and the math, and the audit that drove the shipped exhibit through its own controls and measured what it actually did.

Verdict: Forward kinematics exact to under a millimetre and all three solvers land reachable targets at 0.0–0.3 mm — but DLS and CCD freeze forever on the singular axis, and the flagship Pick & Place preset drives its own links through its obstacles while claiming "Obstacle Avoid".

The physics

Forward is easy: angles in, position out

A planar serial arm is a chain: each link has a length, each joint adds its angle to every angle before it, and the end effector — the tip — sits where the chain's vectors sum. That is forward kinematics (FK), and it is a function in the strict sense: one pose in, exactly one tip position out. The exhibit normalizes every configuration — 2 to 8 joints, three robot profiles — to the same total reach of 400 mm, so the workspace is always the same disc and only the chain's shape inside it changes.

Backward is the real problem: position in, angles out

Inverse kinematics (IK) asks the reverse question, and the reverse question has no unique answer. A 2-link arm reaching a point inside its workspace can do it elbow-up or elbow-down — the exhibit's Flip Elbow button swaps between exactly those two mirror solutions. Add links and the solutions multiply into families; ask for a point outside the disc and there are none. Every IK solver is a strategy for searching that space, and the three on this bench search in genuinely different ways: FABRIK works geometrically on the chain's shape, damped least squares follows the Jacobian's calculus downhill, and CCD rotates one joint at a time toward the target, sweeping from tip to base.

Singularities: where the Jacobian goes quiet

The Jacobian J is the map from joint motion to tip motion: how the tip velocity changes as each joint turns. Where the arm is fully straight, one direction of tip motion simply vanishes from that map — no combination of joint turns moves the tip straight ahead, because every joint's first-order effect points sideways. The Jacobian loses rank, its inverse explodes, and a solver that follows the gradient finds a gradient of exactly zero: no signal, in any joint, about which way to bend. How a solver behaves there is the truest test of its design, and it is where this audit found its sharpest split between the three solvers (Receipt 4 below). The exhibit's manipulability, w = √det(JJᵀ), measures how far from that cliff a pose sits: w is the area of the velocity ellipsoid, and it collapses to exactly zero at the straight pose.

An obstacle is a shape, not a point

Keeping the tip out of a disc is one problem; keeping the whole arm out is another. A link is a line segment, and the honest clearance question is the distance from the obstacle's centre to every segment — elbow, forearm, wrist — not just to the end effector. A controller that only steers the tip can thread the tip neatly around a post while the elbow sweeps straight through it. That distinction — point avoidance versus link avoidance — is the whole story of the Pick & Place finding in Receipt 5, and it is a distinction real robot cells are built around.

The math the game runs

Forward kinematics

x = Σ Li·cos(θ1+…+θi),   y = Σ Li·sin(θ1+…+θi) Each joint angle is relative to its parent link, so link i points along the cumulative sum of the angles up to it. For the 3-DOF industrial profile the lengths are 173.6 / 133.3 / 93.1 mm — 400 mm of total reach, like every profile on the bench.

The Jacobian solvers: DLS and CCD

Δθ = Jᵀ(JJᵀ + λ²I)−1·e,   w = √det(JJᵀ) Damped least squares (DLS) steps the joints along the Jacobian's solution, with the damping λ keeping the step finite near singularities where the undamped inverse would explode. In this build the slider's λ is a scaled knob: it enters the normal equations as λeff = λ · 400 · 0.42 ≈ 168λ mm — 37 mm at the default 0.22 (see the honest box). CCD takes the geometric route instead: for each joint from tip to base, rotate that joint so its link points at the target, and repeat. The shipped CCD is a damped variant — 0.78 gain, ±0.45 rad per joint per sweep — not the textbook original.

FABRIK: reaching, forward and backward

backward pass: tip → target, re-lay each link at full length;   forward pass: base → origin, re-lay again FABRIK (Forward And Backward Reaching Inverse Kinematics) never touches a Jacobian. It drags the chain's tip onto the target, then walks back to the base re-imposing each link's length, then walks forward again from the pinned base — and iterates until the tip stays on the target. Because it works on positions rather than gradients, a perfectly straight chain is not a dead end for it: the build adds a small collinear kick, and that kick is exactly what saves it in Receipt 4.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit loaded the shipped page headlessly and drove it through its own interface — preset buttons, solver selectors, sliders, checkboxes, canvas pointer drags — while reading the page's own telemetry, and checked the numbers against independently computed forward kinematics and Jacobian values. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Sub-millimetre agreement throughout (the page's own telemetry rounds to 1 mm). Flip Elbow on the [30°, −50°, 20°] pose is a true mirror solution: the tip stays at (369, 41) while the angles mirror to (−17°, +50°, −20°), as the geometry requires.

Receipt 2: all three solvers land reachable targets

Six targets at radii 71–370 mm, run under each of FABRIK, DLS, and CCD: every run finished at 0.0–0.3 mm final error — inside the solver tolerance of 0.45 mm in the code — with status "Reached" and end-effector coordinates matching the requested target to the millimetre. Unreachable targets are handled just as exactly: at (600, 0) the tip stops at (400, 0) with an error readout of 200 mm; at (425, 0) the error reads 25 mm — both precisely the overshoot distance, both flagged "Out of Reach". Path tracking holds the same standard: the Square Weld preset over 30 s peaks at 0.4 mm error, "Reached" in 120 of 120 samples, and the 6-DOF Snake figure-8 over 25 s peaks at 0.4 mm.

Receipt 4: manual obstacle avoidance works — as far as it goes

With FABRIK driving, an obstacle of radius 44 mm at (110, 60), and the target behind it at (300, 155), the minimum link-segment distance to the obstacle centre over 7 s was 74.3 mm — against the solver's own safe radius of 44 + 22 (margin) + 8 (link half-thickness) = 74 mm. The target was still reached, error 0, and the "Obstacle Avoid" status appeared. Driven by hand, segment by segment, the avoidance is real. Keep this receipt in mind; the next one is its shadow.

Receipt 5 — FALSE: DLS and CCD freeze forever on the singular axis

Start the 2-link arm dead straight, θ = (0°, 0°), and ask for the target (300, 0) — straight ahead, 100 mm short of full reach, comfortably inside the workspace. After 5 s:

SolverError after 5 sAngles
DLS100 mm — unchanged0° / 0° — never moved
CCD100 mm — unchanged0° / 0° — never moved
FABRIK0 mm — reached28° / −88°

This is the singularity of the physics section, caught in the act: at the straight pose the Jacobian — and CCD's per-joint gradient — is exactly zero, so both calculus-driven solvers receive no signal and sit still, forever. The status pill does say "Near Singularity!", which is honest reporting of the diagnosis; but there is no recovery in either solver, no nudge, no restart — the arm simply never moves. FABRIK escapes the identical setup through its built-in collinear kick and reaches with error 0. Move the target off-axis, to (300, 100), and all three solvers reach it from the same pose: the freeze is specific to the singular axis, exactly where the theory says the gradient vanishes.

Receipt 6 — FALSE: Pick & Place drives its links through its own obstacles

The shipped "Obstacle Pick & Place" preset was sampled for 26.7 s — about four cycles — with joint positions reconstructed from the page's own angle telemetry. Link segments were inside an obstacle disc in 32 of 800 samples. The worst event: segment 3 passed 1.1 mm from the centre of the (−115, 35) obstacle (radius 30 mm) — not a graze, a transit through the middle of the disc — while the status read "Obstacle Avoid". Both obstacles were penetrated: (0, 118) at radius 44 and (−115, 35) at radius 30. Part of the cause is upstream of the solver: the preset's own transfer-arc target passes ~16 mm from the (0, 118) obstacle's centre — inside the disc — and the links chase the target they are given. So the preset is end-effector-point avoidance with link clipping, in the one preset designed to show obstacle avoidance off — and its status line claims the stronger thing. Receipt 4 stands: the avoidance machinery works when a person drives it. The flagship preset does not use it well enough to earn its own label.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The manipulability readout is w/1000, unlabelled: the card says "Manipulability w" but displays w/1000 (a code comment calls it "cm²-like readability") — the 26.4 on screen is really 26,436 mm². Beside it, the "Dexterity √det(JJᵀ)" bar mixes two quantities: the fill width is w normalized against a heuristic reference, while the % text is the condition ratio s₂/s₁. Both are computed correctly; the labelling conflates them.

The manipulability ellipsoid is not to scale: its axes use the true JJᵀ eigen-directions and the singular-value ratio, but both singular values are multiplied by an arbitrary 0.22 display factor, unlabelled. Shape and orientation are honest; absolute size is illustrative.

Inner-annulus targets stop sub-optimally: for a 2-link arm, the target (50, 0) sits inside the annulus hole (true inner boundary 120.8 mm). The status correctly says "Out of Reach", but DLS parks the tip at radius 126.9 mm, off-axis at (109, 65), leaving 87.8 mm of error where 70.8 mm is achievable — it does not find the closest boundary point.

Damping λ is a scaled knob, and its classic trade-off is masked: λ enters as λeff ≈ 168λ mm (37 mm at the default 0.22); the label just says "λ". Because the solver re-runs every frame from the current pose, all tested λ values (0.01 / 0.22 / 1.00) still reached a near-boundary target (392, 40) to ≤0.4 mm — the textbook DLS steady-state bias never appears in this architecture; λ mostly reshapes the transient (peak Σ|ω| 950–1324 °/s, non-monotonic in λ).

"Max Solver Iterations" is per animation frame, not per target: the solver re-runs each frame from the servo's current pose, so even a cap of 2 iterations converges over successive frames — a large jump showed 63 mm error at 0.2 s with cap 2, then converged. The telemetry "1 / 24" is the last frame's count: honest, but easy to misread.

The Singularity preset's circle overshoots the workspace: its "sweep to the outer reach boundary" circle (centre (145, 50), radius 256) actually reaches radius 409.4 mm — 9.4 mm past the 400 mm reach. Measured over 40 s, error peaks at exactly 9.4 mm and the status flips to "Out of Reach" in 15 of 160 samples. The DLS solver is fine; the preset's geometry over-promises by 9.4 mm.

CCD is a damped variant, 0.78 gain with ±0.45 rad per-joint-per-sweep clamps, not textbook CCD. It converges well in every reachable test the audit ran — but the page doesn't say it has been modified.
