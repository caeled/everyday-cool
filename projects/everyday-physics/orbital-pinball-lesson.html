Orbital Pinball — the lesson

Orbital Pinball is a pinball table — plunger, flippers, slingshots, bumpers, drop targets, a wormhole that pays out multiball — with one outrageous piece of furniture added: a gravity well sitting in the middle of the playfield, pulling on the ball the whole time. On a real table the only gravity is the table's tilt, and it points one way. Here the "tilt" is a place: fall toward the star at the centre and every bounce, every arc, every drain is negotiated with it. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, shots flown through the game's own physics as worked examples, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to the collection by Steven Powell; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool, from reading the game's code.

The physics

Two gravities, one ball

A pinball machine is already a physics instrument: the playfield is tilted, so a constant pull — the slope gravity — drags the ball toward the drain, and everything else on the table is an argument with that pull. Orbital Pinball keeps the slope (640 units/s² on Kepler Classic, the default table) and adds a second gravity with an address. The well at the centre pulls the ball toward itself, from every direction, weakening with distance — and near the well it is the stronger of the two: 100 units away, its pull is 992 units/s², half again the slope. High on the table the slope runs your life; down near the star, the star does. The purple field arrows the game draws (toggle them in the Physics Lab panel) are that second gravity made visible — every arrow is the total pull at that spot, computed by the same formula that moves the ball.

The wells bend everything

Because the well pulls sideways to the ball's motion as often as along it, there are no straight lines on this table between bounces — only arcs. A ball crossing above the well sags toward it; a ball crossing below it gets hoisted; a slow ball can be caught outright and swung around. It is exactly the gravity-assist geometry of Gravity Golf, played at arcade speed with a drain at the bottom: same inverse-square pull, same rule that a pass trades height for speed and back — except here the "course" is 840 units tall, the ball is live for seconds, and a bent path usually ends at a flipper, a bumper, or the outlane. The worked examples below put numbers on the bending: at 650 units/s the well is a steering wheel; at 400 units/s, on the same line, it is a trap.

Reflection: angle in, angle out — measured from the normal

Every wall, guide, and apron on the table obeys the mirror rule: split the ball's velocity into the part aimed into the surface (along its normal — the line perpendicular to the surface at the contact point) and the part sliding along it. The bounce reverses the into-part (or part of it — see restitution below) and leaves the along-part untouched. Angle in equals angle out, both measured from the normal. On a flat wall the normal is the same everywhere, so the rule is predictable. On a curved bumper the normal is the radius line — it points from the bumper's centre through wherever the ball touched — so the place of contact chooses the exit: dead-centre hits come straight back at you, edge hits fan out wide, and a ball can be thrown almost sideways by a bumper it barely grazed. That is why the three bumpers feel alive while the walls feel honest: same law, rotating normal.

Flippers are the honest cheat: they do work

Nothing else on a passive table can give the ball energy — walls and wells only trade it around. The flipper can, because it is a machine: a lever rotating about its pivot, driven (in the fiction, by a solenoid; in the code, by an angular motor) through a 0.98-radian swing in about 41 milliseconds. When a moving surface meets the ball, the collision is judged in the surface's frame: what matters is the ball's velocity relative to the flipper face at the contact point, and that face is travelling. The tip of the flipper sweeps at up to 1,728 units/s — faster than any ball on the table is allowed to move (the game caps ball speed at 1,480). So a clean tip strike doesn't reflect the ball; it launches it, and the further from the pivot the contact happens, the harder the launch. That is the whole skill of pinball timing, stated as physics: you are not aiming a bounce, you are choosing where on a moving lever the ball will be standing when the lever arrives.

Elastic is a dial, and this table turns it both ways

A perfectly elastic bounce keeps all the into-surface speed, reversed. Real surfaces keep a fraction of it — the coefficient of restitution, e — and this game's table is a catalogue of the dial: the plunger floor is nearly dead (e = 0.25), the aprons are soft (0.45), the outer walls standard (0.72), the lane guides livelier (0.78), the upper deflectors springier still (0.85), and two balls meeting in multiball bounce at an effective 0.88. Then the table breaks its own scale, deliberately: the slingshots bounce at 0.9 plus a flat 540 units/s kick, the moons bounce at 0.92 in their own moving frame plus 140 — and the bumpers and the well's core don't reflect your speed at all. They replace it: a bumper sends you out at at least 510 units/s, or 1.12× your incoming speed if that's more, whichever is greater. Energy on this table is a managed resource — the slope, the drag, and the drains remove it; the powered surfaces inject it; and the Space-bar repel pulse (charged by bumper, moon, and core hits) spends it to flip the wells into repulsors for 1.15 seconds when a drain is coming and you'd rather argue.

The math the game runs

a = M / (r² + 2600),   capped at 1,600 units/s² The well's pull at distance r. The textbook form is a = G·M/r²; as in Gravity Golf, G is folded into the mass number (Kepler Classic's star: M = 12,500,000) — and there are two honest guards on the formula. The +2600 is a softening cushion (√2600 ≈ 51 units): inside that distance the pull grows gentler than Newton's instead of rocketing to infinity. The cap is a ceiling: the raw formula passes 1,600 units/s² inside ~72 units of Kepler's star, and the code simply refuses to pull harder than that. Worked: at 100 units, a = 12,500,000 / 12,600 ≈ 992 units/s²; at 200 units, ≈ 293. On Zero-G Singularity the effective mass is 17,500,000 × 1.55 = 27,125,000, so the cap rules everything inside ~120 units — that table's well is less a slope than a wall of pull. The Physics Lab's Gravity Well Strength slider rescales M from 0 to 2.2×, for flight and field arrows alike.
v′ = v − (1 + e)·(v·n̂)·n̂ The reflection every segment collision on the table runs: n̂ is the surface normal at the contact point, v·n̂ is the into-surface speed, and (1 + e) decides how much of it comes back. Worked pair of numbers — a ball at v = (300, 400) units/s (speed exactly 500) meets a floor, n̂ = (0, −1), with the outer walls' e = 0.72: v·n̂ = −400, so v′ = (300, 400) − 1.72 × (−400) × (0, −1) = (300, −288). The along-part (300) is untouched; the into-part returns at 72% (288 of 400); the ball leaves at ≈ 416 units/s. Set e = 1 and the formula is the perfect mirror; the table's surfaces are the same formula with the dial turned down — or, at the powered faces, with a kick added along n̂ after it.
vtip = ω × L  =  24 rad/s × 72 units ≈ 1,728 units/s The flipper's surface speed grows with distance from the pivot — the lever in its rotational form. The flipper's angular speed tops out at ω = 24 rad/s (it crosses its 0.98-radian swing in ≈ 0.041 s); the tip, 72 units out, sweeps at 1,728 units/s, while a contact at mid-flipper (36 units) rides a surface moving 864. The collision code computes the contact point's velocity exactly this way — v = ω × r, perpendicular to the flipper arm — and reflects the ball's velocity relative to that moving surface (with e = 0.62 on a rising swing, 0.42 on a resting flipper, plus a small extra snap on clean rising hits). Where you catch the ball on the bat is a speed choice, not just an angle choice.

One more number worth carrying onto the table: the speed that would circle Kepler's star at 100 units is v = √(a·r) = √(992 × 100) ≈ 315 units/s. Balls crossing the well's neighbourhood much faster than that get bent and released; balls crossing slower get bent and kept, at least for a while — which is the difference between Example 1's two passes below.

Worked examples — flown through the game's own physics

These were run with the game's well formula, softening, cap, drag, and step size (8 substeps per frame — 480 steps a second) lifted out of the shipped file and flown by a script, in the open, with the table's furniture removed and the slope switched off, so the well's own numbers stand alone. Receipts, not illustrations.

Example 1: one well, three passes — steering wheel, then trap

Three balls fired horizontally at Kepler Classic's star (centre 240, 425), all at the same height above it or slower:

PassClosest approachExit speedHeading change
650 units/s, passing 150 units above centre137 units559 units/sBent ≈ 21° toward the well
650 units/s, passing 100 units above centre84 units549 units/sBent ≈ 31° toward the well
400 units/s, passing 100 units above centre62 units139 units/s, still circling after 4 sBent ≈ 115° — captured into a decaying orbit

Closer bends more, as the formula promises. But watch the speeds: the fast passes leave slower than they arrived (650 → 549), and the well is not the thief. Run the middle pass again with the game's drag switched off and the ball leaves at ≈ 659 units/s — the well kept nothing (the small surplus is the stepping method's rounding on a close pass). The missing ~100 units/s is the table's air drag, 0.996 of your speed kept per 1/60 s — about 21% a second — which is also why the slow pass never escapes: bent into a circle, it pays the drag toll every lap until the well or a wall settles the account. On the real table the slope is also pulling, the bumpers sit in the way, and a captured ball usually ends as a core sling (+300) or a drain — but the trap itself is pure gravity, and now you've seen its numbers.

Example 2: the wall pays you back 72%; the bumper pays you a salary

The reflection formula above, at two surfaces. The wall: 500 in, 416 out — a bounce is a transaction with a fee, and e is the fee schedule. Now dribble into a bumper at a lazy 200 units/s. The bumper ignores the reflection arithmetic for speed and applies its own rule — out at max(510, 1.12 × in) — so you leave at 510 units/s: about 6.5× the kinetic energy you arrived with (energy grows with speed squared). Arrive fast instead, at 800, and you leave at 896 — the 1.12 multiplier on top. The bumper is a solenoid wearing a mirror's clothes: the direction still obeys the mirror rule (out along the radius, away from the centre), but the speed is whatever the house says. The well's core does the same trick with a twist — out at max(460, 1.08 × in), aimed 0.82 along the outward normal and 0.58 along the tangent, so a core graze doesn't just bounce you, it swirls you: the "+CORE SLING!" is the gravity assist from Gravity Golf, cashed at pinball speed.

Example 3: what a clean flipper strike buys

Take the flipper's receipt: a tip strike putting the ball straight up the table at 1,000 units/s (well within the tip's 1,728 units/s sweep, under the 1,480 cap). The slope gravity of 640 units/s² is the fare for climbing. Rise = v² / 2g = 1,000,000 / 1,280 ≈ 781 units — and the climb from the flipper line (y = 732) to the O-R-B rollover lanes (y ≈ 108) is only 624 units. The ball arrives at the lanes still carrying √(1,000,000 − 2 × 640 × 624) ≈ 449 units/s. That is why a solid flipper hit reaches the top of this table and a dead one dies in the slingshots: the difference between the two is not luck, it is where on the 72-unit lever the ball was standing — pivot-side contacts ride a surface moving at a few hundred units/s, tip contacts ride one moving at 1,728, and the rise formula squares whatever you bring.

How it's built — the coding choices

480 steps a second, in slices the frame rate can't spoil

The Table card for this game says "480Hz Substeps," and the code means it literally: each screen frame's time (clamped to at most 0.033 s, so a hiccup can't teleport the ball) is divided into 8 substeps — at a 60 fps display that is 480 physics steps a second, each about 2 milliseconds of game time. The stepping itself is semi-implicit Euler: update velocity from the forces, then update position from the new velocity. That ordering matters — it is the cheap integrator that doesn't leak energy the way naive Euler does — and at 480 Hz its remaining error is small enough that a ball can circle a well for seconds without the arithmetic inventing or destroying the orbit. Speed still needed one guardrail, applied every step: no ball may exceed 1,480 units/s (which is, not coincidentally, exactly the plunger's maximum launch: 860 + full-power 620).

The whole table is line segments, and one routine bounces them all

Every wall, guide, apron, and deflector is a segment with two endpoints and its own restitution value; even the great arch over the top is 34 short segments approximating the curve. One collision routine serves them all: find the closest point on the segment to the ball's centre, and if the ball is closer than its radius plus the segment's thickness, push it out along the normal and apply the reflection formula. A table's personality — dead apron, lively guides, springy deflectors — is then just data: the e column of the segment list. The flippers are segments too, but theirs rotate, so their routine adds the surface velocity ω × r before reflecting, and the slingshots are segments with a kick field — the same routine, an extra shove along the normal. Circles get their own variants of the same shape: bumpers (replace the speed), the well cores (sling it), the moons (bounce in the moon's moving frame, restitution 0.92, plus 140) — one idea, tuned per surface.

Moons are clocks, and their velocity is a difference

The moons and the Binary Stars wells don't simulate their orbits; they read them: angle advances by a scripted rate, position is centre + radius × (cos, sin) of that angle. But a collision with a moon needs the moon's velocity, and a position formula has none — so the code derives it the honest numerical way, as (this step's position − last step's position) / dt. Kepler's moons thereby sweep at ≈ 128 and ≈ 125 units/s, the Singularity's fast pair at ≈ 202 — real velocities the bounce arithmetic can borrow, from bodies whose orbits are pure timetable. (Whether the timetable agrees with the gravity is another question — see Simplifications.)

The rest of the bench

Scoring is a table of stated numbers — bumpers 250 and 350, moons 350, core slings 300, slingshots 150, drop targets 400, rollovers 200, a warp lock 750, multiball 2,500 — run through a multiplier (up to 8×) that the drop targets and the O-R-B lanes raise, and the high score persists in the browser's local storage. The sound is synthesized live with WebAudio, no audio files: one oscillator-and-envelope routine, and the bumpers play notes from a pentatonic scale — which is why a busy table sounds musical instead of random; any five notes of that scale agree with each other. And the tilt is real: the nudge key shoves every ball and fills a tilt meter (decaying at 0.45/s); at 3.2 the machine locks the flippers for 3 seconds, the way the arcade original would. Even the field arrows are drawn from the same well function the physics uses — with one honest mismatch: during a repel pulse the arrows flip using a polarity of −1 while the physics itself pushes at −1.45, so for that second the picture slightly understates the shove.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the world is a flat 2D table in the game's own units throughout. The moons and the Binary Stars ride scripted circular rails at rates the game's own gravity would not sustain: Kepler's inner moon circles at 1.45 rad/s, but the well's pull at its 88-unit orbit (1,208 units/s²) would need ≈ 3.71 rad/s to hold a true circle — the moons are animated, not orbiting, and they exert no gravity of their own; only the wells pull. The wells themselves are softened (+2600) and capped (1,600 units/s²), so the pull never reaches Newton's values near the centre — and the centre is solid: you cannot fall in, you sling off the core's surface. The ball feels no well gravity while it sits in the plunger lane (an exemption that keeps the lane a lane). Drag is real and strong — about 21% of speed per second — so no orbit on this table is permanent. The ball has no spin and the walls no friction: a segment bounce never touches the along-surface velocity, so there is no throw, no curve off a cushion, nothing like the pool table's spin story. The wormhole is a teleport plus a relaunch: whatever speed you carried in is discarded, and you exit at a fixed 690 units/s on a fixed line. The repel pulse is gravity with its sign flipped (−1.45× for 1.15 s), not a new force. And a hidden hand keeps the game moving: a ball nearly stopped for two seconds outside the lane receives a small random "anti-stall" kick — a real table would need a shake, and this one shakes itself.
