Docking — the lesson

Docking is a rendezvous game: a station is circling a planet, you have a small ship and a finite tank, and the only way to dock is to arrive at the station moving with it — inside 7 units, slower than 0.90 units/s relative to it. The game runs on plain Newtonian gravity and nothing else. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, the math underneath, the solutions its own proof flights found, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The physics

One force, always pulling home

There is exactly one force acting on both craft: the planet's gravity, pulling each of them straight toward the planet's centre, weaker with distance. Your engine is the only other thing that can change your motion, and it is feeble — at the station's altitude, gravity pulls at 4.0 units/s² and your engine pushes at 4.0 units/s². You are never stronger than the planet. Everything in the game is negotiation with that pull.

An orbit is falling and missing

A circular orbit is not floating. The station is falling toward the planet every second — gravity bends its path continuously — but it moves sideways fast enough that the planet's surface curves away beneath it, so it falls around instead of down. At the station's distance (100 units), that sideways speed is exactly 20 units/s. Slower, and the path dips toward the planet; faster, and it bulges outward. An orbit is a speed as much as a place.

There is no "straight at it" in orbit

On the ground, pointing at something and pushing takes you toward it. In orbit, a burn does not mainly change where you are pointing — it changes which orbit you are on. Push forward and you don't simply speed along your path: your orbit grows on the far side, your lap gets longer, and you arrive at where the station was on a schedule it doesn't keep. The station is a moving target riding the same physics, so rendezvous is never an aiming problem. It is a matching problem: first match the station's orbit, then arrange to be at the same point of it at the same time, then arrive gently.

Phasing: to catch up, slow down

This is the game's first lesson and the one every instinct argues with. If you are behind the station on the same orbit, burning forward lifts you into a bigger orbit — and a bigger orbit is a slower lap, so you fall further behind. Burn backward instead and you drop into a smaller orbit with a shorter lap: you gain on the station every circuit, the way a runner on the inside lane gains. Then, as you close in, burn forward again to climb back onto the station's orbit and arrive on its path instead of swinging underneath it. Catch up by slowing down; arrive by speeding up. Both halves feel wrong. Both halves are just the period formula, below.

Up close, everything is relative

Near the station, what matters stops being your orbit and starts being the difference: how far apart you are (the range), how fast that gap is closing, and your relative speed — the size of the velocity difference between the two craft. Docking is a velocity-matching act: inside 7 units with relative speed under 0.90 units/s, the capture ring takes you. Touch closer than 4.2 units while moving faster than that and it is a collision, because that is what it would be. The last few units are flown on small taps, watching the relative-speed readout, not the map.

Δv is the currency

Orbital pilots don't budget fuel in litres; they budget Δv — "delta-v," the total change in velocity your engine can still buy you. The game's tank is measured in Δv directly: the engine accelerates at 4.0 units/s², so one second of burn costs 4 Δv, and Level 1's tank of 16 is four full seconds of engine, total, for the whole mission. Every burn is a purchase. The levels are priced so that the idea is always affordable and wandering is not.

The math the game runs

Three formulas run the whole world. Each is stated the way the code states it, then used with the game's own numbers. The game's gravity constant is μ = 40,000 units³/s² (μ is just "how strong this planet's gravity is," packaged as one number).

a = μ / r² Gravity's acceleration at distance r from the planet's centre, aimed at the centre. At the station's orbit, r = 100: a = 40,000 / 100² = 4.0 units/s² — exactly your engine's push. At the surface, r = 16, it is 40,000 / 16² ≈ 156 units/s², which is why falling home is final.
v = √(μ / r) The one speed that makes a circle at radius r: gravity's inward pull is exactly spent bending the path. At r = 100: v = √400 = 20 units/s — the station's speed, and your starting speed in Levels 1 and 2. Down at Level 3's r = 76: v = √(40,000 / 76) ≈ 22.9 units/s.
T = 2π √(r³ / μ) The lap time (period) of a circular orbit — Kepler's third law for circles. At r = 100: T = 2π √25 = 10π ≈ 31.42 s, the station's lap. At r = 76: T ≈ 20.8 s. Read the phasing lesson off this formula: shrink r even a little and the lap shortens a lot, because r is cubed. That is the whole trick — a slightly smaller orbit is a noticeably faster clock.

Docking itself is arithmetic too: the game measures the gap between the craft and the size of their velocity difference every step, and the win is range < 7 and relative speed ≤ 0.90. No hidden judgement — the HUD shows you both numbers live, and the ring around the station turns green when your relative speed is gentle enough.

Worked examples — from the game's own proof flights

Before any human played Docking, a script flew it. The game's physics is written so a program can hold the controls, and the test that ships with the collection ran three checks. These are their actual numbers — receipts, not illustrations.

Example 1: the station's orbit does not rot

The script let the station fly five full laps — about 157 seconds, roughly 19,600 simulation steps — and measured its distance from the planet. The radius stayed at 100.0000 and the total drift over five periods was 2.16e-11 units: eleven decimal places of nothing. If the integrator leaked energy the way cheap ones do, the orbit would visibly spiral within a minute of play. It doesn't. (Why it doesn't is a coding choice — see How it's built.)

Example 2: the straight-at-it burn, measured (Level 2)

Level 2 starts you parked 13 units outside the station's orbit, right alongside it — the most tempting setup in the game. The script tried the obvious thing, twice, and the phasing answer once:

From 13 units outClosest approachRange after one orbitVerdict
Burn straight at the station, Δv 2.08.9 units114.0 unitsNever enters the 7-unit ring — ends the orbit nearly nine times worse than it started
Burn straight at the station, harder, Δv 3.04.2 units—Arrives — as a crash
One retrograde phasing burn, Δv 1.04.3 units23.1 unitsInside the ring within one orbit, still in the neighbourhood

The gentle straight burn is the instructive failure: it feels careful, and it bends your path into an ellipse that crosses the station's orbit exactly where the station isn't, then carries you out to 114 units. The burn that works is the smallest one on the table, pointed the "wrong" way.

Example 3: what a whole rendezvous costs

The scripted pilot — a planner that picks phasing burns by the period formula, then a servo that nudges the final approach — docked all three levels:

Level 1's 3.04 Δv is about three-quarters of a second of total engine time. Rendezvous is cheap when the orbit does the work — and Level 2, the "easy-looking" one, is the expensive one, because fixing a radial offset costs more than fixing a schedule.

Level 1 as a flight plan you can imitate

Same orbit as the station, 55 units behind it, 16 Δv in the tank. The pilot's strategy, in human-sized steps:

  1. Don't speed up. Every instinct says burn forward (W) to chase. That lifts your orbit and lengthens your lap — you will fall behind faster. Leave W alone.
  2. Tap S — one short retrograde burn, roughly 1 Δv (about a quarter-second at full thrust; the gauge drains 4 per second of burn). You drop into a slightly smaller orbit. Nothing dramatic happens, which is the point.
  3. Coast and watch the range fall. Your lap is now shorter than the station's (the period formula: smaller r, shorter T), so the 55-unit gap closes on its own, a little every second. This is the planet flying the rendezvous for you.
  4. As the gap closes toward ~15–20 units, tap W with roughly the burn you spent in step 2, to climb back onto the station's orbit. Time it so you arrive on its path — skip this and you'll swing past underneath it, close and useless.
  5. Final approach on taps only. Inside 20 units, think in relative numbers: D/A nudge you in and out, W/S adjust how fast the gap closes. Cross into the 7-unit ring with the relative-speed readout under 0.90 — the ring shows green when you're gentle enough — and hold it there.
  6. The receipt to aim at: the scripted pilot docked this level in 23.0 seconds using 3.04 of the 16 Δv. If you're burning more than that, the orbit is doing less of the work than it wants to.

How it's built — the coding choices

Docking is one HTML file with no libraries, and its code is arranged as a lesson too. Three choices do most of the work.

RK4 with small substeps, not naive Euler

The simplest way to move a simulated ship is Euler integration: compute the force, step everything forward, repeat. For orbits it has a quiet flaw — each step adds a whisper of energy that isn't in the physics, so a simulated orbit slowly spirals outward and the station's "circle" rots into an egg, then an escape. The game instead integrates with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method: four trial slopes per step, averaged, which cancels most of that error — and it takes those steps in slices no bigger than 0.008 seconds of game time, however uneven the screen's frame rate gets. The receipt is Example 1 above: five laps, drift of 2.16e-11 units. Accuracy here isn't polish; the whole game is "trust the orbit," so the orbit has to be trustworthy.

The physics is a pure-function core, so a script can fly the ship

Everything physical lives in one sealed block of code (it is literally fenced between two marker comments, PHYSICS-CORE-START and PHYSICS-CORE-END): a function that takes a state — eight numbers, both craft's positions and velocities — plus the controls and a slice of time, and returns the next state. It knows nothing about the screen, the keyboard, or the page. That separation is why the proof flights on this page exist: the test program lifts that exact block out of the shipped file, hands it scripted controls, and flies. The levels were proven winnable — and the straight-at-it trap proven real — by the same code you play, not a copy of it. The pale prediction curve ahead of your ship is the same idea from the other side: it's the core run forward with the engine off. Not a drawing trick — the actual physics, rehearsed.

Thrust is mapped to the ship's frame, not the screen's

The keys don't mean screen directions. W is "along your motion," S "against it," D "away from the planet," A "toward it" — and the code rebuilds those directions from your ship's own velocity and position every substep, because in orbit they rotate as you fly. Screen-up stops meaning anything within seconds of leaving the pad; the ship's frame is the frame the physics itself thinks in, and it's the frame real rendezvous is flown in. It costs the player one honest adjustment — and it is the adjustment the game exists to teach.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the planet is a point mass — all its gravity acts from its centre, with no oblateness or lumpiness. The world is a flat 2D plane; real rendezvous also manages an out-of-plane component. There is no drag, no third body, no perturbation of any kind. The station flies a fixed, unpowered circular orbit and never manoeuvres or notices you. Fuel is metered directly as Δv: the ship's mass never changes as it burns, so there is no rocket-equation model underneath (the game's other rocket, in Stage It, is where that equation gets its own game). Thrust is a constant acceleration available instantly in the four local-frame directions — there is no attitude model, no waiting for the ship to rotate. Hitting the planet is a simple radius check. The numbers are the game's own units throughout, not metres and kilograms dressed up as them.
