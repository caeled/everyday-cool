Marble Run — the lesson

Marble Run is a builder: you assemble chain-reaction machines from ramps, trampolines, conveyors, seesaws, pinwheels, dominoes, bumpers, and wind fans, then drop marbles and watch the machine run. Underneath the confetti, every machine you build is one long energy trade — height becomes speed, speed becomes height, and the interesting pieces either tax the trade or cheat it. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples computed from its own constants, and how it is built.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied by Steven; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool, from reading its code.

The physics

One trade, over and over: height ⇄ speed

Gravity in the game pulls at 760 px/s² (the Gravity slider scales it, 0.25× to 1.8×). A marble held still at the top of your machine owns a store of potential energy — height it hasn't spent yet. Let go and gravity converts that store into motion, pixel for pixel: the further it falls, the faster it arrives, and the exchange rate is exact (the formula is below). A ramp is just a way of spending height gradually instead of all at once in a fall — the marble reaches the bottom of a ramp with the same speed it would have gotten by dropping straight down the same height. Then the next climb converts the speed back into height. Every ramp, drop, and climb in every machine is this one trade, repeated.

Where the trade leaks: bounces and thin air

Two taxes are collected on every run. The first is the bounce tax. When a marble strikes a ramp, only the part of its velocity aimed into the surface bounces, and it bounces back at 0.48 of what came in (the Bounciness slider sets this, 0.1 to 0.92). Energy goes as the square of speed, so a bounce at the default setting keeps only 0.48² ≈ 23% of the incoming bounce energy — the rest leaves as the sound and shudder a real marble would make. Grazing hits along a ramp barely pay; head-on hits pay heavily. The second tax is air drag: every moment of flight, a marble's speed is multiplied by 0.9985 per 1/60th of a second — about an 8.6% speed loss per second of travel, roughly 16.5% of its energy. Long flights and tall machines pay more tax than short ones. What the game does not have is friction between marble and track — see Simplifications, because it matters.

Where the trade is cheated: the powered pieces

Half the palette exists to put energy in, and a good machine is bookkeeping about which pieces tax and which pieces pay:

  • Trampoline — a spring with a motor behind it. It returns the incoming bounce at 1.05× plus a flat kick of 290 px/s, every time. It never just gives your energy back; it gives back more than you brought.
  • Bumper peg — pinball rules. Whatever speed you arrive with, you leave at at least 320 px/s (or 1.08× your arrival speed, if that is more), fired straight out from the peg's centre. A marble that drifts into a peg at a crawl leaves like it was shot.
  • Conveyor — a motorised belt (240–300 px/s in the presets). While a marble touches it, the belt drags the marble's along-track speed toward its own, closing about a quarter of the gap per contact step. It will speed a slow marble up or haul a fast one back — either way, the motor pays or absorbs the difference.
  • Wind fan — a cone of moving air that pushes at 1,350–1,550 px/s² near the fan, fading to 55% of that at the cone's far end. Gravity is 760. Near the fan the wind wins outright, which is how the preset machines lift marbles up shafts: a fan is a gravity override with a range limit.
  • Motorised pinwheel — a spinner with its motor on holds its rotation speed and swats marbles with it. Flip it to free-spin and it becomes an energy sink instead: marbles push it, it slows them, and its own spin bleeds away at 0.985 per frame-tick.

This is why the preset called Perpetual Loop loops forever: not because the physics allows perpetual motion — the taxes above see to that — but because its conveyor and two stacked fans keep paying the taxes. It is a powered loop wearing a perpetual-motion name tag, and noticing the difference is most of the lesson.

The pieces that trade in rotation

Seesaws and dominoes trade in turning instead of rolling. A seesaw is a rigid beam on a pivot: a marble landing on one end delivers an impulse, the beam rotates, and the other end can catapult a second marble — linear energy in, rotation, linear energy out, with the pivot's inverse inertia (1/14500 in the code) deciding how much of the hit becomes swing. A falling domino is an upside-down pendulum: once its tilt passes a few degrees, gravity's torque grows with the tilt itself — α = G × 1.45 × sin(tilt) / h — so it falls slowly, then all at once, and hands 88% of its swing speed to the next tile in the row. A domino run is potential energy that was stored by whoever stood the tiles up, released one tile at a time.

The loop-the-loop this game doesn't have

There is no loop piece in the palette — but the physics that makes loops hard is worth learning here, because you can fake a loop from a dozen short ramps arranged in a circle, and the game will charge you the real price. A marble at the top of a loop is falling even while it follows the track: gravity pulls it away from the rail, and only its speed keeps it pressed against it. The contact holds only while the track has to push inward with at least nothing — which works out to a minimum top speed of v ≥ √(g·r) for a loop of radius r. Arrive at the top slower than that and the marble simply leaves the rail and falls through the middle, which is the loop's way of billing you: the top of the loop charges centripetal force, payable in speed only. The math the game runs

The marbles have no mass in the code — every marble is the same 11 px circle — so the game's energy story is kept per unit mass, in two lines:

PE = g × h   ·   KE = ½ v² Potential and kinetic energy per unit mass, with g = 760 px/s² and h the height in pixels. A marble resting 100 px above a catch point holds 76,000 units of PE; arriving at the bottom it holds the same total as KE if nothing taxed the trip.
v = √(2 g h) The exchange rate between height and speed. Fall (or roll down) h pixels from rest and you arrive at this speed, whether the path was a cliff or a gentle ramp — the path changes the trip, not the trade. At g = 760: a 100 px drop buys √(2 × 760 × 100) ≈ 390 px/s; the full 660 px height of the canvas buys ≈ 1,002 px/s.
KE kept in a bounce = e² e is the restitution — the fraction of bounce speed returned. Ramps run at the Bounciness slider's value (0.48 by default), so a square bounce keeps 0.48² ≈ 23% of its normal-direction energy. The trampoline's e is 1.05 plus its 290 px/s kick, which is why it is a power source wearing a pad's clothes. Marble-on-marble collisions use e = 0.63 at default settings and split the impulse evenly — equal marbles, equal shares.
v² ≥ g × r   at the top of a loop The loop condition: at the top, gravity alone must not exceed the centripetal requirement v²/r. Below this speed the marble leaves the rail. Combined with the energy trade, it means a loop of radius r must be entered at √(5 g r) or better — and the drop that buys that entry speed starts at least 2.5 × r above the loop's floor, before taxes.

One honest note about the ledger: unlike its sibling Pendulum Grapple, which draws live KE/PE bars on screen, Marble Run shows no energy numbers at all — its telemetry counts only marbles in the goal and marbles rolling. The ledger above is one you keep in your head while you build, and learning to estimate it — "that drop is worth about 400, the bounce will tax three quarters of the head-on part" — is the skill the game is quietly training.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Every number below is computed from constants read out of the shipped code, at the default gravity of 760 px/s².

Example 1: the Grand Goldberg's first ramp

The opening preset spawns marbles above a ramp that runs from height y = 120 down to y = 185 — a 65 px drop spread over 260 px of travel. A marble rolling that ramp from rest reaches the bottom at √(2 × 760 × 65) ≈ 314 px/s, exactly the speed a 65 px free fall would give it. The ramp didn't change the trade; it changed the direction of the arrival — the speed now points along the machine instead of at the floor. That is all any ramp ever does, and it's enough to build everything else on.

Example 2: the trampoline pays you

Drop a marble 105 px onto a trampoline. It arrives at √(2 × 760 × 105) ≈ 400 px/s. The pad returns 1.05 × 400 + 290 ≈ 710 px/s — and a marble leaving upward at 710 px/s climbs 710² / (2 × 760) ≈ 331 px before gravity stops it. It fell 105 and rose 331: the pad donated the difference, roughly 226 px of height, out of its own pocket. Chain two trampolines and the donation compounds — which is how the Perpetual preset's trampoline throws marbles up to a conveyor they could never have reached on their own energy.

Example 3: a bumper peg is a power supply

A marble that has spent almost everything — crawling at 100 px/s — touches a bumper peg. The code sends it out at max(320, 1.08 × 100) = 320 px/s, straight away from the peg's centre. Its kinetic energy per unit mass goes from ½ × 100² = 5,000 to ½ × 320² = 51,200 — 10.2× the energy it arrived with. The Musical Plinko preset is a field of these: the board looks like a gravity toy, but the pegs are quietly re-energising every marble that touches them, which is why the music never dies down at the bottom of the board the way a real Plinko's would.

Example 4: pricing a loop the game makes you build yourself

Say you ring twelve short ramps into a loop of radius 110 px. The top of the loop demands v ≥ √(760 × 110) ≈ 289 px/s. Getting to the top costs 2 × 110 px of climb on top of that, so the entry speed at the loop's floor must be √(289² + 2 × 760 × 220) ≈ 647 px/s — and buying 647 px/s from rest takes a drop of 647² / (2 × 760) = 275 px, exactly 2.5 × the radius, the classic textbook answer. That is the price before taxes: every ramp joint in your hand-built ring is a small bounce-tax collection point, and air drag skims the whole trip, so in practice you start higher, enter faster, or feed the loop with a trampoline. Real roller-coaster loops are teardrop-shaped for a related reason — a smaller radius at the top lowers the √(g·r) bill exactly where the marble is poorest.

Example 5: the fan that almost can't

The Grand Goldberg's wind fan pushes at 1,350 px/s² with a range of 210 px, and its push fades to 55% at the cone's end: 1,350 × 0.55 ≈ 743 px/s² — just under gravity's 760. Near the fan the wind wins by a mile; at the top of its range it loses by a hair, so a marble riding that column needs a little arrival speed to coast the last stretch — the preset gives it some, via the conveyor below. The Perpetual Loop's fans are stronger (1,550) and stacked in pairs up a shaft: 1,550 × 0.55 ≈ 853 px/s² at worst, above gravity everywhere, so that elevator never stalls. Two presets, two fan budgets, and the difference between "lifts, with help" and "lifts, period" is one multiplication you can do on the settings panel's behalf before you ever press Play.

How it's built — the coding choices

Marble Run is one HTML file, no libraries, and its whole world is built from two shapes.

Everything is a segment or a circle

Ramps, trampolines, and conveyors are stored as line segments — two endpoints and a type. Seesaw beams and pinwheel blades are segments that rotate. A goal cup is three segments in a U. Bumpers are circles. The collision code therefore needs exactly one piece of geometry, used everywhere: closestPointOnSeg, which finds the nearest point on a segment to the marble's centre. If that distance is less than the marble's radius plus the track's thickness, the marble is pushed out along the line from the segment to its centre, and its velocity is split into a normal part (into the surface) and a tangential part (along it). The normal part is bounced with the piece's restitution; the tangential part is left alone — or, on a conveyor, dragged toward belt speed. One function, honestly applied, is the entire track physics. A young builder can steal this wholesale: most "physics engines" start as exactly this.

Small steps, taken eight at a time

The simulation advances with semi-implicit Euler integration — update velocity from forces, then update position from velocity — in 8 substeps per frame, with the frame time clamped at 0.032 s so a slow screen can't take a giant step. Substeps are what keep fast marbles honest: at 1,000 px/s a marble crosses 17 px per frame, more than its own radius, so a single step per frame would let it tunnel through thin ramps. Eight slices bring that to about 2 px per step — the marble can no longer jump a wall without meeting it. Speed is also capped at 1,300 px/s, a number the canvas justifies: reaching it from rest would take a drop of 1,112 px, and the canvas is only 660 tall, so the cap is a safety rail the physics can rarely touch.

The rotating pieces use real impulses

Seesaws, pinwheels, and dominoes aren't faked with canned animations. A marble striking one delivers an impulse at the contact point; the code divides by an inverse moment of inertia (1/14500 for a seesaw; 1/6500 for a free pinwheel, 1/45000 for a motorised one — the motor makes it effectively heavier, so marbles barely slow it) and converts the blow into angular velocity, while the marble keeps the rest of the exchange. That's why a heavy hit on a seesaw's long end catapults properly and a glancing one doesn't: the lever arm (the cross product of contact offset and impulse direction) is in the arithmetic, not in a lookup table.

The machine is data, and the presets prove it

Every piece is a plain object in one array — {type, x1, y1, x2, y2} for a ramp, {type, x, y, r} for a bumper. The five presets, including the Grand Goldberg, are just literal lists of those objects constructed by one function. Undo is a stack of JSON snapshots of the array, 40 deep: save the whole machine as text, restore it by parsing. And there is no separate "build mode" and "play mode" — editing is live while the sim runs, because when your world is one array of data and one step function, building and playing are the same screen. The music is data too: a collision's note is picked from an 11-note scale by the height of the hit, so a machine's melody is a map of where its marbles are striking — the soundtrack is telemetry you can hear.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there is no surface friction anywhere in the code — a marble's along-track speed is never taxed by contact, so on a level ramp a marble rolls until air drag alone stops it, which takes a while. (The game's "friction losses," such as they are, are the bounce tax and the drag.) Rolling is drawn, not simulated: on contact the marble's spin is assigned from its along-track speed (ω = vt/r) to animate the swirl you see, but spin stores no energy and costs none — a real rolling marble banks 2/7 of its kinetic energy in rotation and accelerates down a ramp at 5/7 of the sliding rate, while this marble accelerates at the full sliding rate like a frictionless puck wearing a rolling costume. There is no loop or funnel piece in the palette; loops are a build-it-yourself project (Example 4). Powered pieces create energy — trampolines, bumpers, conveyors, fans, motorised pinwheels — with no fuel or power budget modelled; the "Perpetual Loop" is a powered loop. Marbles have no mass and are all identical; nothing in the game displays PE or KE numbers. Air drag is a flat exponential decay (×0.9985 per 1/60 s) in all directions, not a speed-squared law. Gravity is uniform everywhere — no weakening with height. The rigid pieces use tuned constants (a domino's toppling torque carries a hand-set 1.45 factor) rather than measured properties. The numbers are the game's own pixels throughout, not metres dressed up as them.
