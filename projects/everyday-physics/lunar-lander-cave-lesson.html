Lunar Lander — the lesson

Lunar Lander — Thruster Cave drops you into five caverns with a rocket engine, a tank of propellant, and one job: set the ship down on a glowing pad, gently, before the tank runs dry. What makes it more than a reaction test is that the ship changes as you fly it — every second of burn throws mass overboard, so the same engine pushes a lighter ship harder. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples from its own numbers, and how it is built — readable while you play.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to Everyday Cool by Steven Powell. This lesson page was written for Everyday Cool to explain what is inside it.

The physics

Two forces, and a ship that keeps getting lighter

Only two forces ever act on the lander. Gravity pulls it straight down, at a rate fixed by the mission — 66 units/s² in Tranquility Crater, up to a punishing 118 on the High-G Super-Earth. The main engine pushes along the ship's own axis with a fixed force of 248 (the code's comment calls them "Newtons-equivalent"). Newton's second law, a = F / m, does the rest — and here is the twist the whole game hangs on: m is not constant. The ship's mass is its dry mass of 1.0 t plus whatever propellant remains, and a full gauge adds 0.75 t. Every burn shrinks the denominator. The force never changes; the ship it pushes does.

TWR: why the same burn lifts harder late in the flight

Pilots summarise this with the thrust-to-weight ratio: how many times your own weight your engine can push. The game's flight computer computes it live, every frame, from the current mass and the mission's gravity. On Tranquility you launch at a TWR of about 2.15 — comfortable. But watch the readout as the tank drains: by the time you are nearly dry it reads about 3.76. Nothing about the engine improved. The weight fell. This is why landings get twitchy near the end of a long flight: a burn length you learned at the start of the mission now delivers far more acceleration than it did, and a tap that was a nudge becomes a leap. Real lander crews plan around exactly this. So does anyone who has ever carried a heavy backpack uphill and noticed the last mile feels easier per step — except here it happens in ninety seconds.

Tilt is steering — and it costs you lift

The engine is bolted to the ship, so it pushes wherever the ship points. The code splits the thrust by the ship's angle: the sideways part goes as sin(angle), the upward part as cos(angle). Upright, all 248 units fight gravity and none move you sideways. Tilt to chase a pad and you are spending lift to buy sideways motion — at the maximum safe landing tilt of 16.5° you still keep about 96% of your lift, but at 45° you would keep only about 71%, while gravity keeps all of its share. Every sideways correction is paid for out of the vertical budget. That trade — tilt to travel, level to climb — is the entire flying skill of the game, and of the real Apollo landings.

Spin, RCS torque, and what SAS actually does

The A and D keys fire reaction-control thrusters — small jets that don't push the ship anywhere; they turn it. In the code this is an angular acceleration of 4.6 / mass, so rotation obeys the same shrinking-mass rule as everything else: a dry ship spins up about 75% faster than a full one (2.63 rad/s² at full tanks, 4.6 rad/s² dry). Left alone, a spin mostly persists — the code applies only a whisper of natural damping (angular velocity multiplied by 0.992 per frame-step). The SAS Attitude Stabilizer is the fix, and it is worth being precise about what it does: when SAS is on and you are not actively steering, it multiplies your spin rate by 0.86 per frame-step — your rotation halves roughly every 0.08 seconds, and about 90% of it is gone in a quarter second. But SAS only brakes spin. It never rotates you back upright. If you stop spinning at a 20° tilt, SAS will hold you proudly at 20° until the floor arrives. Stopping the turn and fixing the tilt are two different jobs, and the game makes you do the second one yourself.

Landing is a velocity budget, not a place

You do not land by reaching the pad; you land by reaching the pad slowly enough, level enough, with both feet on it. The code checks three numbers at the moment a foot touches: downward speed under 78, sideways speed under 56, tilt under 16.5° — and both feet inside the pad's span. Miss any one and the same code path that lands you instead calls the explosion. There is no "hard landing" in between; the envelope is the whole story. Notice what that does to your flying: altitude is not safety, speed is the budget, and the last hundred units of descent are spent buying speed down with propellant — propellant that was also your ride home across the cave. (One honest wrinkle: the game's avionics panel prints a slightly stricter envelope — 76 / 55 / 16° — than the 78 / 56 / 16.5° the code actually enforces. Fly to the printed numbers and you have a small margin in hand; the code forgives a little more than the panel admits.)

The cryo-geyser: a third force with a mood

Mission 5 adds a column of rising gas venting from the cave floor. Inside the column the ship feels an upward force of 165 / mass — strongest at the vent (a factor of 1.0) and fading to 0.45 of full strength at the top of the 380-unit column, the way a real plume spreads and weakens as it rises. Near the vent, a fully fuelled ship (mass 1.8625 t) feels about 88.6 units/s² of lift against that mission's gravity of 74 — the geyser can genuinely hold you up, or throw you at the ceiling if you wander in heavy and high. And the code stirs in a small random twist to your spin while you are inside it, because a turbulent column would. It is a force you can use and a force you must respect, which is what makes it a good level and a good physics exhibit at once.

The math the game runs

Four formulas run the whole world. Each is stated the way the code states it, then used with the game's own numbers.

m = 1.0 + 0.75 × (fuel / 100) The ship's mass in tonnes, recomputed from the fuel gauge every physics step (the getShipMass() function). A full 100-unit tank means m = 1.75 t; the cave missions that start with 115 units of fuel start at m = 1.8625 t. Empty is always 1.0 t — the dry ship.
a = F / m,   with F = 248 The engine's acceleration, recomputed with the current mass. On Tranquility at launch: 248 / 1.75 ≈ 141.7 units/s² against gravity's 66. Nearly dry: 248 / 1.0 = 248 units/s². The main engine drains the gauge at 11.5 units per second, so a 100-unit tank holds about 8.7 seconds of total burn — that number, more than any other, is the game's difficulty.
TWR = 248 / (m × g) Thrust divided by weight — the flight computer's live readout. TWR above 1 means full thrust can hold you against gravity; how far above 1 is how much acceleration you have left over to manoeuvre with. Tranquility, launch: 248 / (1.75 × 66) ≈ 2.15. Tranquility, nearly dry: 248 / 66 ≈ 3.76. High-G world, launch: 248 / (1.825 × 118) ≈ 1.15 — see Example 3 for what that feels like.
Δv = ve · ln(mfull / mdry) — the rocket equation, arriving uninvited The code never writes this formula. It just applies a = F / m step by step while m falls. But add up what that process delivers over one full tank and the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation falls out of the arithmetic: fuel mass leaves at 0.08625 t/s under a force of 248, an effective exhaust velocity of 248 / 0.08625 ≈ 2,875 units/s, and a mass ratio of 1.75 / 1.0. Total velocity change available on a 100-unit tank: 2,875 × ln(1.75) ≈ 1,610 units/s — before gravity takes its cut (a continuous vertical burn on Tranquility would hand gravity about 66 × 8.7 ≈ 574 of those units back). This is the deep reason the shrinking-mass rule matters: a rocket's budget is set by the ratio of full to empty mass, not by the size of the tank — which is why the game's other rocket, in Stage It, is built around throwing empty structure away.

The engine's push per tonne rises 1.75× across the flight — but your net climb-away acceleration rises about 2.4×, because gravity's 66 units/s² is a fixed tax taken off the top before you get anything. Early in the flight most of your thrust is spent just standing still against gravity; late in the flight almost all of it is yours. This is why experienced players do their travelling early and their landing late — and why a landing burn timed by early-flight habits overshoots.

Example 2: how much sky does a redline descent cost?

The fastest survivable touchdown is a downward speed of 78. Suppose you are falling at exactly that speed and decide to brake to a stop above the pad. Braking distance is v² / (2a), with a your net upward acceleration:

Full tank (a = 75.7)Nearly dry (a = 182)
Braking distance from 78 units/s≈ 40 units of sky≈ 17 units of sky
Burn time to stop≈ 1.03 s≈ 0.43 s
Propellant spent stopping≈ 12 gauge units≈ 5 gauge units

The canvas is 620 units tall and the pads sit near the bottom, so 40 units of braking room is real estate you can run out of. The lesson the numbers teach: arrive light and the ship forgives late decisions; arrive heavy and it collects on them. Fuel is not just range — late in the flight, lightness itself is a safety system.

Example 3: the High-G world barely lets you off the ground (g = 118, fuel = 110)

On the High-G Super-Earth you start at m = 1.825 t, weighing 1.825 × 118 ≈ 215.4 against your engine's 248. TWR ≈ 1.15: at full throttle your net upward acceleration is 248 / 1.825 − 118 ≈ 17.9 units/s² — about a tenth of what the same burn gives a dry ship on Tranquility. The ship wallows. But every second of that wallowing burn makes it lighter, and by the time the tank is dry the same world gives you a TWR of 2.10. The mission is a lesson in the tyranny and the mercy of the mass ratio: on a high-gravity world, burning fuel is how you earn the ability to fly, and hovering early to "be careful" spends the very lightness you need. Real rocket designers know this world well — it is why real launch vehicles leave the pad slowly and accelerate hardest in their final minute.

How it's built — the coding choices

Lunar Lander is one HTML file, no libraries, and several of its construction choices are worth stealing outright.

The missions are data, not code

All five caverns live in one array of plain objects: a gravity number, a fuel load, a start position, two lists of points for floor and ceiling, a list of pads, a list of geysers. The engine code never mentions Tranquility or the Cryo-Geyser Shaft by name — it just reads whichever object is current. That is why the game can have five genuinely different levels (open crater, canyon, cave, high-gravity world, geyser shaft) for the price of one physics engine: a new level is a new paragraph of data. If you build one thing after reading this page, build your next game this way — content as data, rules as code.

Terrain is two polylines and one honest question

The cave is not a picture with collision painted on; it is two chains of points, and a single function answers the only question the physics ever asks: at this x, what is the terrain's y? It finds the segment you are over and interpolates linearly between its endpoints. The ship itself is tested at just three points — left foot, right foot, top dome — each rotated from the ship's local frame into the world by the same sin/cos split the thrust uses. Three point-against- polyline checks per step replace an entire polygon-collision library, and they ask the physically right questions: feet for landing, dome for the ceiling you forgot. Cheap, exact, and debuggable — when a landing misjudges, you can print three numbers and see why.

Small steps, velocity first

The frame loop clamps its timestep (never more than 0.032 s, however much the browser stalled) and runs the physics in four substeps per frame. Within each substep it updates velocities from the forces first, then moves positions by the new velocities — the semi-implicit Euler order, which behaves noticeably better than the naive order at the same cost. And the mass is re-read from the fuel gauge inside every substep, so the acceleration curve of a long burn is a smooth ramp, not a staircase. None of this is visible on screen. All of it is why the ship feels the same on a fast machine and a slow one.

One particle system, four jobs

There is a single particle list and a single spawn function. The main engine's flame, the pale RCS puffs, the geyser's spray, and the 42-piece explosion are all the same object — position, velocity, colour, radius, a life that fades the particle out — wearing different parameters. Debris from a crash is a second, tiny list of tumbling line segments that bounce off the floor polyline with the same terrain function. Writing one flexible system instead of four special effects is the difference between a game that gets finished and a folder of demos.

The sound is a noise buffer wearing a filter

The engine roar is not a recording. It is two seconds of random white noise, looped through a low-pass filter, with its volume tied to whether the engine is firing — static, tamed, becomes a rocket. The landing chime and crash are plain oscillator tones. The whole soundscape is about thirty lines, generated fresh by the Web Audio API every session, with no audio files to load. Generated media — noise for roar, math for terrain, parameters for particles — is a pattern this game uses everywhere, and it is why the file can be small and still feel rich.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: gravity is constant and straight down everywhere in a cave — the same at the ceiling as at the floor, with no weakening over distance and no round planet underneath. There is no atmosphere and therefore no drag on the ship: sideways speed never bleeds off on its own, which is why killing horizontal velocity is entirely your job. The engine is on/off only — there is no throttle, so hovering is done by pulsing burns, and "throttle" anywhere in this lesson means burn length. The ship is a point mass with three collision samples; it has no real moment of inertia, and the RCS turn rate of 4.6 / mass is a tuned number, not something computed from where the little jets sit (they are drawn at the ship's sides, but their geometry does no work). Propellant is an abstract gauge that maps linearly onto mass — 0.75 t per 100 units — and the RCS and main engine drink from the same tank at fixed rates (1.4 and 11.5 units per second) regardless of anything. The landing-gear "spring" in the game's subtitle is a drawing: on touchdown the legs squash visually, but the spring absorbs no energy — the velocity envelope is pass/fail. SAS is an idealised spin brake that never saturates and never runs out; real stabilisers have limits, and real ones also cannot right a ship, which part the game gets right. The geyser's fade with height is a hand-set formula, and its turbulence is a dice roll. The world's edges are the canvas edges, with soft bounces. Finally, the numbers are the game's own units, tuned for a 1000 × 620 stage: the relationships — mass ratio, TWR, torque, braking distance — are the real physics, but 66 units/s² of "lunar" gravity and a 78 units/s safe touchdown are arcade scale, not Apollo's. The real Lunar Module touched down slower than a person walking.
