Jelly — Soft-Body Platformer — the lesson

You are a blob of jelly: you roll, bounce, splat against floors, ooze through vents, and climb chimneys. There is no "jelly" object anywhere in the code. There are 20 point masses arranged in a ring, sewn together with springs, with a pretend gas inside pushing the skin outward — and everything the jelly does, from the landing splat to the post-bounce jiggle, falls out of those three ingredients. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples computed from the game's own numbers, and how it is built.

The physics

A ring of masses that remembers being a circle

The jelly's body is 20 identical point masses placed on a circle of radius 36 px, connected by springs in three families. Each spring has a rest length — the length it was born with — and a spring's whole personality is that it pulls its two ends back toward that length. Shape, for this jelly, is not a picture. It is 50 rest lengths the springs keep trying to remember:

Spring familyConnectsRest lengthStiffness weightJob
Skin springsEach node to its neighbour11.26 px1.0The surface itself — holds the ring together
Bend springsEach node to the one two steps away22.25 px0.52Resist sharp kinks; keep the skin smooth instead of crumpling
Spoke springsEach node to the one directly opposite72 px0.14Anti-flattening backbone — a pancake has to fight these

Stretch a spring and it pulls in; squash it short and it pushes out. That single rule, applied 60 times per step, is the jelly's entire skeleton. There are no bones, no angles, no "shape" stored anywhere — knock the blob into a new shape and the springs vote, every step, to go home.

The inside pushes back: pressure from enclosed area

Springs alone would make a floppy necklace. What makes the jelly feel inflated is the second ingredient: the game continuously measures the area enclosed by the ring (its "volume," in a 2D world) and compares it with the area the blob wants to have. Squeeze the blob smaller than its target area and a pressure builds that pushes every edge of the skin outward, along the direction perpendicular to that edge — the normal. The push on each edge is proportional to the edge's length, which is exactly how real pressure behaves: push per unit of surface.

One subtlety does a lot of quiet work: on a round, undisturbed blob, all those outward pushes cancel each other out — left edge pushes left, right edge pushes right, and nothing moves. The pressure force only becomes visible where the shape is dented, because there the cancellation breaks. That is why the jelly pops back out of a splat so eagerly: the dent is precisely where the unbalanced force lives.

Squash & stretch: the bulge has to go somewhere

Land hard and the jelly flattens — and widens. That is the pressure system defending the enclosed area: push the bottom in and the sides bulge out, because the gas inside "wants" its area back and the skin can only give ground where nothing is pressing. Cartoonists call this squash & stretch and draw it by hand; here nobody drew it. It emerges from area being defended — not perfectly conserved (watch the Area Ratio readout in the game's sidebar: it swings as you play, and holding S deliberately retargets it, which is the next trick).

Deflate is a smaller target, not a squeezing hand

The Squeeze Vent level has a gap a full-size jelly cannot fit through. Holding S doesn't crush the blob with an outside force — it changes what the blob wants to be. The target area drops to 46% of normal, and every spring's rest length shrinks by √0.46 ≈ 0.68, the exact factor that scales an area to 46% if all lengths shrink together. The pressure goes negative — the gas now pulls the skin inward — and the jelly actively deflates itself to ooze through the gap. Release S and it re-inflates. Same machinery, different wish.

Why it jiggles after landing

A landing dumps energy into the springs: they stretch, snap back, overshoot, stretch the other way. Whether a spring system settles instantly or rings like a bell depends on damping — friction inside the spring that bleeds motion into nothing. Jelly's springs carry a damper, but a deliberately weak one (the numbers are in the worked examples): the system is under-damped, so each bounce overshoots and the blob wobbles a few times, trading energy between spring-stretch and motion, before the damping — plus a small global velocity damping applied to every node — drains the wobble away. The jiggle isn't an animation. It's what under-damped springs do, and the game's Material Physics Lab sliders let you prove it: raise the stiffness and the wobble gets faster; the jiggle's character changes because the physics changed, not the artwork.

The math the game runs

Four formulas run the whole blob. Each is stated the way the code states it, in the game's own units: pixels, seconds, and one unit of mass per node (forces below are applied directly as accelerations, because every node's mass is 1).

F = k·(d − rest) + c·v_rel Hooke's law with a damper, per spring — f = (d - rest) * kBase * s + relV * cBase * s in stepPhysics(). d is the spring's current length, rest its rest length (scaled by √squish when deflating), v_rel how fast the two ends are separating along the spring. kBase = 640 × the Skin Spring Stiffness slider; cBase = 14.5; s is the family's weight (1.0 / 0.52 / 0.14). Gravity, for comparison, is 780 px/s² × the Gravity slider.
P = targetArea / currentArea The pressure number on the game's HUD — literally a ratio of areas. This is the one place the "Ideal Gas" label is exactly honest: for a real gas held at fixed temperature, PV = nRT says pressure rises in inverse proportion to volume, so squeezing to 90% of the area really would give P = 1/0.90 ≈ 1.11, and half area would give 2.00. The code computes the ratio directly and shows it to you live.
pressure force = ((target − current) / current) × 260,000, applied as F = pressure × edgeLength × 0.0042 The force law behind the ratio — and here the label and the law part company (see Simplifications). The code never evaluates PV = nRT; it builds a restoring push proportional to the fractional area error and spreads it over each skin edge in proportion to the edge's length, pushing both of the edge's nodes along the outward normal. The current area is floored at 20% of the rest area (4,071 px²) so the division can't explode when the blob is crushed flat.
A = ½ |Σ (xᵢ·yᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ₊₁·yᵢ)| The shoelace formula, in computeCentroidAndArea(): walk the ring, cross-multiply consecutive nodes, sum, halve. That's how the game knows its own area every step — the same number the pressure law and the HUD both read. The blob's centre is the plain average of the 20 nodes, computed in the same loop.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Example 1: one skin spring, stretched 20%

A skin spring's rest length is 11.26 px. Land awkwardly and stretch one to 13.5 px — an extension of 2.25 px. Its pull is 2.25 × 640 × 1.0 ≈ 1,440, against gravity's 780. A single skin spring stretched by a fifth pulls about 1.85× as hard as gravity on each of its nodes. That's why the blob snaps back instead of draping — and why the springs, not the pressure, win the first instant of every bounce.

Example 2: the pressure at a 10% squeeze

Splat the jelly down to 90% of its rest area. The HUD pressure reads 1/0.90 ≈ 1.11. Underneath, the force pressure is (1 − 0.90)/0.90 × 260,000 ≈ 28,900, and each skin edge at its rest length of 11.26 px pushes its two nodes outward with 28,900 × 11.26 × 0.0042 ≈ 1,370 — about 1.75× gravity, per edge, all around the ring. On the round parts of the blob those pushes cancel; at the flat spot where you landed, they don't. The dent is pushed out by the whole ring's worth of disagreement. Squeeze to half area and the readout hits 2.00 and the per-edge push roughly triples — the vent levels are tuned so that fighting the pressure is a losing plan and deflating is the winning one.

Example 3: the anti-pancake spoke

Land flat enough that one pair of opposite nodes is squeezed from 72 px apart to 60 px. The spoke spring's compression is −12 px, so each spoke entry pushes with −12 × 640 × 0.14 ≈ −1,075. And here the code has a quirk worth naming: the build loop adds a spoke for every node i to its opposite i+10 — which means each opposite pair is added twice, once from each end. The pair actually pushes with ≈ 2,150, about 2.8× gravity, prying the pancake back open. Whether the doubling began as an accident or as tuning, it is part of why this jelly refuses to stay flat, and the lesson reports the code as it is, not as it was probably meant to be.

Example 4: the jiggle, measured

Take two neighbouring nodes joined by a skin spring. Their relative wobble has angular frequency ω = √(k/μ) with reduced mass μ = ½: ω = √(640/0.5) ≈ 35.8 rad/s — a period of about 0.18 s, roughly five to six wobbles a second, which matches the jelly's visible shiver. The damper gives a damping ratio of about ζ = 14.5 / (2·√(640·0.5)) ≈ 0.4 — well under the ζ = 1 that would settle without overshoot. So each wobble comes back at a good fraction of the last, a few visible rings before the dampers and the global velocity damping (every node's velocity × 0.992 per frame-step) spend it all. Firmly under-damped — by design, because a critically damped jelly would feel like wet cement.

How it's built — the coding choices

Forces first, motion second, collisions last — ten times a frame

Each frame, the game runs its physics step 10 times on slices of the frame time (itself clamped to at most 0.03 s, so a stuttering tab can't feed the springs one giant, explosive step). Every substep follows the same order: total up all forces into fresh arrays — gravity and the player's roll forces, then pressure, then all 60 spring entries, then the mouse grab if you're holding the jelly — then integrate, then collide. The integration is semi-implicit Euler: velocity is updated from force first, and position is then moved by the new velocity. That ordering looks trivial and isn't — it's the cheap integrator that doesn't steadily pump energy into oscillating systems, which matters enormously when your whole character is an oscillator. Small substeps do the rest: stiff springs (k = 640) punish big steps with phantom energy, so the game takes little ones, 600 a second.

Collision: points against boxes, out the nearest face

Every node is a point (with a 4 px padding) tested against each level block's rectangle. If a point ends a substep inside a box, the code measures the penetration to all four faces and pushes the node out through the nearest one, fixing its velocity to match: landing on top keeps 18% of your downward speed as a small bounce and multiplies sideways speed by 0.86 (friction); bounce pads ignore all that and simply set upward velocity to −670; walls register a contact the jump logic can spend on a wall-jump, and holding Shift against a wall multiplies fall speed by 0.55 per substep — the "sticky grip" is friction applied relentlessly. Moving platforms even hand their sideways velocity to nodes standing on them. Simple, legible, and — because the jelly is 20 independent points — the blob can drape over a corner in a way no single-circle character could.

The drawing is a smoothing trick

The skin you see is not the polygon of nodes. The renderer draws quadratic Bézier curves using each node as a control point and each edge's midpoint as an anchor — a classic trick that rounds every corner for free, so 20 points read as a smooth blob. The eyes are pinned to the centroid and glance in the direction you're steering. And the game ships its own X-ray: the Material Physics Lab's toggles draw the actual spring mesh and the pressure normals (16 px arrows along each edge's outward normal), so the machinery this page describes is something you can switch on and watch.

The telemetry reads the physics' own numbers

The sidebar's Area Ratio, Pressure, and Contacts are not estimates for display — they're the same area, ratio, and contact count the physics step just used. The game shows its receipts as it goes, which is why the worked examples above could be checked against the HUD instead of trusted.

Levels are data

All five levels — Squish Meadow through the Physics Playground — are plain data: spawn point, goal, three stars, and a list of blocks with a kind (solid, bounce, mover). One physics engine, five arrangements of furniture. Adding a level is typing, not coding — the same separation of world-description from world-simulation that lets a script verify the physics without the page.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the header's "Ideal Gas Pressure (PV = nRT)" is a label, not the law — the code never tracks n, R, or T, and its pressure force is proportional to the fractional area error, a very stiff area-restoring push wearing the gas law's name. Only the HUD ratio (target ÷ current area) behaves the way Boyle's law actually would. "Area conservation" is a tendency, not a conservation law: the area swings constantly and the S key retargets it to 46% on purpose. Every node has mass exactly 1 and forces are applied as accelerations, so the numbers are the game's own units throughout. The nodes are points — collision pads them by 4 px, but the skin has no thickness, nodes can cross each other, and the ring can locally invert (the weak spoke springs only resist it; nothing forbids it). Restitution and friction are per-substep multipliers chosen for feel, not material properties. The world is 2D, gravity is a uniform 780 px/s² everywhere, and each opposite pair of nodes is, as noted above, joined by two spoke-spring entries rather than one.
