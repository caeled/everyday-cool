SLAM Robot — the lesson

A small robot wakes up in a building it has never seen, with no map, no GPS, and wheel sensors that lie a little more every metre it travels. All it has is a spinning lidar that reports how far the walls are — noisily — in every direction at once. From that alone it must answer two questions that depend on each other: where am I? and what does this place look like? That circular problem is SLAM — simultaneous localization and mapping — and this exhibit builds most of it for real: the rays, the noise, the drifting odometry, the Bayesian map, the explorer that hunts the map's unfinished edges. This page is the other half: the engineering, the mathematics the robot runs, and the audit that measured all of it — including the correction layer where the exhibit quietly cheats, printed just as plainly as its genuine receipts.

Verdict: The sensing, mapping and navigation are real — exact ray casting, honest noise (σ 3.99 measured vs 4.0 claimed), genuine log-odds fusion — but 'ICP' is correlative search rather than ICP, the pose is pulled toward ground truth (16% a call, 72% on a snap), loop closure is a cooldown counter, and 'Map Complete 100%' fired at 98.39%.

The SLAM Robot exhibit was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the shipped code and driving it headlessly.

The engineering

The chicken-and-egg problem at the heart of SLAM

To place a lidar reading on a map, you must know where the robot was standing when it took the reading. To know where the robot is standing, you compare its readings against the map. Neither answer exists first — so a SLAM system builds both at once, incrementally: dead-reckon a short distance, use the new scan to correct the drift and extend the map, repeat. Every part of the exhibit exists to serve that loop. The world is a grid of 160 × 104 cells at 0.15 m — 24.0 × 15.6 m of building — and the robot's belief about that world is a second grid, built scan by scan, which the Fog view shows you honestly: the robot plans and drives on the belief, not on the truth you can see in X-Ray.

Lidar: distance by ray, 120 times a sweep

The sensor fires 120 rays over the full 360°, each out to a maximum range of 6.0 m (40 cells), and reports the distance to the first occupied cell along its line. The exhibit casts those rays with a DDA — digital differential analyser — walk through the actual drawn grid, static and dynamic obstacles alike, so a wall you paint is a wall the lidar genuinely hits. Real sensors lie, so the exhibit's do too: every hit range carries additive Gaussian noise (σ = 4.0 cm by default), while a ray that hits nothing honestly reports max range. At 120 rays and one scan per step at 60 steps a second, the telemetry's 7,200 rays/sec is the page's own arithmetic, and it checks out.

Odometry: dead reckoning, and why it always drifts

Between scans, the robot tracks itself the way sailors once did: integrate the motion the wheels report. The exhibit integrates the robot's actual post-collision displacement — the collision resolver's output, not the commanded motion — and then corrupts it with the error terms real wheels suffer: a systematic heading bias plus stochastic noise, scaled by the drift dial. The result is the pale "ghost" trailing away from the true robot on screen, and it is the honest centre of the whole demonstration: dead reckoning is exact when the errors are zero and hopeless when they are not, and no amount of careful integration fixes a bias — only an outside reference (the scan, matched against the map) can pull the estimate back.

Occupancy grids: a map that keeps its doubts in numbers

The belief map never says "wall" or "no wall." Each cell carries a log-odds score — the logarithm of the odds that the cell is occupied — which every scan nudges: up where a ray ends, down along the path the ray travelled freely. Log-odds is the engineer's way of keeping Bayesian evidence additive: independent observations simply sum, the score is clamped to [−4.6, +5.4] so no cell becomes unchangeably certain, and a cell no ray has ever touched stays at exactly zero — unknown, a third state no binary map can express. The explorer lives off that third state: a frontier is a known-free cell touching an unknown one, and the map's frontiers are, literally, the edge of what the robot knows.

Getting around: frontiers, A*, and Pure Pursuit

Exploration is a real pipeline. Frontier cells are clustered by breadth-first search, filtered (clusters under 3 cells are ignored), and scored by size, distance, and heading continuity; the winner becomes a goal for A* — a genuine binary-heap, 8-connected search over a costmap inflated around believed walls (hard radius 2.4 cells by default), with soft costs, unknown-cell penalties, and diagonal corner-cut prevention. A Pure Pursuit controller then steers the differential-drive robot along the path — curvature 2y/L² toward a lookahead point, with pivot behaviour when the heading error is large — through a motion model with acceleration limits and a circle-versus-cell collision resolver. Nothing in that chain is decorative, and the audit drove it into walls and corners to prove the last link.

The math the game runs

The log-odds update

l(cell) ← clamp( l(cell) + Δl, −4.6, +5.4 );   Δl = +locc at the endpoint, a proportional free increment along the ray The update is projected from the estimated pose using the noisy measured ranges — the map is fused from what the robot believes it saw, standing where it believes it stands, not painted from ground truth. One noise-free scan from the office start, taken at the true pose, changed 1,561 cells: 94 went occupied (above +0.35), 1,471 went free (below −0.35), and the rest stayed unknown. Two small bends, named in the honest box: the occupied cell is placed 0.12 cells (1.8 cm) beyond the measured range, and the robot's own footprint is forcibly held at or below −1.2 log-odds every scan.

The odometry error model

estimate ← integrate(actual displacement) + systematic heading bias + stochastic terms, scaled by the drift dial With drift set to 0 the integration is exact — zero error over a 19.764-cell (2.96 m) straight drive. At drift 1.0 with scan-matching off, the same drive ended 4.567 cells (68.5 cm) off, with a heading error of +0.471 rad (27.0°); at drift 3.5 the error reached 17.80 cells (2.67 m). The dominant term is a systematic heading bias of about 0.018–0.022 rad per cell travelled, its sign randomised per preset — which is why the ghost doesn't just jitter, it veers. The "×" on the dial has no calibrated physical baseline; it is a game dial, not a drift rate.

The scan matcher — and what ICP actually is

score(pose) = Σ likelihood-field value at each scan endpoint;   search ±0.55 then ±0.18 cells, ±0.035 then ±0.012 rad This is correlative scan-to-map matching: try a small grid of candidate poses, coarse then fine, score each by how well the scan's endpoints land on likely-occupied cells of a distance-blurred map, keep the best. Real iterative closest point is a different algorithm — it pairs each scan point with its closest map point and solves for the rigid transform that minimises the pairing error, iteratively. The exhibit's matcher has no correspondences and no closest-point solve. It works, within limits the audit measured: a small imposed error of 0.583 cells was corrected to 0.130 cells (heading 0.030 → 0.006 rad), but a 3-cell (45 cm) displacement was not corrected at all — outside its capture basin, the search simply never finds home.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit instrumented the shipped page in memory (the file on disk was not changed) to expose its state and functions, then tested DDA rays against known geometry, collected thousands of noisy range samples, displaced the estimated pose by known amounts, drove the robot into a wall and a corner, ran autonomous exploration in all six presets, and drove the DOM controls, canvas tools, pan/zoom, and keyboard drive. Every number below is measured.

Receipt 1: the rays are exact, and the noise is honest

With noise set to zero, every measured range equalled the DDA's true range. Against known geometry in the Blank arena, the first-occupied-cell boundaries came back exact in every direction the audit probed:

Then the noise: over 300 scans and 11,700 hit ranges at the office start, the mean error was −0.11 cm and the standard deviation 3.99 cm against the claimed σ = 4.0 cm, with extremes of −15.5 / +14.6 cm. That is a Gaussian doing exactly what the label promises. One bend at the extremes: measured distances are clamped to [0.4 cells, max range], so at σ = 24 cm the distribution is truncated rather than purely Gaussian (one-scan RMS ≈ 22.5 cm). No-hit rays correctly stay at max range — in the Blank arena from (24, 52), only 39 of 120 rays hit anything within 6 m, and the rest said so.

Receipt 2: the navigation chain is real, and the robot does not go through walls

Frontier detection, the inflated-costmap A*, and Pure Pursuit are working implementations, not labels (see The engineering). The audit's physical test was blunt: drive at full command into a wall for five seconds, then diagonally into a corner.

One timing bend on the "reroutes live" claim: painting a wall changes the true grid at once, but the planner's costmap is built from the belief grid — a new wall affects routes only after the lidar observes it and the next replan cycle runs (0.38 s timer; a brush stroke shortens it to 0.05 s). Live on a sub-second, after-observation basis — which is, to be fair, the only basis a real robot has.

Receipt 3 — the false layer: the pose is pulled toward ground truth

After the correlative search runs, the shipped code does something no real SLAM system can do: if the match score exceeds 0.38 (or on a forced snap), it moves the estimated pose directly toward state.bot — the true robot pose, ground truth the robot is supposed to be estimating — by 16% per normal call, and by 72% on a forced snap. The audit measured what that buys: a forced snap with a 5.83-cell imposed error ended at 0.409 cells and counted a loop closure — a recovery far outside what the correlative search, which cannot correct even a 3-cell displacement, could have supplied. With matching enabled on a 2.96 m drift-1.0 drive, the estimated-pose error was a handsome 0.428 cells (6.4 cm) — good-looking, and not achievable from the matcher alone as implemented. In the High-Noise & Drift preset (which the audit verified sets σ = 13.5 cm and drift = 2.4×), a 60 s run held SLAM error to 7.0 cm while raw odometry error in the same run was 5.18 m; across the six 60 s runs, odometry error grew to 15–52 m — the ghost is unbounded and leaves the world — while SLAM error stayed at 6–29 cm. Those SLAM numbers are real measurements of the shipped system; they are measurements of a system with a truth leak, and cannot be credited to scan matching. Toggle the matcher off and the controlled trial shows the difference honestly: a 0.583-cell error stayed exactly where it was put.

Receipt 4 — the false counter: "loop closure" closes no loops

Real loop closure is place recognition: the robot notices "I have been here before," adds a constraint between its current pose and a past one in a pose graph, and re-optimises the whole trajectory. None of that machinery exists here. The counter increments when accumulated correction plus odometry error pass thresholds after a 4.5 s cooldown — or immediately on any successful forced snap. Receipt 5 — the false banner: "Map Complete 100%" fired at 98.39%

The coverage metric itself is a bend worth understanding first: "Map Explored" counts reachable cells whose log-odds have fallen below the free threshold, divided by a reachable-cell total computed from the true static grid. Ground truth as an evaluation denominator is reasonable for a score — but "explored" really means "confidently observed free"; observed walls and weakly observed cells do not count toward it. Against even that metric, the completion banner overstates: in the Blank arena the robot was declared "Map Complete!" with 0 frontiers while coverage read 98.39% — the banner's "100% of reachable space discovered" overstates the page's own number by 1.61 points. The completion rule fires when no frontier clusters survive the costmap and frontier filters (or at ≥ 98.8%, or as a fallback above 94% when the remaining frontiers are unreachable). "No reachable frontier left" is a sensible stopping condition; it is not 100%, and the banner should not say it is.

Receipt 6: the controls all do real work

No wholly dead control was found. All six presets load with distinct starts and reachable-cell totals (Office 14,042; Warehouse 14,521; Maze 12,668; Dynamic 15,007; Noisy 14,739; Blank 15,571). Every slider — speed, brush, ray count (→ 180), range (→ 9.0 m), noise (→ 12.5 cm), drift (→ 2.0×), log-odds gain (→ +1.40), A* inflation (→ 3.4 cells) — updated both its label and the internal state. "Clear Belief Map" took the log-odds absolute sum from 1033.7 (1,561 nonzero cells) to exactly 0 and re-anchored the estimated and odometry poses to the robot; "Reset Ghost Drift" re-anchored the ghost to the true pose exactly. Camera pan, zoom (6.396 → 7.291), Fit / Follow, drawer, Cinema, and the keyboard (Space, B, X, H, R, and a 0.5 s W-hold that switched to Manual and drove the robot from x = 24 to x = 27.36 at y = 52) all work.

How it's built — the coding choices

Two grids, kept honestly apart — mostly

The architecture's virtue is the separation the whole field depends on: a true grid the lidar casts against, and a belief grid fused from noisy scans at the estimated pose, which is the only map the planner ever sees. Painting and erasing edit the truth; "Clear Belief Map" zeroes the belief; the robot re-learns what it can no longer assume. The separation leaks in exactly the places the receipts name — the truth pull in the pose estimate, the truth-based coverage denominator, and one drawing shortcut: the lidar visibility polygon and ray beams in Fog view are drawn from the scan's true hit coordinates, while only the small hit sparks use the noisy measured endpoints at the estimated pose. In Fog view, the fan shows exact wall geometry the sensor model never measured.

An instantaneous scan wearing a spinning turret

The sensor model is a full 360° scan taken in a single physics step; the rotating turret is visual dressing. That is a fair simplification for a grid world at this scale — but it is why the rays/sec counter behaves as it does at 4× speed, where the loop casts a complete scan per physics substep and the count rises accordingly. Real scanning lidars smear a sweep across the robot's motion; this one freezes the world politely, 120 rays at a time.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The pose correction is not sensor-only: beyond the correlative matcher, the estimate is pulled toward the true pose by 16% per strong-match call and 72% on a forced snap. No real SLAM system has access to that term. Every flattering SLAM-error number on the page (6–29 cm across the 60 s runs, against 15–52 m of raw odometry error) is partly this term's work.

"ICP" is a mislabel: the matcher is coarse-to-fine correlative scan-to-map search over a blurred likelihood field — no point correspondences, no iterative closest-point solve — and its capture basin is small: no correction at all for a 3-cell (45 cm) displacement.

"Loop closure" is a counter, not a detection: no place recognition, no pose graph, no revisit test — accumulated correction plus odometry error past a threshold, gated by a 4.5 s cooldown, or any forced snap. It fires at the start pose with zero travel and at the cooldown cadence (12–14 per 60 s) in every preset.

"Map Complete — 100%" overstates the page's own metric: it fired at 98.39% coverage in the Blank arena. The rule is frontier exhaustion (or ≥ 98.8%, or a > 94% fallback when remaining frontiers are unreachable) — a reasonable stop, mislabelled as completeness.

Coverage means "confidently observed free": the denominator is computed from the true static grid, and observed walls do not count as explored. As a score it is defensible; as a plain-language claim about discovery it bends.

The map's small bends: occupied endpoints are written 0.12 cells (1.8 cm) beyond the measured range; the robot's footprint is forcibly held free (≤ −1.2 log-odds) every scan; and dynamic-obstacle hits enter the same persistent grid with no temporal decay, so moving obstacles can leave ghost walls until re-observed as free. The dynamic obstacles themselves are genuinely simulated, raycast, and avoided — and they politely pause rather than crush the robot, while the robot's collision state keeps "pushing" at commanded speed even when the resolver has clamped its position.

Telemetry scope: "Pose (x, y)" and heading display the estimated pose; the error readout is computed against ground truth — available to a simulator as a score, never to a real robot. The drift dial's "×" is uncalibrated, and its systematic heading bias (0.018–0.022 rad per cell) is very large by real-wheel standards.

Small print: the snap button's success banner appears even when nothing snapped (empty map); in Split view, a pointer press within 16 px of the divider is captured as a divider drag even with the Wall tool armed, so a paint click near the divider drags instead of painting; and the noise model's clamping ([0.4 cells, max range]) truncates the Gaussian at extreme σ settings.
