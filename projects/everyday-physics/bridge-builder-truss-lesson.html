Bridge Builder — the lesson

Bridge Builder hands you a gorge, a budget, and three materials, then drives a vehicle across whatever you built and shows you — in colour, member by member — exactly where your bridge is being pulled apart and where it is being crushed. This page is the other half of it: why triangles carry the load, how to read the colours as instructions, what the built-in examples actually cost, and how the simulation underneath works. If a span keeps defeating you, this is the door in.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to the collection by Steven Powell; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool to explain it.

The physics

Why triangles: the shape that cannot fold

Pin four bars into a square and push it sideways: it folds into a leaning parallelogram. Builders call that racking, and nothing in the square's shape resists it — the corners are hinges, and the bars never have to change length for the shape to collapse. Now pin three bars into a triangle and push. The triangle cannot change shape at all unless one of its bars gets longer or shorter, because three side lengths fix all three angles completely. That is the entire secret of the truss: a structure made of triangles can only fail by a member stretching or crushing — things materials are good at resisting — instead of by folding, which costs the structure nothing to do. Every diagonal you add to a rectangle is you converting two of its corners into triangles. Everyday Engineering's frames bench teaches this same fact with the training wheels labelled; this game is where it gets a truck.

Tension and compression: pulled versus pushed

Every member in a truss works one of two ways. In tension it is being pulled at both ends, like a rope in a tug of war; the member's whole job is to not stretch apart. In compression it is being pushed at both ends, like a column holding up a roof — and pushed members have a second way to fail that pulled ones don't: they can buckle, bowing sideways suddenly instead of shortening gracefully. That is why the game's materials are all rated slightly weaker in compression than in tension (the table below), and why real bridges make their compression members short and stocky and let long, skinny members — cables — work only in tension, where skinny is fine. A cable under push doesn't buckle dramatically; it just goes slack and stops helping, and the game models exactly that.

Load paths: every pound has to walk home

The vehicle's weight lands on the deck, and from there it must travel — member to member, joint to joint — until it reaches the amber anchor pins on the cliffs, the only places the world agrees to hold your bridge up. Engineers call that route the load path, and the rule is absolute: at every joint along the way, the forces must balance. If a joint has a member pulling it down and nothing pulling it up, the joint moves — that is what a collapse is, one unbalanced joint at a time. When you look at your bridge, trace the path with your eye: deck joint → diagonal → top chord → diagonal → bank. Any joint where the path dead-ends, or where one lone member carries what two could share, is where the colour will go bad first.

Why the middle suffers — and why the ends work hard too

Two different jobs peak in two different places. The bending of the whole bridge — the sag — is worst at mid-span, farthest from both supports, so the long top and bottom chords that resist bending carry their biggest forces near the middle. The shear — the deck trying to slide down past the banks — is worst right next to the supports, which is why the diagonals near the cliffs earn their keep. And the longest unsupported stretch of deck is where the vehicle's wheels press on the fewest joints at once. Practical reading: a bridge that fails near a bank wants more diagonal help near the bank; a bridge that fails in the middle wants a deeper truss, a stronger chord, or — the Viaduct level's whole point — a support in the middle that turns one long span into two short ones.

The math the game runs

The materials, exactly as coded

The game's three materials live in one table in the code (the MATS object), and everything the game believes about them is in these numbers. The limits are strains — how far a member may stretch or shorten, as a fraction of its own length — before the game calls it broken.

Road deck$1200.925.2% stretch5.0% shortening1.0

Read the cable's row carefully: it has the highest tension limit and the lowest price, which makes it the best pure pulling material in the game — and it contributes nothing at all when pushed. (The code gives it a nominal compression limit of 999, but a separate rule — tension-only members go slack the moment they shorten — means that number is never used.)

cost of one member = round( length ÷ 80 × base cost ) You pay by length, in 80-pixel units. An 80 px steel member is $160; the standard Warren diagonal (40 across, 80 up — 89.4 px long) is round(89.4 ÷ 80 × 160) = $179; an 80 px cable is $80, and cable works out to roughly a dollar a pixel. Length is money: two short members are not just stronger than one long one, they are priced by the same ruler.
strain = (current length − rest length) ÷ rest length · stress % = |strain| ÷ limit × 100 This is what the game calls stress. Each step, the solver measures how far each member sits from the length it was built at; the sign of the strain says tension (positive, stretched) or compression (negative, shortened), and the size of it, divided by that material's limit from the table, is the percentage in the telemetry panel. At 100% the member breaks — there is no partial damage and no fatigue: a member can flash red on one crossing and be whole on the next if the load moves off it. "Peak Stress" is simply the worst percentage seen so far in the test.

Joint equilibrium, in one honest miniature

Real truss analysis starts with the rule the load path obeys: at any joint, the forces sum to zero — everything pulling one way is exactly answered by everything pulling the other, or the joint accelerates. Here is that rule worked once, with the game's own geometry. In the built-in Warren truss, each diagonal runs 40 px across and 80 px up, so it leans at arctan(80 ÷ 40) = 63.4° from horizontal. Imagine a deck joint where a downward load of 20 units arrives, held by two such diagonals rising symmetrically to the left and right, working in tension:

2 × T × sin(63.4°) = 20 → T = 20 ÷ (2 × 0.894) ≈ 11.2 units in each diagonal Vertically, each diagonal lifts with T × sin(63.4°) ≈ 10 units, and the pair answers the 20-unit load exactly. Horizontally, each pulls sideways with T × cos(63.4°) ≈ 5.0 units — equal, opposite, cancelled. Sum of forces: zero, both ways. That is a joint in balance, and it is also why the diagonals can be so much weaker than the load feels: at a steep 63.4°, most of each member's pull points the useful way. Flatten the truss — make the apex lower — and sin(θ) shrinks, so the same load demands bigger member forces. Depth is cheap strength: a taller truss asks less of every member in it. (This is the method of joints, the first tool of truss analysis, at pocket size.)

The colour code, decoded from the code

During a test, each member's colour is computed from its signed strain that step:

  • Green — stress under 12%, whichever way it is loaded. The member is loafing.
  • Cyan shading toward deep electric blue — tension. The bluer it gets, the closer its stretch is to the material's tension limit.
  • Amber shading toward bright red — compression. The redder it gets, the closer its shortening is to the compression limit.

So the heatmap is not decoration; it is a work order. Blue tells you this member is being pulled toward its limit — answer it with cable, the tension specialist, or a second path to share the pull. Red tells you this member is being crushed toward its limit — answer it with steel, a shorter member, or a diagonal that turns the crush into a pull somewhere else. The sidebar's gradient strip (tension on the left, compression on the right) is a legend for the two families; the members themselves use the family colour and deepen it with danger. In edit mode the colours just name the materials — grey road, sky-blue steel, purple cable — the heat only exists while the test is running.

Worked examples — the game's own designs, costed

The ✨ Example Truss button builds a Warren truss: a road deck in 80 px segments, and over each segment a steel apex 80 px above its midpoint, joined to both deck joints, with neighbouring apexes linked by a top chord. Here is what that design — and each level's extras — actually cost under the game's pricing, and what each teaches.

Example 1: Creek Crossing (240 px span, $2,400 budget)

Three deck segments ($120 × 3 = $360), six Warren diagonals ($179 × 6 = $1,074), two top-chord members ($160 × 2 = $320): total $1,754, leaving $646 of headroom. This is the template to internalise. The first triangles go immediately: one apex over every deck segment, no bare deck span longer than 80 px, and every deck joint held from above by two diagonals. The $646 of slack is your upgrade fund for the heavier vehicles — the Tank load (2.1×) presses each wheel with 2.1 times the Van's weight, and the members that flash first under it are where the fund should go, not spread evenly over the whole bridge.

Example 2: Suspension Canyon (480 px span, $4,800 budget) — the example that busts its budget

The same Warren base costs $3,668 here (six segments), and then the level adds six stay cables from the two tower-top anchors, 160 px above the deck, down to deck joints at 80, 160, and 240 px out from each tower. By the cost formula those cables are $179, $226, and $288 apiece — the long centre stays are the priciest members on the bridge — for $1,386 of cable. Total: $5,054 — $254 over the $4,800 budget. The built-in example will carry the vehicle, but the game will only call it "PASSED (OVER BUDGET)", not certified. The lesson is worth the embarrassment: stay cables are superb value per unit of support — tension-only, the strongest pulling limit in the game (8.2%), a dollar a pixel — but length is money, and the elegant fan of six is a starting sketch, not a certified answer. Trimming a design like this is the real level: shorten the longest stays by landing them on nearer deck joints, and watch which members the heatmap says were actually working before you cut the ones that weren't.

Example 3: Viaduct Pier (560 px span, $5,600 budget) — buy a shorter span

The longest gorge in the game comes with one gift: a pier anchor mid-river at (480, 440), 120 px below deck level. The example's Warren base costs $4,306 (seven segments), plus two steel struts from the pier top up to the deck joints at 440 and 520 — each 126.5 px, $253 by the formula — for $4,812 total, $788 under budget. Those two struts are the cheapest strength on this page: they turn a 560 px span into two spans of about 280 px, and since mid-span suffering grows with the span, halving the span is worth far more than $506 of thicker members spread along the whole bridge. Note they are steel, not cable: a pier strut works in compression, pushing the deck up — the one job cable refuses to do.

The cheapest saves, in order

  1. A member flashes blue (tension): add a cable alongside it. $80 per 80 px, the highest tension limit in the game, and it weighs almost nothing (mass factor 0.35). Never spend this on a member that flashes red — the cable will simply go slack and watch it fail.
  2. A road member flashes either colour: rebuild it in steel. For an 80 px member that is +$40 (from $120 to $160), and it buys a bigger limit both ways — 5.2% → 6.8% in tension, 5.0% → 6.4% in compression — plus the stiffest response in the game.
  3. A member flashes red (compression): shorten it. Put a joint in its middle and brace from there. Shorter members sag less under the solver, cost less by the length formula, and in the real world resist buckling better too.
  4. The middle of the bridge flashes: deepen the truss. Raise the apexes. The joint miniature above is the reason — steeper diagonals answer the same load with less force. It costs longer diagonals; it usually saves more than it spends.
  5. Nothing works: look for the missing support, not a stronger member. An anchor below the deck (Creek and Gorge both pin anchors 80 px under each bank) or the Viaduct's pier changes the span itself. Geometry beats metallurgy at this game's prices.

How it's built — the coding choices

Verlet nodes and distance constraints, not a statics solver

The game never solves the truss on paper. It builds the bridge out of point masses (the nodes) joined by members that behave as stiff distance-keepers, then simulates: each step, every free node coasts under gravity (420 px/s², in the code's constant G) using Verlet integration — its velocity is just the gap between where it is and where it was, damped by a factor of 0.994 — and then the code runs 14 relaxation passes over all the members, four simulation substeps per screen frame. In each pass, a member that is longer or shorter than its built length pushes its two end nodes back toward the right distance, by a fraction set by the material's stiffness (steel corrects 0.98 of the error per pass — nearly rigid; cable 0.85), split between the nodes in inverse proportion to their mass. Amber anchor nodes are fixed: their share of every correction is zero, which is how "the cliff holds it" is expressed in arithmetic. Run that loop fast enough and the bridge settles into the shape a statics textbook would have computed — the equilibrium is found by the simulation instead of being solved for, which is why you can watch the bridge sag, strain, and fail as a process rather than read a verdict.

Where the numbers you see come from

On the last relaxation pass of each step — when the members are as settled as that step gets — the code measures each member's strain and converts it to the stress percentage from the formula above, breaking the member on the spot if it reaches 100%. The vehicle is deliberately simple: it doesn't collide with the bridge as a body. Instead, each frame it asks "what road is under my two wheels?" — and only road members answer; a steel member laid at deck level is not a driving surface in this game, which is why the deck must be road, unbroken, bank to bank. Each wheel then presses its share (9.5 × the load multiplier: 1.0 for the Van, 1.5 for the Truck, 2.1 for the Tank) down onto the two nodes of the road member beneath it, weighted by where along the member the wheel sits. Heavier nodes — nodes with more or longer members attached — shrug off a smaller share of the push, which is the game's quiet way of letting mass spread load.

The blueprint is cloned before it is broken

One builder's choice worth stealing: when you press Test Bridge, the code deep-copies your design into a separate simulation state (simNodes / simBeams) and destroys that. Your blueprint survives every collapse untouched, so the loop of the game — fail, read the colours, fix one thing, retest — costs you nothing but the fix. The example bridges are likewise generated by one parameterised builder (the same Warren routine, given a span, a segment width, and a height), with each level's personality added as extra members on top: the Suspension level fans its cables, the Viaduct adds its pier struts. Four levels, one truss generator, small honest differences.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the world is a 2D side view; a real bridge also twists and sways sideways. Joints are perfect pins — members carry only pull and push along their own length, never bending, and no joint has strength of its own to fail. The "stress" figure is normalised strain, not engineering stress: it is never divided by a member's cross-section, and the materials do not yield, creep, or accumulate damage — a member is whole until the step its strain crosses the limit, then it is gone. Compression failure uses a slightly smaller strain limit as a nod to buckling, but buckling itself — the sideways bow, and its strong dependence on member length — is not simulated; length enters a member's fate only through cost, mass, and geometry. The vehicle is two weighted wheels and a sampling rule, not a rigid body on suspension; it loads only road members, and only while both wheels find deck beneath them. There is no wind, no water current, no earthquake, no second vehicle, and the gorge's river is scenery. Gravity is a flat 420 px/s² in the game's own units throughout.
