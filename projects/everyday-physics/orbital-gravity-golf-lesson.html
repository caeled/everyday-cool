Gravity Golf — the lesson

Gravity Golf is a slingshot game: you drag back, let go, and a small probe flies a course laid out among planets, binary stars, a black hole or two, repulsor cores, and wormholes — steered by nothing after the launch but gravity itself. Sink the probe into the green beacon in as few strokes as par allows. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, the math underneath, some shots flown through the game's own physics as worked examples, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to the collection by Steven Powell; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool, from reading the game's code.

The physics

Every mass pulls, all the time

In Docking there is one planet and one pull. Here there can be three bodies on a course, and the probe feels all of them at once: each body tugs it toward itself, the tugs add up as vectors, and the sum is the only steering the probe gets. That is the whole "n-body" idea in plain words — not one gravity, but a committee of gravities, voting every instant. A shot threaded between two planets (Hole 2, Kepler Bend) is an S-curve because the committee's majority changes as you cross the course: the first planet bends you one way, the second catches you and bends you back. There is no path through such a field that a ruler can draw; the curve is negotiated continuously, which is why the game shows you a computed preview instead of asking you to guess.

Why the path curves — and keeps curving

Gravity never pushes the probe along its motion; it pulls sideways to it, toward each body, and a sideways pull bends a path. Fall toward a planet and you speed up; climb away and you slow down; pass at just the right distance and speed and the bending closes into an orbit — falling around the planet instead of into it. Every arc on the course is some mixture of those three: a plunge, a climb, or a near-orbit that never quite closes before the next body takes a vote.

The slingshot, honestly: a fixed planet bends you, a moving one pays you

Here is the part the game's name promises, stated the way the physics actually keeps its books. Swing past a stationary planet and you leave with the same speed you arrived with — faster at the bottom of the swing, slower again as you climb out, net zero. What changes is your direction, and it can change enormously: the worked example below swung a probe through about 87° around Hole 1's planet. A fixed planet is a steering wheel, not an engine.

A moving body is an engine. In the moving star's own frame the same rule holds — speed in, speed out — but the star's motion is added on both sides of the encounter, so back in the course's frame you can leave with up to twice the star's speed more than you brought, if you pass behind it (crossing its path in the direction it's travelling), or give up the same amount passing in front. That is the real gravity assist that swung Voyager out of the solar system, and the game has it wherever a body moves: the Binary Waltz stars travel at 82.8 units/s, so a well-flown pass can pay out up to ~166 units/s; the moon on Bank Shot Nebula moves at 155 units/s, worth up to ~310. Timing a launch so the star is there, moving the right way, when your probe arrives — that is the shot those holes are actually asking for.

Chaos is why it's hard

Gravity Golf is difficult for a reason no amount of practice removes: near a strong body, the outcome is sensitive — genuinely, mathematically sensitive — to the launch. The bending a probe gets depends on how close it passes, and close passes magnify small differences the way a pencil balanced on its point magnifies a breath. The worked example below puts a number on it: at Hole 3's black hole, two launches differing by 4 units/s in one component — about one pixel of mouse drag — are the difference between a clean 180° hairpin and a crash. This isn't the game being unfair. It's what n-body gravity is: no formula hands you the path, only step-by-step simulation does, and the simulation amplifies whatever it is fed. The preview exists because the honest answer to "where will this shot go?" is "fly it and see" — so the game flies it, in miniature, before you commit.

The special bodies, as they actually are in the code

Black holes are not a separate physics. A black hole in this game is an ordinary body with an extraordinary number in the mass column: 29,500,000 on Hole 3, against 17,500,000 for Hole 1's planet, packed into a smaller disc (radius 34 instead of 44). Same formula, same rules — just a well so deep that at 50 units from its centre the pull is 7,973 units/s², about 68 times what you feel at Hole 1's tee. There is no event horizon and no relativity: you "crash" when you touch its drawn surface, exactly as with a planet.

Repulsor cores are a minus sign. A repulsor's mass is negative (−13,500,000 on Anti-Gravity Alley), and the same formula that pulls you toward a planet therefore pushes you away from a repulsor, with the same fall-off. Anti-gravity here is not a new force — it's gravity with the sign flipped, which is why a repulsor "cushion" feels like a planet's pull in mirror image.

Wormholes are not gravity at all. A wormhole is a pair of gates: if the probe's centre comes within the gate's radius (22 units on Wormhole Express), the probe is simply moved to the other gate — position teleported, velocity untouched — and a half-second cooldown stops it bouncing straight back. Your speed and direction survive the jump; only your address changes.

The math the game runs

One formula runs the whole course. Each body's pull is computed and the pulls are added:

a = Σ  G·m / (r² + 1200),   aimed at each body in turn The textbook form is a = G·m/r². The code folds G into the mass number (so G = 1 and a body's "mass" is really G·m — the slider labelled Gravity Constant rescales every mass from 0.5× to 1.6×, which is the same thing). The +1200 is a softening cushion: pure 1/r² rockets to infinity as r → 0, which no game — and no integrator — survives. With the cushion, the pull peaks gently instead: at Hole 1's surface (r = 44) it is 5,580 units/s² rather than the pure formula's 9,039, and the cushion's reach is √1200 ≈ 35 units — inside that distance, gravity here is deliberately gentler than Newton's.

At Hole 1's tee, 385 units from the planet's centre, the formula gives 17,500,000 / (385² + 1200) = 117 units/s² — the pull your probe feels the instant it leaves your hand. The one speed that would circle that planet at that distance is v = √(a·r) = √(117 × 385) ≈ 212 units/s, and escape speed is √2 times that, ≈ 300 units/s. A full-power launch is 674 units/s — more than double escape — which is why a full-blooded drive on Hole 1 doesn't bend home; it leaves, and the course resets you from deep space. Golf, here, is mostly restraint.

The preview is numerical integration — and it needs a good integrator

Because no formula predicts an n-body path, the dotted preview is the game flying your shot in advance: start with the launch velocity, compute the acceleration, step a fiftieth of a second, repeat — 110 steps for the default 2.2-second preview, 225 if you slide it to 4.5 s. The stepping method is RK4, the four-trial-slopes method from Docking's lesson, and it matters more here than there: a sloppy integrator doesn't just wobble, it lies — it leaks energy, so its preview shows orbits holding that the real flight would decay, and hairpins succeeding that the real flight turns into crashes. The preview and the flight call the very same stepping code, so the preview is the actual physics rehearsed — with one honest caveat, in Simplifications below: the preview steps at 0.02 s and the flight at about 0.0028 s, so on the wildest passes the rehearsal and the performance can still part company slightly. Chaos amplifies even that.

Energy: the game's quiet bookkeeper

Around fixed bodies, one quantity is conserved: the probe's total energy, kinetic plus potential, E = ½v² − Σ G·m/r. It is the reason for the slingshot's honesty above — falling into a well converts potential to speed, climbing out converts it back, and the books must balance when you leave, so a fixed planet can change your direction but never your departing speed. A moving body breaks the bookkeeping on purpose: its well is itself moving, energy flows between the star's motion and yours, and that flow is the gravity assist. When you gain 100 units/s off the Binary Waltz, the star lost it — the amounts are real, even if the game doesn't show the star's side of the ledger.

Worked examples — shots flown through the game's own physics

These were run with the game's acceleration and RK4 code lifted out of the shipped file and flown by a script, at the flight's own step size. Receipts, not illustrations.

Example 1: the flyby that proves the rule (Hole 1's planet)

A probe launched at 400 units/s, passing the planet with a closest approach of 55 units:

Same speed, new direction: a fixed planet is a steering wheel. (Flown in the raw physics, this shot exits the course and resets — on the real Hole 1, the wall and the beacon are also in play. The physics numbers are the game's own.)

Example 2: one pixel between the hairpin and the funeral (Hole 3)

From Hole 3's tee (165, 165), two launches at the black hole, identical except for 4 units/s of vertical velocity — and one pixel of drag is 3.85 units/s, so this is a difference smaller than your mouse can reliably make:

Launch velocityClosest approachOutcome
(365, 20) units/s41.4 unitsClean hairpin — whipped around and left at 113° from its arrival line
(365, 24) units/s40.2 unitsCrash — the surface is at 40.8 units, centre distance

The surviving shot cleared the surface by 0.6 units — six-tenths of a pixel. Nothing about the two launches looks different on the tee. That is the chaos paragraph above with its sleeves rolled up, and it's why the game's own advice — nudge, watch the preview, nudge again — is the only strategy the physics respects.

Example 3: what the preview costs, and buys

The default preview looks 2.2 s ahead in steps of 0.02 s: 110 RK4 steps, each step pricing every body four times — 440 body-pulls per frame on Hole 1, 1,320 on the three-body Lagrange Slalom. Sliding the preview to its 4.5 s maximum doubles that to 225 steps: twice the foresight, twice the arithmetic, every frame, while you aim. The flight itself is stepped almost seven times finer (≈ 0.0028 s, six substeps per screen frame) — the game can afford fineness for one probe already flying, and spends coarseness on the preview because a rehearsal that's 95% right, instantly, beats a perfect one that arrives after you've let go.

How it's built — the coding choices

One acceleration function, three jobs

Everything gravitational in the game flows through a single function that answers one question — "at this point, at this moment, what is the total pull?" The flight calls it, the preview calls it, and the toggleable field display (the little arrows on a 42-unit grid, drawn wherever the pull is at least 28 units/s²) calls it to draw each arrow. Because there is one source of truth, the preview cannot disagree with the flight about what gravity is — only about step size. It also means the Gravity Constant slider, which simply rescales that function's masses, retunes the preview and the flight together, instantly and consistently.

Moving bodies are functions of time — and RK4 asks "when?"

A moving body's position is computed from the clock: centre plus a circle, angle = phase + speed × t. The integrator exploits this properly — its four trial evaluations ask for the pull at the step's start, midpoint, and end times, so a star that will have moved by the end of the step pulls from where it will be, not where it was. That is what makes the Binary Waltz preview honest: the dotted line already knows the stars are dancing. The bodies themselves ride rails, though — they circle their anchors at fixed rates and never react to the probe or to each other (see Simplifications).

The preview replays the whole ruleset, not just gravity

The prediction loop doesn't only integrate; it re-runs the game's discrete rules on the imagined path — wall bounces with the same reflection arithmetic as the flight, wormhole jumps with the same cooldown, drawn as a break in the dotted line — and it stops at the first predicted impact with a body or arrival at the beacon. The preview never draws you sailing through a planet: if the rehearsal dies, the dots end where it died, which is information too.

Winning, crashing, and bouncing are distance checks with stated numbers

No hidden judgement anywhere. The beacon captures you when your centre comes within its radius plus 4 units (28 on most holes) — at any speed; skimming the ring fast still counts. A body crashes you when your centre closes to its radius plus 6.8 units. Walls reflect you with the into-wall part of your velocity reversed and scaled by 1.88 in the reflection formula — a bounce that keeps 88% of that component, springy but not free. Fly 120 units past any edge of the course and you're reset to the tee, stroke counted, with your last shot kept as a dashed grey ghost — the game's memory of what you tried, so the next attempt starts from evidence instead of from vibes. Even the procedural-hole button is the same honesty turned sideways: random bodies, random wall, same physics — a course generator, not a different game.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the world is a flat 2D plane, in the game's own units throughout. The "n-body" is one-directional: every body pulls the probe, but the probe pulls nothing, and the bodies do not pull each other — moving bodies ride fixed circular rails at fixed rates, so the Binary Waltz stars will never spiral, drift, or notice you. Gravity is softened (the +1200 in the formula), so inside ~35 units of a centre the pull is gentler than Newton's and never infinite. The black hole is a very massive ordinary body: no event horizon, no relativity, no tidal shredding — you crash on its surface like any planet. The wormhole is a teleport that preserves velocity exactly. The preview integrates at 0.02 s steps against the flight's ≈ 0.0028 s, so on the most chaotic passes the dots and the flight can disagree slightly — the preview is a rehearsal, not a guarantee. The beacon's capture is a proximity check at any speed, despite the game's legend calling it "capture orbit" — no orbit is required, or checked. And the energy a moving star hands you in an assist is not deducted from the star, whose motion is scripted: the ledger balances in the physics, not in the animation.
