Everyday Physics · The back door to Get to Orbit

Get to Orbit — the lesson

Get to Orbit is the climb that Docking starts after: choose a world, a vehicle, a launch angle, a latitude, and an hour; then lift off and try to turn a fall into a circle. It is also the most ambitious of the collection's guest builds, because it does not stop at one planet. Earth, the Moon, Venus, and Saturn are four different gravity wells, and the L1 and L4 targets are not painted on the sky — they are balance points in a moving two-body field. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the audit that checked it against real orbital mechanics, and the receipts — including a proof that the game's circles hold, a proof that its gravity really does not care how heavy the ship is, and a proof that its L1 point, left alone, does exactly what a real L1 point is supposed to do: fall off its perch.

Get to Orbit was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction, from Steven's own brief; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

The physics

Orbit is falling and missing

The game's lesson line, printed over the canvas, is the whole thesis: orbit isn't about going high — it's about going sideways fast enough that you keep falling around the curve of the world. That is Newton's cannonball, from 1687: fire a cannonball from a mountaintop and it falls to the ground; fire it faster and it lands farther away; fire it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it at the same rate it falls, so it never lands at all. It is in orbit — in continuous free fall, with its horizontal speed doing all the work. The speed that makes the circle close at a distance r from the centre of a body with gravitational parameter μ is:

vcirc = √(μ / r) At Earth's surface that is 7.91 km/s — about 17,700 mph, or Mach 23. A little higher, at the game's low target of 320 km, it is 7.73 km/s; the game is honest about that distinction in its arithmetic, though its "Orbit Speed v₀" label does not say which altitude the number belongs to. See the constants table in the audit.

Escape speed — the speed that takes you away for good — is the same formula with one more factor of √2:

vesc = √(2μ / r) = √2 · vcirc 11.19 km/s from Earth's surface, 2.38 km/s from the Moon's, 10.36 km/s from Venus's, and about 35.5 km/s from Saturn's cloud tops. The √2 is not a coincidence: it is the ratio between the kinetic energy needed to climb out of a gravity well and the kinetic energy of the circular orbit at its rim.

Why the launch site and the hour are real physics

A rocket does not start from rest. It starts on a spinning planet, and the planet throws it. Earth's surface at the equator moves east at 465 m/s; at latitude φ it moves at 465·cos(φ) m/s. That is why real rockets launch east, and why they launch from Florida rather than Alaska: the boost is free, paid for by the planet, and it counts directly against the ~7.7 km/s a low orbit demands. It is also why the game's latitude slider matters and why its "time of day" is not decoration: the hour sets where on the planet the pad sits relative to the companion body, so it sets the geometry of the whole flight — the launch window, in miniature. Saturn's version of the same fact is spectacular: its equator spins at 9.87 km/s, more than the circular-orbit speed of any planet in the inner Solar System, which is why the game's Saturn note tells you to launch east from the equator. (Saturn's spin is also why its "surface" is a fiction the lesson has to name — see the honest box.)

Lagrange points: balance in a rotating field

Put two massive bodies in orbit around each other and ask where a third, small body can sit so that it keeps its place relative to them. In the frame that rotates with the two bodies, gravity from both pulls inward and the centrifugal pseudo-force pushes outward; at five points those balance exactly. Those are the Lagrange points. L1 sits between the two bodies, L2 just beyond the smaller one, L3 on the far side of the larger one — all three are saddles: a craft parked there will stay for a while, then drift away exponentially, the way a ball balanced on a ridge eventually rolls off. L4 and L5 sit 60° ahead of and behind the smaller body, forming an equilateral triangle with the two masses, and they are bowls: a craft that wanders is pulled back — provided the smaller mass is light enough, less than about 3.85% of the total mass. That is the Routh criterion, and it is why the game's companion mass ratio, μ ≈ 0.022, is not an accident: it is chosen to sit safely below it, so L4 is a genuine stable perch in the game's world. Real L4/L5 points are occupied: Jupiter's Trojan asteroids live at its L4 and L5, and NASA's Lucy mission is visiting them.

Mass cancels — until you bring an engine

In pure gravity, the craft's mass does not matter at all. Newton's second law says F = ma, and Newton's gravity says F = GMm/r²; the little m appears on both sides and cancels. A feather and a freight train fall on the same trajectory — the equivalence principle, which is why the Moon in this game is a vacuum: drop two different payloads there and they fly identical arcs. But the moment you bolt on a rocket, mass collects its debt. A rocket's Δv — the total change in velocity its propellant can buy — obeys Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation:

Δv = Isp · g0 · ln(mstart / mend) Isp is the exhaust speed in seconds' worth of thrust per unit of propellant weight; g0 is 9.80665 m/s²; the logarithm is the whole story. Doubling your fuel does not double your Δv, because you must also accelerate the fuel you have not burned yet. The only escape from the logarithm is a better exhaust: chemical rockets top out near Isp 450 s, nuclear thermal near 900 s, and electric ion engines reach 2,800 s and beyond — at thrusts so small that a real ion drive accelerates a ship by a fraction of a millimetre per second squared. The game prices all four of those trades, and the audit checks each one below.

The math the game runs

Each world's gravity is built from two numbers it stores — surface gravity g and radius R — by the identity μ = gR². The primary's pull is the plain inverse-square law, and the companion's pull is added with the indirect term that makes primary-centred coordinates honest: because the primary itself is being pulled toward the companion, the ship's acceleration relative to the primary must subtract the primary's own acceleration. The code's acceleration function is therefore:

a = −μ1r/r³ − μ2(r − d)/|r − d|³ − μ2d/D³ r is the ship's position from the primary, d the companion's position, D = |d| the companion's distance. The third term is the indirect term — the primary's own fall toward the companion, subtracted so the frame stays centred on the primary. Without it, the L points would be in the wrong place; with it, the primary can sit fixed at the origin on screen while the relative dynamics stay correct.

Time is advanced by a symplectic step (the audit has something to say about which symplectic step — below), with substeps capped at 0.45 s during burns and in atmosphere and at 3.5 s in vacuum, at up to 400× warp. Drag, when an atmosphere exists, is the textbook quadratic law, Fd = ½ρv²CdA, with an exponential density ρ = ρ0e−h/H and the air rotating with the planet. Fuel burns by ṁ = T / (Ispg0) — the mass-flow form of Tsiolkovsky, applied every step, so the ship genuinely gets lighter and its acceleration genuinely rises as the tanks empty.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

Get to Orbit arrived with Steven's own brief: settable weight and force (different force types), launch angle, time of day and position on Earth, a launch that tries to hit orbit, target orbits including an L point, and selectable gravity wells. The audit lifted the game's own physics functions out of the shipped file — same constants, same acceleration function, same step, same win checks — and ran them headlessly. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

The cause is visible in the code: Saturn's g is 10.44 m/s², an effective surface gravity that already includes the spin, and μ = gR² then inherits it. The other three worlds are built the same way and land within a third of a percent; Saturn is the one where the shortcut costs real accuracy.

The game's "Orbit Speed v₀" is not the surface circular speed in the table above: it is the circular speed at the low target altitude — 7.714 km/s at 320 km for Earth, 1.633 km/s at 95 km for the Moon, 7.107 km/s at 380 km for Venus, and 24.203 km/s at 2,200 km for Saturn. The arithmetic is right; the label does not say which altitude the number belongs to. Saturn's world note, "v₀ ≈ 24.4 km/s", is roughly that target-altitude figure, not the surface one.

Receipt 2: the circles hold — and the integrator is not what its label says

An exact circular launch — radius at the low target, speed √(μ/r) to the metre per second — was flown for five full periods through the game's own step, at its own vacuum substep of 3.5 s, with the companion switched off to isolate the integrator:

RunRadial range over 5 periodsRadius at the endSpecific-energy drift
Earth, companion off±13.6 km (0.404%)−34 m (−0.0005%)−2.6 × 10−9% — no leak
Moon, companion off±2.9 km (0.312%)−5.9 m (−0.0003%)−1.0 × 10−9% — no leak
Moon, companion on±2.7 km (0.297%)−79 m (−0.004%)+0.0091% — companion work, not a leak

The circles hold: no decay, no spiral, energy essentially conserved over five periods. But the radial range is the fingerprint of the integrator, and it does not match the label. The page's honesty card says "Velocity Verlet" twice; the code comments say it too. The shipped step is not velocity Verlet. It evaluates the acceleration once, at the old position, kicks the velocity, and then drifts the position — semi-implicit Euler, a first-order symplectic step. The audit proved the identification twice over: an independent semi-implicit Euler, run at the same conditions, reproduced the game's radial ranges to the printed digit (Earth 0.4035286060%, Moon 0.3120596923%), while a true velocity Verlet at the same 3.5 s substep hugs the circle about five hundred times tighter (Earth 0.000814%, Moon 0.000487%). Semi-implicit Euler is a respectable integrator — it is symplectic, which is why the energy does not leak — but it is first order, and the ±0.2% breathing of the game's orbits is its signature. The label is wrong; the orbits are fine, and the lesson names the difference because a player who reads "Verlet" is entitled to the tighter circle it promises.

Receipt 3: mass cancels in gravity — exactly

Two craft, one with a 1-tonne dry mass and one with 30 tonnes, launched on identical trajectories in the Moon's vacuum with thrust off, were flown for 2,000 steps through the game's own step. Their final positions and velocities were identical to the last printed digit — differences of exactly zero in all four numbers. The reason is structural: the gravity function takes only position and companion angle; the ship's mass never enters it. That is the equivalence principle, built into the code rather than asserted by it. Three honest qualifications, all in the lesson's box below: in an atmosphere the fixed drag area makes drag acceleration depend on mass; the railgun's fixed-energy pulse gives a heavier ship less speed; and the rocket engines make mass matter through thrust and fuel, which is the subject of the next receipt.

Two more mass facts the sliders hide. The payload slider sets the dry mass, and fuel scales with it at a fixed ratio — so for chemical and nuclear rockets the total Δv does not change with payload at all (12.24 km/s at 1 tonne and at 30 tonnes alike); only the thrust-to-weight ratio changes, because thrust is computed from a fixed 10-tonne reference vehicle. At 1 tonne the chemical TWR is 23.15; at 30 tonnes it is 0.77 and the game refuses to launch. And the railgun's fixed energy means muzzle velocity falls as 1/√mass: 22.32 km/s for a 1-tonne ship, 7.89 at 8 tonnes, 4.08 at 30. The energy is fixed; the speed is not.

Receipt 5: rotation — the boost is exact, the hour is a phase

Placed on the pad, Earth gives the ship exactly the spin the code claims: 465 m/s at the equator, 449.2 at 15°, 402.7 at 30°, 328.8 at 45°, 232.5 at 60°, and 120.4 at 75° — the cos(latitude) law to the printed digit. The slider stops at 75°, so there is no true polar pad; extrapolating the law, the equator-to-pole difference is the full 465 m/s. Time of day rotates the pad around the planet — noon puts it at +X with velocity +Y, midnight at −X with −Y, dawn at −Y with +X, dusk at +Y with −X — but it changes the direction, never the magnitude: the speed is always the latitude's spin boost. What the hour really buys is geometry: the companion's starting angle is reset to 0.45 radians on every pad placement, so the hour sets the pad's phase relative to the companion — the launch window. The auto-planner knows this and dials the hour to 9.25 for L1 and 13.25 for L4. One more honesty note: the planet does not actually rotate as the flight runs. The pad angle is an initial condition, and the atmosphere's rotation is computed from the pad latitude's spin boost, not from a rotating planet or the ship's current latitude.

Receipt 6: the L points are real — and L1 falls off its perch on schedule

Every world gets the same fictional companion: distance 9.0 planetary radii, mass ratio μ ≈ 0.022 (Venus 0.020). For Earth that means a companion 57,339 km away — not the real Moon at 384,400 km — with a mass 1.8× the real Moon's, circling in 37.6 hours instead of 27.3 days. The game's L1 sits at 0.818 of that distance from Earth: 46,910 km from Earth's centre, 10,429 km from the companion. The real Earth–Moon L1 is ~326,000 km from Earth's centre and ~58,000 km from the Moon. The game's figure is exact for its own compressed system — the solver is a genuine Newton iteration on the collinear-point equation, not an approximation — and wrong for the real Earth–Moon system, which the honesty card never claims to be modelling. (For Saturn the fiction is wider still: Titan is really 20.3 Saturn radii out with a mass ratio of 0.00024; the game puts its "Titan" at 9 radii with μ = 0.022, ninety times too massive.)

The dwell test is the proof that matters. A craft was parked at the game's L1 with exactly the L point's position and velocity, engines off, and flown with no guidance:

That is the correct behaviour, and it is the audit's favourite receipt in this game: a fake L1 would freeze the craft in place forever. This one drifts exponentially — roughly tripling its distance every two hours once it starts to go — and falls toward the companion, exactly as a saddle point should. The departure time is sensitive to numerical noise (a finer 0.1 s substep delays the collision to 27.8 hours), which is itself the signature of an unstable equilibrium: the seed changes when it falls, not whether. L4, by contrast, is a bowl: parked exactly, it wandered at most 22.7 km over seven days and ended 2.4 km from the point, moving at 0.10 m/s relative to it — stable because μ = 0.022 sits below the Routh limit of 0.0385.

Receipt 7: the win conditions are generous — by design, and worth knowing

An orbit counts when the ship's osculating elements (computed around the primary alone, ignoring the companion) sit inside a band for 20 seconds — 40 for the L points — with the engine off. Low circular: periapsis above the safe altitude, apoapsis below 0.85 planetary radii of altitude, and eccentricity under 0.18. High circular: a ±35–45% band around the high target, eccentricity under 0.20. Elliptical: periapsis safe, apoapsis past 3.8 radii of altitude, eccentricity between 0.35 and 0.98. L1/L4: within 1.45 planetary radii of the moving point — 9,238 km for Earth — with relative speed under 25% of the point's own orbital speed (545 m/s for Earth's L1). No mission requires completing a revolution. A highly eccentric orbit that technically circles counts for the elliptical mission, because that is what the mission asks for; a Low Circular win can sit well above the 320 km target ring as long as it is nearly circular. The 20-second dwell is a game rule, not an orbital one — and the timer decays at half speed rather than resetting when the conditions lapse, so a ship can drift in and out of the band and still collect the win.

How it's built — the coding choices

One companion per world, compressed to fit the screen

Four worlds, one template: each gets a companion at nine planetary radii and μ ≈ 0.022, so the planet, the companion, and all three Lagrange points fit in one navigable view. The companion is named per world — Moon for Earth, Titan for Saturn, a "Solar Tide Node" for Venus, a "Lagrange Partner" for the Moon — but the physics is the same template with different names. The L1 and L2 ratios are solved by Newton iteration on the collinear-point equation at load; L4 is placed exactly at the companion's distance, 60° ahead. The companion's drawn radius is 0.24 planetary radii and its collision radius 0.25 — a billiard ball, not a scaled body.

Guidance does most of the flying

By default the Flight Computer Assist is on, and it flies the mission: a gravity turn from vertical toward the launch angle, throttle cut at the target apoapsis, a prograde circularisation burn at apoapsis, or a velocity-matching capture at an L point. The launch angle is therefore not a one-time velocity vector for rocket types — it is the gravity-turn target the autopilot steers toward; only the railgun's impulse is truly a single vector at a single angle. Turning the assist off still leaves the gravity turn running during ascent; it only disables the coast and circularisation phases. The Auto-Plan button dials in a working profile per world and mission (nuclear for L points, railgun for the Moon's low orbit), and the manual burn keys override the autopilot only while held. That is a design choice, not a bug — but a player who wants to fly the whole ascent by hand should know the autopilot is still holding the stick.

Adaptive substeps, honest warp

The main loop clamps real frame time to 0.04 s, multiplies by the warp (1× to 400×), and splits the simulated interval into substeps of at most 0.45 s during burns and in atmosphere, 3.5 s in vacuum, capped at 140 substeps per frame. Warp changes how much simulated time each real second buys, never the substep size inside a regime — so a fast machine cannot change the physics. The completed-mission log lives in localStorage under one key, get_to_orbit_log_v1, and tracks 20 combinations: four worlds × five missions. The file carries no external references.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The integrator's name is wrong: the honesty card says "Velocity Verlet" twice, and so do the code comments, but the shipped step evaluates the acceleration once per step and then kicks and drifts — semi-implicit Euler. The audit proved it by reproduction: an independent semi-implicit Euler matches the game's radial ranges to the printed digit, while a true velocity Verlet at the same step hugs the circle ~500× tighter. The orbits are stable either way; the label overstates the method.

The companion system is fictional for every world: 9.0 radii and μ ≈ 0.022 everywhere. The real Moon is 60.3 radii out with μ = 0.01215; real Titan is 20.3 Saturn radii out with μ = 0.00024; Venus has no moon at all, and the Moon world's "Lagrange Partner" is invented. The L points are exact for the compressed system and wrong for the real ones — the card says "compressed companion orbit" and gives the numbers, so this is a disclosed toy, but the names Moon and Titan invite a reader to forget it.

Saturn and Venus have no honest launch pad: Saturn is a gas giant — there is no surface to stand on, and the code's own comments call the launch an "upper 1-bar cloud deck aerostat" launch, but the page never says so; it draws a solid disk, calls an impact an "Impact on Saturn surface", and lets a low orbit at 2,200 km fly below the main rings, which are drawn but never collide with anything. Venus is the mirror image: the code places the pad at the surface radius but runs an atmosphere of ρ0 = 2.6 kg/m³ — a cloud-deck density, not the ~65 kg/m³ of the real surface — and the world note's "3.4× Earth density" contradicts the code's own 2.6/1.225 = 2.1×. The honest card does not mention either convention.

Hidden balancing in the propellant: Saturn's chemical Isp is quietly raised to 580 s (from 450) and all Saturn fuel loads are multiplied by 2.8; Moon fuel loads are multiplied by 0.35. Neither is on the page. The ion drive's advertised Δv carries an unexplained ×0.72 factor, and its post-booster thrust is a fixed 0.22g — a magic electric drive, not an ion engine.

"Exact 3-body Newtonian gravity" is overstated twice: the companion's pull is softened inside 0.22 planetary radii (the force goes to zero at the companion's centre), and the primary sits fixed at the origin — the relative dynamics are correct thanks to the indirect term, but the page's note about tracking both bodies "accurately around barycenter" describes a frame the code does not actually integrate in. The win checks' orbital elements also ignore the companion entirely, which is fine far from it and an approximation near an L point.

Small print: the "Orbit Speed v₀" readout is the circular speed at the low target altitude, not at the surface. The heating model is a toy — a single coefficient, a linear cool-down, and a hard abort at 100% — with no max-Q or structural limit, as the card admits. The planet does not rotate during a flight; time of day is a starting phase, not a clock. L2 is drawn but has no mission, and L3 does not appear at all. The atmosphere's rotating air uses the pad latitude's spin boost at every altitude and position, not a true latitude-dependent wind. And the auto-planner silently rewrites your payload, propulsion, force, angle, and hour when you switch worlds or missions — convenient, and worth knowing before you read the dials as your own choices.
