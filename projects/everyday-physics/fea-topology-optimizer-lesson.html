Topology Optimizer — the lesson

Give a block of titanium a load, a wall to hang from, and permission to keep only 40% of itself — then watch it carve away everything the load never touches, until what remains is a shape no one drew: arches, trusses, spokes, the skeleton the forces themselves would choose. That carving is topology optimization, and this exhibit does it the real way — a genuine finite-element solve underneath a genuine SIMP optimizer, not a picture that fades where stress looks low. This page is the other half: the engineering, the mathematics the optimizer runs, and the audit that solved, benchmarked, and swept the shipped code — including the one place its stress numbers stop agreeing with its own physics.

Verdict: Substantially genuine — a real Q4 finite-element solve (cantilever within 3.0% of Timoshenko) and a real SIMP/OC optimizer cutting compliance 70–88% — but grey-material stress is scaled by ρ instead of the SIMP modulus (a 6.25× discrepancy: SF 0.94 shown, 5.89 consistent), and the Volume Used label mixes bases (Arch reads 42.5% at a 40% target).

The engineering

Asking a block of metal where it wants to be

Most structural design starts from a shape a person imagined and checks whether it survives. Topology optimization inverts that: you specify the domain (a rectangle of material), the supports, and the loads, and ask which distribution of material inside that domain is stiffest for a fixed budget of metal. The answer is rarely a shape anyone would have sketched — material gathers along the paths the load actually travels and vanishes everywhere else, which is why optimized parts look grown rather than machined. The exhibit's six presets are the field's classic examination questions: the cantilever, the MBB beam, the arch, the L-bracket, the wheel, and a plate that must route around a hole.

Finite elements: a structure as a very large spring problem

You cannot solve the elasticity equations over an arbitrary shape with a pencil, so the finite element method cuts the domain into small, simple pieces — here, bilinear four-node quadrilaterals (Q4 elements) on a regular grid — inside which the displacement is a simple interpolation of the corner values. Each element contributes a small stiffness matrix built from the material's plane-stress constitutive law; assembled, they form one enormous linear system, K u = f: stiffness times displacement equals load. Solve it and you know how every node moves; differentiate the displacement inside each element and you know its strain, its stress, and its strain energy. The exhibit's default mesh is 48 × 24 = 1,152 elements, 1,225 nodes, 2,450 degrees of freedom — small by industrial standards, entirely real in structure.

SIMP: letting every element choose how solid to be

The optimizer's design variable is a density ρ for every element, from 0 (void) to 1 (solid). A fractional density needs a fractional stiffness, and the field's standard answer is SIMP — Solid Isotropic Material with Penalisation: stiffness scales as ρp, with the penalty p (3, as shipped) chosen so that half-density material delivers only an eighth of the stiffness. Grey is deliberately a bad bargain, so the optimizer is pushed toward black-and-white designs a factory could actually cut. Each iteration solves the finite-element system, asks every element how much the structure's compliance would fall if it were a little more solid (its sensitivity), smooths those sensitivities over a neighbourhood so the design cannot checkerboard, and then redistributes density under a volume budget using an optimality-criteria (OC) update — the same recipe as the reference codes the research groups teach from. Repeat, and the skeleton emerges.

Compliance, and what "better" means here

The quantity being minimised is compliance — fTu, the work the load does through the displacement it causes. A stiff structure barely moves under its load, so its compliance is small; compliance is, in effect, inverse stiffness wearing a measurable number. The exhibit's telemetry reports strain energy U = ½ fTu, exactly half the compliance — the label on the HUD is the honest one, and every percentage reduction quoted below is the same under either name. What optimization buys is dramatic: on every preset, the carved design keeps the same volume budget and gives up 70–88% of the starting compliance.

The math the game runs

The solve

K u = f,   solved by Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients The stiffness operator is applied matrix-free, element by element; fixed degrees of freedom are removed from the solve, and loads are distributed bilinearly to nodes. The element stiffness is formed from the plane-stress D matrix and the strain-displacement matrix B at the four points of a 2×2 Gauss quadrature rule. Material as shipped: Ti-6Al-4V, E₀ = 114 GPa, ν = 0.30, yield 880 MPa, thickness 20 mm, element size 25 mm — so the default domain is 1200 × 600 mm. The audit measured the free-DOF equilibrium residual of the initial uniform design at ‖f − Ku‖ / ‖f‖ = 9.09 × 10−5, inside the requested 1 × 10−4 tolerance; a high-accuracy solid solve reached 9.26 × 10−12.

The SIMP interpolation and its sensitivity

Eeff = Emin + ρp(1 − Emin),   Emin = 10−5;   dc/dρ = −p(1 − Emin)ρp−1(ueT Ke ue) Exactly the standard compliance sensitivity for this interpolation — the audit checked the formula in the shipped code term by term. Sensitivities then pass through the usual Sigmund-style cone filter (weights rmin − distance, neighbour ρ·dc summed and divided by ρ and the weight sum), which is what keeps single-element checkerboards out of the designs. Changing the filter radius rebuilds the filter and un-converges the design, as it should.

The OC update

ρ ← clamp( ρ · √( −dc / (λ · mean|dc|) ),   move limit 0.2,   bounds [0.001, 1] ) A genuine optimality-criteria update: the Lagrange multiplier λ is found by bisection so the updated densities land exactly on the volume target, no element may move more than 0.2 in one iteration, and the normalisation by mean sensitivity magnitude is absorbed into the bisected λ without changing the update's form. The audit's 60-step volume checks landed at 0.200001 for a 0.20 target, 0.3999995 for 0.40, and 0.6500001 for 0.65 — the budget is kept to five decimal places, over the elements the optimizer is actually allowed to design.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit extracted the solver class from the shipped page and exercised it directly — equilibrium residuals, patch tests, a solid cantilever benchmark, force scaling, penalty sweeps, all six presets run to the page's own convergence flag — and then drove the browser UI: presets, sliders, paint tools, load dragging, views, and shortcuts. Every number below is measured.

Receipt 1: the element is a real Q4, and rigid motion costs it nothing

A correct element must report zero stress when the whole body simply moves or rotates — otherwise the solver invents forces from nothing. The audit's patch tests put the shipped element through a constant translation and an infinitesimal rotation:

Patch testMeasured
Constant translation — strain energy4.85 × 10−12
Infinitesimal rotation — strain energy1.37 × 10−16
Peak von Mises in either rigid fieldbelow 1.6 × 10−13 MPa

Zero, to numerical precision. Combined with the equilibrium residual above, this is the receipt that the deflection pictures on screen are the solution of a stiffness system, not an artist's impression of one.

Receipt 2: the cantilever lands within 3.0% of beam theory — the right beam theory

The audit built a fully solid 1200 × 600 × 20 mm cantilever, hung 120 kN at the tip centre, and compared the solved tip deflection against the two textbook predictions:

Prediction / measurementTip-centre deflection
Euler–Bernoulli beam theory1.684 mm
Timoshenko beam theory (shear included)2.013 mm
Shipped FE solve2.073 mm — +3.0% from Timoshenko

The choice of comparator is the lesson. This beam's length is only twice its depth, so shear deformation — which Euler–Bernoulli ignores — carries a real share of the deflection, and the FE result sits +23.1% above Euler precisely because it models the shear the simple theory omits. Against Timoshenko, which includes it, the solve agrees to 3.0%. Doubling the load on a fixed solved state scaled displacement ×2, stress ×2, and energy ×4, exactly as linear elasticity requires. One bend, kept visible: the fixed-end bending stress reads high — FE centroid σxx of ±139.1 MPa against ±113.8 MPa nominal at the same points, about 22% over, with clamp end-effects in the other components. Plausible coarse-Q4 behaviour; a reason to trust the deflections more than the local stress peaks.

Receipt 4 — the false number: grey-material stress uses the wrong factor

Here the exhibit contradicts its own constitutive law, and the audit prints it plainly. The displacement solve gives an element stiffness Eeff = Emin + ρp(1 − Emin). But when the code computes element stress for the von Mises view, the safety factor, and your probe, it multiplies by ρ instead. In solid (ρ = 1) and void (ρ ≈ 0) material the two nearly agree; in grey material they diverge badly. Measured at the initial uniform design, ρ = 0.4, p = 3, where Eeff = 0.064009:

Peak von Mises / safety factorValue
As the page reports it (stress × ρ)933.5 MPa · SF 0.94
Consistent with the page's own SIMP law (stress × Eeff)149.4 MPa · SF 5.89
Discrepancy6.25×

Same mesh, same solve, same element — the only difference is which factor the post-processor applies, and it flips the verdict from "failing" (SF 0.94) to "comfortable" (SF 5.89). The scope matters and is stated exactly: in all six fully converged preset runs the peak happened to sit in a ρ = 1 element, so those final peaks were not changed by the factor. The false numbers appear during optimization, in any probe of grey material, and in any design whose peak sits in a grey element — which is to say, precisely while you are watching the optimizer work and deciding whether the emerging shape is safe. Read the stress view as a map of where stress concentrates; do not read its grey-material values, or any safety factor drawn from them, as physical.

Receipt 5: "Volume Used" and the target slider use different denominators

The optimizer's volume target applies to the free, designable elements, and there it is kept exactly (Receipt 3's five-decimal landings). The HUD's "Volume Used," however, excludes permanent voids from its denominator while including permanent solids — a different base. On presets with built-in voids or fixed regions, the two numbers visibly disagree:

Preset (target slider: 40%)HUD "Volume Used"

Neither number is fabricated — they are honest fractions of different wholes, presented side by side as if they shared one. When the Arch reads 42.5% beside a 40% target, the optimizer has not overshot; the label has changed the subject.

Receipt 6: the controls all do real work

The audit drove the interface, and found no wholly dead control. Painting a void near element (10, 22) moved the live solve from 1942.9 J / 32.38 mm / 934 MPa to 2393.5 J / 40.61 mm / 1390 MPa; painting the same spot solid, then erasing, moved it back through 2154.5 J / 33.03 mm to 1958.9 J / 32.51 mm. Dragging the load anchor changed the solve (1958.9 J → 1305.9 J), and clicking a new load point moved it again (→ 380.1 J, max deflection 10.76 mm). Rotating the force from 270° to 180° turned a 32.38 mm-class response into a 6.77 mm one. All six presets load with the correct mesh (48 × 24, or 34 × 34 for L-Bracket and Wheel) and force angle (90° for the Wheel, 270° for the rest); Step, Play/Pause (including Space), the three views, the four tools, and the toggles all work.

How it's built — the coding choices

A matrix-free solve, sized for a browser tab

The stiffness matrix is never assembled into a giant array; the PCG solver applies it element by element, which keeps 2,450 degrees of freedom cheap enough to re-solve every optimizer iteration in a web page. The trade is pace: the main loop performs at most one optimizer step per animation frame, so the delivered iteration rate is bounded by frame rate and step cost — the solver alone ran 100 default steps in 4.07 s headlessly (about 24.6 steps/s before rendering). The pace slider's 18 it/s is plausible on a good desktop; its 60 it/s ceiling is not guaranteed by the loop's structure.

One solve behind every view

Density, von Mises, and principal-stress views are three renderings of the same density field and the same displacement solution, which is why painting, erasing, and load-dragging update all of them coherently. One reporting quirk, measured and minor: a step leaves the densities one update ahead of the last solve, so the first telemetry refresh after a step can pair the new density field with the previous equilibrium solution; the next solve removes the inconsistency. It is a one-iteration lag in the readouts, not in the physics.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The stress factor is the serious one: element stress is scaled by ρ where the page's own SIMP law requires Eeff = Emin + ρp(1 − Emin) — a 6.25× discrepancy at ρ = 0.4, p = 3 (933.5 MPa / SF 0.94 reported; 149.4 MPa / SF 5.89 consistent). Converged finals escaped it only because their peaks sat in solid elements.

Even solid-region peaks are not design-grade: they are element-centroid values on a coarse mesh, they sit at clamps and load points where stress is singularity-prone, and the fixed-end centroid σxx already reads about 22% above nominal beam stress (±139.1 MPa vs ±113.8 MPa). Trust the shapes, the deflections, and the energy reductions; treat any single peak number as an indicator, not a certification.

Volume Used mixes bases: the optimizer's target is a fraction of designable elements; the HUD's percentage excludes permanent voids and includes permanent solids — Arch 42.5% and Wheel 59.5% beside a 40% target, and L-Bracket at 40.0% of its designable base but 24.8% of its whole bounding domain.

"Compliance" vs "Strain Energy": the code reports U = ½ fTu. The HUD label "Strain Energy" is correct; the internal name "compliance" is off by a factor of two if read literally, since compliance proper is fTu = 2U. Percentage reductions are unaffected.

Convergence is a stopping rule, not a proof: the flag is "maximum density change below 0.0045 after at least 22 iterations." The default cantilever needed 165 steps to trip it (at step 100 the change was still 0.00620). Nothing checks an optimality gradient — standard practice for teaching codes, worth knowing before calling a design "optimal" rather than "converged."

The deformation slider is a picture control: rendering normalises displacement, capping the maximum visual deflection at about 0.24 × deformScale element widths, rather than multiplying the physical displacement by the labelled factor. The telemetry deflection remains the physical value; the drawing is scaled for legibility.

Small print: the "Clear Painted" button does more than its label — it reloads the whole preset, resetting densities, iteration count, load positions, and painted masks together; the material constants (E₀ = 114 GPa, ν = 0.30, yield 880 MPa for Ti-6Al-4V) are standard textbook values, with real yield in practice depending on product form and temper, which the page does not specify; and the model is 2-D plane stress at fixed 20 mm thickness — no buckling, no fatigue, no manufacturing constraints on the shapes it grows.
