The Resonance Lab Lesson — the physics behind the exhibit

The back door to Resonance — the Physics of Vibration, in Everyday Physics. The exhibit is the front door — you learn by sweeping the dial. This page is the same lesson with the sums showing: what resonance is, why the phase flips, what Q really counts, the numbers the exhibit's own proof runs produced, and the famous bridge story told correctly.

The exhibit in one paragraph

Four panels, one equation. A mass on a spring is driven by an oscillating force and its response is measured — not just calculated — frequency by frequency. A row of Barton's pendulums shows that the same drive, delivered identically to six different lengths, is drunk deeply by exactly one of them. Two pure tones a few hertz apart produce beats, and the pulse rate is counted out of the summed signal itself. And a series RLC circuit turns out to be the identical mathematics in wires — which is why the fourth panel is a radio tuner. Every panel carries its own measurements, and the proof table below is the exhibit's core examining itself.

The physics

Natural frequency: the rhythm a system chooses

Leave a mass on a spring alone and it oscillates at one frequency, f₀ = (1/2π)√(k/m) — stiffer spring or lighter mass, faster rhythm. A pendulum chooses (1/2π)√(g/L) — length only; the bob's mass is irrelevant, which is the whole secret of Barton's demonstration. A series LC circuit chooses 1/(2π√(LC)). In each case the frequency is set by a contest between an inertia (mass, gravity's lever, inductance) and a restoring pull (spring, gravity, capacitor), and in each case the system, disturbed, returns to that rhythm and no other. That is the frequency the rest of this page is about feeding.

Driving: pushes that arrive in step, and pushes that don't

Now push, rhythmically, from outside. If your pushes come much slower than the natural rhythm, the system simply follows your hand — it is always roughly where the force points, and the motion is small: the static stretch F₀/k, give or take. If your pushes come much faster, the inertia cannot keep up; the mass barely moves, and what motion there is runs against the force. In between, at the natural frequency, something special happens: each push arrives exactly when the mass is already moving the way the push points. Energy handed over is never handed back. Cycle by cycle the amplitude grows — until the energy lost to friction (damping) each cycle grows to match what each push delivers. That balance is the steady state, and at resonance it can be enormous: the amplitude at f₀ is Q times the static stretch. A child on a swing, pushed in step, is this paragraph: small pushes, large result, purely because of timing.

The phase tells you where you are

The response lags the drive, and the lag is a dial that reads out your position on the curve. Far below f₀ the lag is near 0° — the mass moves with the force. Far above, the lag approaches 180° — the mass moves against it. Exactly at f₀ the lag is 90°, a quarter cycle: the force is then always aligned with the velocity, which is the precise condition for maximum energy transfer — force doing positive work at every instant. This is why the proof's phase measurement matters: 90.0° at f₀ is the signature that the peak really is where the theory says the energy flows best. (Strictly, the velocity peaks exactly at f₀. The displacement peak sits a hair below it — see the honest note in the proof table.)

Q: how many cycles to ring down, how sharp the peak is

Q (quality factor) is the single number that sizes a resonance. It has two faces, and they are the same fact seen from two sides:

Ring-down: stop driving and count. The amplitude decays like e^(−π·n/Q) over n cycles — after about Q/π cycles it has fallen to 1/e (37%) of where it started; the energy, going as amplitude squared, falls to 1/e in about Q/(2π) cycles. Equivalently, Q = 2π × (energy stored) / (energy lost per cycle). A tuning fork with Q in the thousands rings for minutes; a car suspension with Q near 1 settles in a cycle or two.

Sharpness: the width of the response peak, measured between the half-power points (where the amplitude has fallen to 1/√2 of the peak), is Δf ≈ f₀/Q. High Q rings long and peaks narrow — the same low losses cause both. For a radio this width is the selectivity, the whole game of panel 4: Δf is how much of the dial your station occupies. A broadcast FM station needs roughly 200 kHz of width; a tuner whose Δf were 2 MHz would hear ten stations at once, and one whose Δf were 2 kHz would slice the music to ribbons.

Beats: resonance's quiet cousin

Add two tones of nearly equal frequency and no driving is involved at all — just interference in time. The tones drift in and out of step: when they agree, the sum is twice as loud; when they oppose, silence. The loudness pulses |f₁ − f₂| times per second. Tuners of pianos and guitars live on this: beat rate zero is the definition of "in tune," and a slow wah-wah is a measuring instrument with hertz-level resolution that fits in an ear.

The math

The driven, damped oscillator — the only equation on the page:

m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F₀·cos(2πf·t),   with c = m·ω₀/Q,   ω₀ = √(k/m)

Its steady-state answer, x = A·cos(2πf·t − φ):

Checking the f₀ row by hand: at ω = ω₀ the first term under the root vanishes, leaving A = (F₀/m) / (ω₀²/Q) = F₀·Q/(m·ω₀²) = F₀·Q/k, since m·ω₀² = k. The exhibit's defaults make it tangible: F₀ = 10 N on a k = 40 N/m spring stretches it 0.25 m statically; at Q = 8, driven at f₀, the same force swings the mass ±2.00 m — eight times the stretch, from timing alone. Nothing about the force changed.

The 0.40% is the proof being more accurate than the slogan. The displacement peak does not sit exactly at f₀; it sits at f₀·√(1 − 1/(2Q²)), which at Q = 8 is 0.99609·f₀. The search found 0.9960 and 0.4980 — the peak's true address, to four significant figures. The claim as tested ("within 1% of f₀") is the honest version of the textbook slogan, and the exhibit's Help says so. Likewise the bandwidth: 0.1013 against the leading-order f₀/Q = 0.1000 — the 1.3% excess is the real width of the displacement curve, which the f₀/Q rule approximates. And the circuit's current, being the analogue of velocity, peaks at f₀ to every digit the search can resolve — exactly as the theory of the velocity resonance promises.

How the Barton number was earned

The row runs from rest for a simulated 45 s — about seven time constants of the matched pendulum (τ = 2Q/ω_d ≈ 6.4 s) — with every pendulum driven by the identical string signal. Amplitudes are then read by DFT at the drive frequency over the last six drive periods. The matched pendulum's steady amplitude, κ·Θ·Q = 0.12 × 0.30 × 10 = 0.36 rad, matches the measured 0.359 rad; the strongest pretender, the 0.62 m pendulum, manages 0.093 rad — its ω²/|ω² − ω_d²| leverage of about 2.6, times the same κ·Θ, with nothing accumulated. Same pushes, same string, same 45 seconds: the only difference is the length, and therefore the rhythm.

The bridge, told correctly

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed on 7 November 1940, four months after it opened, in a wind of roughly 40 mph. The film of its twisting — the deck rolling in a torsional wave until it tore itself apart — became the most famous exhibit in all of engineering education, and for decades the caption under it read resonance: wind shedding vortices at a rhythm that matched the bridge's natural torsional frequency, pushing it the way you push a swing.

It is a beautiful story and, on the modern evidence, it is not what happened. Billah and Scanlan's 1991 re-examination ("Resonance, Tacoma Narrows bridge failure, and undergraduate physics textbooks," American Journal of Physics) showed the numbers never fit: the vortex-shedding frequency that morning was not the bridge's torsional frequency, and the oscillation's character — a sudden switch into a violent torsional mode at a threshold wind speed — is the signature of aeroelastic flutter. In flutter, the structure's own motion through the air creates the forces that enlarge the motion: the deck twists, the twist changes the airflow, the changed airflow twists it further. No external rhythm is required; a perfectly steady wind will do, because the bridge is supplying the rhythm itself. Engineers now design against flutter explicitly (it is the same instability that once destroyed aircraft wings and that bridge decks are wind-tunnel tested for), and the honest caption is: a self-excited aeroelastic instability in a steady wind.

Why keep the story in a resonance exhibit at all? Because the correction is the lesson. Resonance is real — panels 1, 2 and 4 will measure it for you — but "resonance" used as a synonym for "it vibrated and broke" hides the mechanism, and the mechanism is where the engineering lives. The bridge did vibrate, and it did break. What it mostly wasn't, was pushed in step.

And the wine glass

The glass trick, by contrast, is genuine resonance — a rim driven at its own ringing frequency until the strain at a flaw exceeds the material. It has been done with an unaided voice (vocal coach Jamie Vendera, 2005, on camera), and it remains hard: a thin glass, a note held within a hertz or so of the glass's f₀, at something like 100 dB at the rim, without wandering. The exhibit's panel 1 is the reason for both halves of that sentence — the peak is that rewarding, and that narrow.

How the exhibit is built

One simulation core (marked RESO-CORE in the page source) serves all four panels and the proofs: RK4 integrators for the oscillator, the circuit, and each Barton pendulum; an analytic steady-state for the drawn curves; and a measurer that integrates from rest, watches a DFT of the motion over whole drive cycles until the reading settles, and reports amplitude and phase. The sweeps you run from the buttons use that same measurer at a coarser step. The Barton driver is held at constant amplitude — a real driver pays its energy into the string and slowly fades; holding it is the one stated liberty in panel 2, and the Help on the exhibit page says so.
