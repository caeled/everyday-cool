Turing Garden — the lesson

Turing Garden is a petri dish with two chemicals in it and no instructions. You paint a few drops of reagent V onto a dish full of reagent U, tune two knobs — the feed rate f and the kill rate k — and the dish decides what to grow: leopard spots, zebra stripes, branching coral, dividing cells, travelling waves. Nothing in the code knows what a spot is. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the 1952 idea the dish is built on, the two equations it actually runs, and the audit that grew every preset the game ships — plus a few famous addresses it doesn't — and wrote down what really came up.

Turing Garden was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its solver headlessly.

The science

Turing's last paper: how the leopard gets its spots

In 1952, Alan Turing published The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis — his final major work, and a guess about how an embryo, starting from near-uniform tissue, talks itself into having a pattern. His proposal: two chemicals (he called them morphogens) that react with each other and diffuse at different rates can amplify microscopic randomness into macroscopic order — spots, stripes, rings, dapples — with no blueprint, no template, and no cell in charge. The mathematics sat mostly untested for decades because the patterns it predicted are exquisitely sensitive to the chemistry; the Gray–Scott model in this dish, a stripped-down two-chemical system studied in depth by John Pearson in 1993, became the version everyone could actually run. It is a proof of principle, not a portrait of a real embryo — the game's own honesty card says as much, and the audit below found the card accurate as far as it goes.

The whole trick: the brake must travel faster than the accelerator

Here is the mechanism in plain words. Chemical V is the activator: where V is present, the reaction UV² converts U into more V — V makes more of itself, but only where it already is, and it diffuses slowly (DV = 0.1050). Chemical U is the feedstock, and its local absence is the inhibitor: a growing blob of V eats the U around it, and because U diffuses fast (DU = 0.2097, almost exactly twice as fast), the shortage spreads out in a wide halo around the blob. So every blob promotes itself at short range and suppresses its surroundings at long range. That asymmetry — short-range activation, long-range inhibition — is the entire engine. It sets a natural wavelength: blobs repel each other into regular spacing, which is why the spots in this dish space themselves out like trees in an orchard instead of clumping. Break the asymmetry — make both chemicals diffuse at the same speed — and the dish can only ever go uniformly one colour or the other. The pattern lives in the ratio.

One pair of equations, a whole zoo — the knobs choose the animal

Everything the dish grows comes from the same two rules; the feed rate f (how fast fresh U is supplied) and the kill rate k (how fast V is removed) select the regime. Rich feed and brisk kill near the top of the living region give expanding branching fronts — coral. A little less feed and spots become self-sustaining individuals that swell, pinch, and divide — the mitosis regime. Starve the system slightly differently and the spots stop dividing and simply hold their spacing — leopard spots. Lower the kill rate and spots elongate, merge, and wander into a connected labyrinth — zebra stripes. Down in the low-feed corner the chemistry goes oscillatory: fronts that travel and spirals that rotate, forever. And outside the living region entirely, the two honest failures: too much kill and every seed starves (extinction), too little and V floods the dish to a uniform red sheet (the flood). The audit grew all of these, below — including two addresses from the folklore map of this system that turned out to be mislabelled in this dish's territory.

The math the game runs

The solver is the genuine Gray–Scott system, term for term — the audit checked each one against the printed equations on the game's own honesty card:

u′ = u + DU·∇²u − uv² + f·(1 − u) The feedstock. It diffuses, it is consumed by the cubic reaction term −uv² (one U and two V meet; the U becomes V), and it is replenished toward full strength at rate f — the feed.
v′ = v + DV·∇²v + uv² − (f + k)·v The activator. It diffuses at half U's speed, it is created by the same cubic term with the opposite sign — every reaction event moves one unit from the first ledger to the second — and it decays away at the combined rate (f + k): the kill, plus dilution by the feed itself. Time step is folded into the constants (dt = 1 per step), both fields are clamped to [0, 1] after every step, and the fields are kept in Float32 — the audit's long run watched v bottom out at 5.7 × 10−44, a float32 denormal, in the emptiest corners of the dish.

The Laplacian is a 9-point isotropic stencil — and here the audit found the build's one quiet liberty. The classic weights for this stencil are 0.20 on the four edge neighbours, 0.05 on the four diagonals, −1 on the centre. The game ships 0.80 / 0.20 / −4: the classic stencil multiplied by exactly four, which at dt = 1 moves four times as much chemical per step relative to the reaction as the canonical implementation does. The garden still grows the canonical species — the comparison run in Receipt 3 grew the same coral with the classic weights — but it grows them faster and in smaller, more numerous colonies. The stability books still balance: the stencil's most violent mode (a perfect checkerboard) has eigenvalue −6.4, so the shipped DU uses 0.2097 × 6.4 ≈ 1.34 of the explicit-scheme limit of 2 — about 1.5× of headroom — and the 20,000-step run below shows no checkerboard growth whatsoever.

The audit — presets grown, addresses checked, receipts kept

The audit lifted the game's own stepper out of the shipped file — same constants, same stencil, same clamps, same Float32 fields, same default toroidal boundary — and ran it headlessly on the game's 240 × 240 grid (57,600 cells, 43,348 of them inside the dish rim), seeded with the game's own seeding patterns. Morphology was scored with the game's own telemetry definitions: coverage (share of dish cells with v > 0.12), cluster counts and circularity from its half-resolution connected-component analysis, and its activity meter (mean |Δv| per step × 10,000 — near zero for a frozen pattern, high for a living one).

The dead address deserves its footnote, because the reason is a theorem, not a tuning quirk. A uniform (non-patterned) living state of this system can only exist when k ≤ ½√f − f — the saddle-node line. At f = 0.035 that ceiling is k = 0.0585, and 0.065 is far above it. Localised spots can outlive the uniform state a little — the game's own mitosis at (0.028, 0.062) does exactly that — but at (0.035, 0.065) even they starve. The published maps of this system were drawn for particular implementations over particular runs; carried into a solver whose diffusion runs four times hotter per step, the borders move. The game's labels match its territory. The folklore labels, pasted onto the same coordinates, do not — and now there are receipts either way.

How it's built — the coding choices

The brush writes chemistry, not pixels

Painting a seed writes actual field values into the grid — u ≈ 0.42–0.48, v ≈ 0.28–0.42 with a small random jitter per cell — and the eraser writes the sterile state (u = 1, v = 0). There is no separate "seed object" anywhere: once painted, your drop is just chemistry, and the solver treats it exactly like chemistry that grew there. The preset seedings are brushwork too — coral gets 18 polyps in two rings, mitosis gets 4 clean droplets so the division is unmistakable, waves get a half-and-half block whose broken edge nucleates the spirals.

The game judges statistics, not resemblance

Pattern-match scoring never compares your dish to a picture. Every eighth frame the game computes field statistics — coverage, a complexity score (mean |∇v| × 350, a boundary-density meter), and a connected-component census on a half-resolution 120 × 120 mask: cluster count, each cluster's isoperimetric circularity (Manhattan-calibrated so a discrete circle scores ~1), the count of isolated round spots (area 6–150 mask cells, circularity ≥ 0.62), the largest cluster's share, and a mitosis gain tracked from your cluster-count baseline. Each challenge is four such statistics held in range simultaneously for 3–4 seconds. The dish's status line is read off the same fields: EXTINCT below 1.5% coverage, FLOODED above 78% (or above 66% with low complexity — a smooth red sheet), OSCILLATING when activity and complexity are both high. Wins are stored in one localStorage key, turing_garden_specimens_v1, and nothing else persists.

The honest trade-offs: resolution, rate, and a sketch for a map

The dish is 240 × 240 Float32 cells stepped 10 times per animation frame — about 600 steps a second at a 60 fps display — rendered at native resolution into a 240-pixel canvas that CSS enlarges (the soft microscope look is partly that upscale). The morphology census runs at half resolution, which is why spot areas in the telemetry are quarter-scale. The (f, k) phase map beside the sliders is drawn, not measured: its upper edge is the true saddle-node curve ½√f − f plus a small fitted bump, and its width is a sine-shaped allowance. As a sketch of where life lives it is serviceable — and Receipt 2 shows exactly where its edges stop being true.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate: the equations it prints are the equations it runs, constants included; it confesses the 2-chemical toy model, the flat static grid with no growth or curvature, and the absence of molecular noise and cell boundaries. Real morphogenesis — gene networks, dozens of morphogens, mechanics — is named as out of scope. All of that holds.

The stencil runs four times hot: the shipped Laplacian weights (0.80 / 0.20 / −4) are the classic 9-point weights ×4, so per step the diffusion is four times stronger relative to the reaction than in the canonical implementation these Du/Dv values come from. Measured effect: same pattern species, faster growth, finer grain (Receipt 3). Anyone carrying (f, k) addresses from published Gray–Scott maps into this dish should expect the borders to have moved — Receipt 2's dead mitosis address is the standing example.

The phase map disagrees with the solver it decorates: three of the six presets — Mitosis (0.028, 0.062), Spots (0.030, 0.062), and Waves (0.014, 0.047) — sit above the map's own drawn living band, in the region it paints as extinction, yet all three thrive in the dish (Receipt 1). The map's top edge tracks where the uniform living state dies; localised patterns outlive it. Trust the dish over the map.

The petri dish is a mask on a torus: the default boundary is toroidal wrap on the full square grid. In wrap mode the chemistry runs everywhere — the circle is drawn over it, outside-rim cells are hidden from the render and excluded from telemetry, but they are simulated, and the brush can paint there; a colony outside the rim can grow unseen and wrap back into view. Switch Walls to sealed and the rim becomes a true zero-flux boundary (the solver copies edge values outward, cell by cell, including a precomputed rim mapping for the circle).

Seatbelts the canonical model doesn't wear: both fields are clamped to [0, 1] after every step, and the fields are Float32. Neither changed any morphology the audit observed — the clamps bind only where the mathematics was already heading out of range — but they are part of why this solver cannot blow up, and they are not in the printed equations.

Coral is a phase, not a resting state: left alone, the coral preset's coverage was still climbing at step 8,000 (64%) toward the game's own 78% FLOODED line. The coral challenge's 25–55% window is a window in time as much as in parameter space — grow it, hold it, log it, before the reef eats the dish.
