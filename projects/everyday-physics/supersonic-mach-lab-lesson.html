Mach — the lesson

Fly faster than your own pressure signals can run ahead of you, and the air stops flowing around you politely. It piles up into shock waves — surfaces where pressure, density, and temperature jump in a distance thinner than a sheet of paper — and every rule of subsonic flight is rewritten by them. This wind tunnel and rocket-nozzle bench runs the real compressible-flow mathematics those jumps obey. This page is the other half of the lab: the physics of shocks, expansion fans, and choked nozzles, and the audit that checked the shipped solvers against NACA 1135 and an independent recomputation — digit for digit.

Verdict: The gas dynamics are table-perfect — NACA 1135 to every digit, the cone at exactly asin(1/M), the nozzle solver digit-perfect — but drag carries a silent +0.012, the airfoil secretly flies at 0.72× its named half-angle, and the freestream readout keeps air's gas constant under CO₂ and helium (helium off 2.7×).

The physics

The Mach cone: outrunning your own news

Sound is how air announces a disturbance. A source moving at Mach M outruns the announcement sideways, and the wavefronts pile onto a cone whose half-angle is μ = asin(1/M) — the Mach angle. At M = 2.2 the cone sits at 27.04°; at M = 4, at 14.48°. Nothing upstream of the cone has heard you coming. The lab draws that cone from the nose at exactly ±μ, and its bow-shock hyperbola asymptotes to the same angle far downstream — geometry checked against the solver, below.

Shocks: the jump, head-on and at an angle

Meet the flow head-on and the jump is a normal shock: pressure, density, and temperature step up by ratios fixed entirely by M and γ, the flow behind drops subsonic, and total pressure is lost — the tax entropy collects. Tilt the surface and the shock tilts with it: an oblique shock at angle β turns the flow through the wedge angle θ, and the θ–β–M relation picks β — usually the weak branch, the shallower shock a real attached wedge flies with. Turn too far — past θmax, which is only 22.97° at M = 2 — and no attached solution exists: the shock detaches and stands off the nose as a bow shock.

Expansion fans: the gentle opposite

Where a surface turns away from supersonic flow, there is no jump at all. The flow fans out through a spread of Mach waves, accelerating and cooling continuously — a Prandtl–Meyer expansion, governed by the function ν(M), which counts how much turning a flow at Mach M has "spent." A diamond airfoil is the two ideas stitched together: oblique shock at the leading edge, Prandtl–Meyer fan at the shoulder, and the pressure difference between the faces is its lift and its wave drag. Shock–expansion theory computes both exactly — which is what makes the lab's drag number auditable, and its small silent addition findable.

The nozzle: choke, expand, push

A rocket nozzle is a converging–diverging hourglass. At the throat the flow chokes — M = 1, the maximum mass flow the chamber pressure can push through that area. Past the throat the widening duct does what intuition says it shouldn't: it accelerates the now-supersonic flow, the exit Mach set purely by the area ratio Ae/A* through the area–Mach relation. Thrust, exit pressure, mass flow, and specific impulse all follow. Match the exit pressure to the ambient and the plume leaves clean; mismatch it and the exhaust corrects itself in the open air — in the shock diamonds you can see in test-stand photographs.

The math the game runs

Normal and oblique shocks

p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1)·(M₁² − 1)  ·  θ–β–M weak-branch solver  ·  μ = asin(1/M) The normal-shock relations are the Rankine–Hugoniot jumps for a perfect gas; the oblique solver iterates the θ–β–M relation onto its weak branch and renders the shock rays at exactly the solved β. Stagnation temperature follows T₀ = T·(1 + (γ−1)/2·M²): at the default M = 2.2 in sea-level air, T₀ = 567.1 K and the freestream speed is 748.6 m/s — both exact.

Prandtl–Meyer

ν(M) = √((γ+1)/(γ−1)) · atan( √((γ−1)/(γ+1)·(M²−1)) ) − atan( √(M²−1) ) ν(2.0) = 26.380°, ν(3.0) = 49.757°, ν(4.0) = 65.785° — the published values to the last digit, and the inverse solver returns M = 2.0000 and 3.0001 for ν = 26.38° and 49.76°. The fans are drawn between μ(M₂) and μ(M₃) about the surface angles — the correct construction — with a shoulder turn of 2θ for wedge and airfoil, θ for the rocket.

The nozzle solver

A/A* = (1/M)·[ 2/(γ+1)·(1 + (γ−1)/2·M²) ](γ+1)/(2(γ−1))  ·  F = ṁ·ve + (Pe − Pa)·Ae  ·  Isp = F/(ṁ·g₀) At the defaults — Pc = 3.20 MPa, Ae/A* = 7.5, γ = 1.24, Pa = 101.3 kPa — the solver produces Me = 3.1743, Pe = 53.29 kPa, ṁ = 84.30 kg/s, F = 208.5 kN, Isp = 252.2 s. The audit's independent recomputation reproduced every one of those digits.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The silent +0.012 in drag: the default diamond's CD is 0.0905 on the page and 0.0785 by pure shock–expansion (linear theory: 0.0771) — a difference of exactly +0.012, a hard-coded constant in the axial-force sum, presumably a skin-friction allowance, disclosed nowhere. At +15% on the default it is relatively larger at low-drag settings. The solver it rides on is exact; the addition is the bend.

The airfoil's secret geometry: the airfoil body flies at an effective half-angle of 0.72× the slider's (the rocket at 0.92×, the wedge honestly at 1.00×), and the drawn airfoil is a biconvex lens of max half-thickness 0.44·tanθ — a third thickness again. Its headline numbers are plausible — at the lift preset (M = 2.4, α = 9.5°), CL = 0.3119 against Ackeret's 4α/√(M²−1) = 0.3043 — but the coefficients describe a geometry the slider does not name.

Subsonic and transonic coefficients are heuristic curves: Prandtl–Glauert below M = 0.8, a Gaussian wave-drag spike bell between 0.8 and 1.2 — plausible in shape and magnitude (the transonic preset's CD = 0.162), not computed from the flow, and the page never says so. Worse, the regimes don't meet: at α = 5°, CL = 0.786 just below M = 0.80 but 0.562 at M = 0.80 — a −28% step you can watch the needle take as you sweep Mach through the boundary.

The capsule: its standoff uses Billig's real correlation, Δ/R = 0.143·exp(3.24/M²), giving a plausible Δ = 0.0465 chord at M = 3.8 — but the aero module's nose radius (0.26) differs from the drawn body's (0.276), its CD = 0.82·cpmax = 1.272 is a Newtonian-flavoured invention in the right neighbourhood (Apollo ≈ 1.3), and its CL sign at positive α (Receipt 4) is at best an undocumented trim convention.

Fields and probe are art guided by real values: the sampler starts from the solver's true region values, then blends toward freestream with perpendicular distance — so the schlieren, pressure, Mach, and temperature fields inherit the fade, and the trailing "recompression shock" is drawn at μ(M₃) + 0.14 rad, a Mach-line proxy rather than the θ–β–M angle for the wake turn. Probe numbers far from the body are not the flow.

Smaller print: chamber temperature Tc is hard-coded at 3350 K for every propellant — credible for RP-1/LOX-class, low for LH₂ (~3600 K), and meaningless read as cold gas for the N₂ button (page Isp 205.9 s vs ~75 s for a real cold-gas thruster); only γ and a γ-keyed R change. The γ values themselves are real (air 1.40, CO₂ 1.29, He 1.66) and the shocks follow them correctly. The solver detaches about 0.5% of θmax early — at M = 2, θ = 22.90° is reported detached although the attached solution exists. The altitude readout is a scale-height approximation, −7.2·ln(Pa/101.325): 25 kPa gives 10.08 km against ISA's ≈10.36 km. The tunnel runs at fixed sea-level temperature and makes no altitude claim. The static first-paint placeholders (36.5° / 1.89× / 0.088) disagree with the page's own solver by 1–3% and are replaced on the first frame. And the capsule preset at M = 3.8 is bannered "Re-Entry · Plasma" where T₀ is only 1120 K — artistic licence, named.
