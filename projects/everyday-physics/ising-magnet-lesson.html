Curie Point — the lesson

Curie Point is a magnet you can hold at exactly the temperature where it stops being one. A grid of spins, each one up or down, each one preferring to agree with its four neighbours — and out of that single preference, with no other ingredients, the simulation grows domains, walls, hysteresis, and a phase transition at the most famous number in statistical mechanics: Onsager's Tc = 2.269. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that checked it against the exact solution — including a magnetization curve that tracks Yang's formula to the third decimal, a critical point found where the exact solution says it must be, a hysteresis loop that is real dynamics and not a drawing, and one honest trap the audit fell into so you can watch for it.

Curie Point was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its Monte Carlo engines headlessly.

How to play

There is no score. The exhibit is a laboratory that opens, pointedly, at the most interesting temperature in the room: the simulation boots at T = Tc exactly, with fractal domains already on the lattice. Everything else is a way of pushing the magnet around its phase diagram and watching it answer.

  • Drag the temperature. The T slider runs 0.40 to 4.50, in units where the coupling J = 1 and Boltzmann's constant is 1. Below 2.269 the lattice orders; above it, it forgets. The HUD reads T/Tc, magnetization ⟨M⟩, energy per spin, susceptibility χ, specific heat Cv, and the fraction of domain walls — all computed live from the spins.
  • Apply a field. The h slider (−1.50 to +1.50) is an external magnetic field. Below Tc the magnet answers late and remembers — that memory is hysteresis. The automated Sweep Hysteresis Loop experiment drives h around a full cosine cycle and traces M(h) for you; Sweep Across Curie Point cools from T = 4.0 to 1.40 through the transition, slowing down as it crosses.
  • Flip the coupling. J = +1 is the ferromagnet. J = −1 is the antiferromagnet: neighbours now prefer to disagree, the ordered state is a Néel checkerboard, and the HUD's order parameter quietly switches to the staggered magnetization — watch for it.
  • Change the engine. Metropolis-Hastings flips one spin at a time, the way heat actually jiggles a magnet. The Wolff engine flips whole clusters at once — the standard cure for the critical slowing down you can feel near Tc, where single flips take forever to rearrange the fractal domains. Keyboard W toggles it.
  • Pick a preset. Critical Point (boots at Tc), Quench Coarsen (a sudden drop to T = 1.5, domains growing in real time), Hysteresis Loop (T = 1.65, field sweeping), Antiferromagnet (J = −1), Thermal Noise (T = 4.0 paramagnet), Wolff Clusters (Tc under the cluster engine).
  • Paint. The brush writes spins directly onto the lattice — left button up, right button down. Below Tc a small painted bubble shrinks away under domain-wall tension while a big one grows: nucleation, with a mouse. Lattice sizes 64×64, 128×128 (the default), and 200×200; speeds 0.25× to 8×; Space pauses, C jumps to Tc. The Barkhausen audio, if you enable it, turns flip activity into crackle.

The physics

The simplest magnet that is still a magnet

The Ising model is a deliberate act of throwing things away. A real iron atom has a quantum spin that can point anywhere, sits in a crystal with phonons and conduction electrons, and feels every other atom at once. The model keeps exactly two facts: each site carries a spin s = ±1 — up or down, nothing between — and neighbours prefer to align. The energy of a configuration is:

H = −J Σ⟨i,j⟩ sisj − h Σi si The first sum runs over neighbouring pairs, each counted once: aligned neighbours lower the energy by J, opposed neighbours raise it by J. The second sum is the external field h rewarding spins that point its way. J > 0 makes a ferromagnet; J < 0 makes an antiferromagnet, whose ground state is the checkerboard. That is the entire model — the game integrates nothing else, and everything you see is this formula plus heat.

Heat enters as a lottery. A proposed change that lowers the energy is always accepted; one that raises it by ΔE is accepted with probability e−ΔE/T. That single rule — the Metropolis rule — makes the simulation visit configurations with exactly the Boltzmann probabilities of a real magnet at temperature T. The game is not animating what a magnet looks like; it is sampling what a magnet is, one thermal jiggle at a time.

Why there is a transition at all

Two tendencies fight. Energy wants unanimity: every wall between an up region and a down region costs 2J per unit of length, so the energy minimum is one single domain. Entropy wants variety: there is exactly one all-up configuration, but astronomically many half-and-half ones. At low T, energy wins and the lattice picks a side — spontaneously, since nothing in the rules prefers up over down. That choice, made by the system itself, is spontaneous symmetry breaking, and the magnetization it produces is exact mathematics (Yang, 1952):

m0(T) = (1 − sinh(2J/T)−4)1/8,   T < Tc The spontaneous magnetization of the infinite 2D lattice. The exponent 1/8 — the famous β = 1/8 — means the magnetization dives into Tc with a vertical tangent: it is still 0.91 at T = 2.0, then falls off a cliff. The audit checks the game's curve against this formula point by point (Receipt 3).

At high T entropy wins and the magnetization melts to zero. The border between the two regimes is not gradual — it is a point, and for the square lattice Lars Onsager found it exactly in 1944, in one of the celebrated calculations of twentieth-century physics:

Tc = 2 / ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269185 The condition sinh(2J/Tc) = 1, with J = 1 and kB = 1. The game hard-codes this number — computed in the code as 2.0 / Math.log(1.0 + Math.SQRT2) — and the audit's headline job was to check that the simulation's own transition, the one that emerges from the flipping spins, actually happens there (Receipt 4). A hard-coded label is a claim; a measured peak is a receipt.

Real magnets have Curie points too — iron's is 1043 K — and they are named for the same phenomenon: heat the magnet past its Curie temperature and the permanent magnetization is gone, cool it back and a direction is chosen anew. The game's T is measured in units of J/kB, not kelvin, so 2.269 is a property of the model, not of iron — but the shape of what happens at the point is universal, shared with fluids at their critical point and a dozen other systems. That universality is why this toy deserves its shelf.

Domains, walls, and the memory of iron

Below Tc a real magnet is rarely one domain. It breaks into regions — domains — separated by walls, because walls cost energy per unit length but let the magnet compromise with fields, edges, and its own history. Cool the game from T = 4 and you watch the compromise negotiate itself: tiny domains nucleate, walls straighten to shorten themselves, big domains eat small ones. The Quench preset is that process with the temperature dropped suddenly, and the gold wall overlay traces exactly the unsatisfied bonds the HUD counts.

Hysteresis is domain memory made visible. Sweep the field up from negative saturation and the magnetization lags: walls are pinned, domains of the old direction survive past the field that should have flipped them, and at h = 0 the magnet still holds most of its magnetization — the remanence. Push far enough and it lets go in an avalanche, at the coercive field. Sweep back down and it lags the other way. The loop's width is not a constant of the material alone: sweep faster and the magnet has less time to answer, so the loop widens. The audit measured the game's loop at the game's own sweep rate and at a tenth of it, and the width moved the way real dynamic hysteresis moves (Receipt 5).

Fluctuations are the measurement

The two remaining HUD numbers are not computed from any formula about the average state — they are computed from how much the state wobbles. The susceptibility χ measures how eagerly the magnetization answers a field, and by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem that is the variance of the magnetization itself. The specific heat is the variance of the energy. Near Tc the wobble explodes: domains of every size flicker in and out (the game's "critical opalescence"), χ peaks, and Cv climbs toward the logarithmic divergence Onsager found. When the HUD shows χ spiking as you cross 2.269, you are watching the theorem work, not a scripted banner — the audit verified the formulas the readouts use (Receipt 2).

The math the game runs

Everything is in units J = 1, kB = 1, on a square lattice with periodic boundaries — the right and left edges are neighbours, as are top and bottom, so the magnet has no edges at all. The Metropolis step picks a random site and prices its flip exactly:

ΔE = 2s · (J · Σneighbours s + h) Flipping spin s changes each of its four bond terms and its field term; this is the total, with no approximation. The neighbour sum can only be −4, −2, 0, +2, or +4, so the game precomputes the acceptance probability e−ΔE/T for all five sums and both spin directions into two small tables, rebuilt whenever T, J, or h changes. The audit checked the tables against brute-force energy differences over the full Hamiltonian — see Receipt 1.

One Monte Carlo sweep (1 MCS) is N² flip attempts — one per spin on average. At 1× speed the Metropolis engine runs one sweep per frame (2.5 during a hysteresis sweep); the speed control multiplies that from 0.25× to 8×. The Wolff engine grows a cluster from a random seed, adding each aligned neighbour with probability:

padd = 1 − e−2|J|/T At Tc that is 1 − e−0.881 ≈ 0.586. Clusters grown this way are exactly the correlated domains that single flips cannot rearrange, which is why Wolff defeats critical slowing down at h = 0. With a field on, the game adds the standard correction: the finished cluster flips only with probability min(1, e−2h·S/T), where S is the cluster's net spin — the audit put that correction on trial too (Receipt 6).

The observables are recomputed from the spins every frame: m = Σs/N², energy from the right-and-down bonds (each pair once), domain walls as the fraction of unsatisfied bonds. χ and Cv come from a rolling window of the last 120 frames' m and E values:

χ = (N²/T)(⟨m²⟩ − ⟨|m|⟩²),    Cv = (N²/T²)(⟨e²⟩ − ⟨e⟩²) The ⟨|m|⟩ in χ is the standard finite-lattice estimator: below Tc a finite magnet occasionally flips its whole magnetization, so ⟨m⟩ averages to zero and ⟨|m|⟩ is the honest order parameter. The formulas are exactly right; the 120-frame window they are fed is a caveat, and the honest box owns it.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own functions out of the shipped file — the xorshift32 generator, allocateLattice, rebuildMetropolisTable, stepMetropolis, stepWolff, computeObservables, character for character — and ran them headlessly with fixed seeds. Every number below is measured from the game's code, not quoted from its page.

Receipt 1: the flip price is exact

On a 32×32 lattice, 16,000 random flips across four (J, h, T) combinations were priced two ways: by the game's lookup tables, and by brute force — compute the full Hamiltonian, flip, compute again, subtract. The formula ΔE = 2s(J·Σneighbours + h) matched the brute-force difference on every single flip, and the worst disagreement between the table's acceptance probability and min(1, e−ΔE/T) was 7.8 × 10−16 — machine epsilon. The table is not an approximation of the rule; it is the rule, cached.

Receipt 2: the ground states and the readouts are honest

Start all-up at T = 0.4 and the energy per spin reads −2.00000 — the exact ground state, −2J per spin for four satisfied bonds — and 300 further sweeps move it by nothing. Start in the Néel checkerboard with J = −1 and the same: −2.00000, staggered magnetization exactly 1, net magnetization exactly 0. The energy formula counts each bond once and gets the one number it must get. The χ and Cv readouts implement the fluctuation formulas printed above, including the ⟨|m|⟩ estimator — no hidden scaling.

Receipt 5: the hysteresis loop is dynamics, not drawing

The audit replayed the game's own sweep protocol headlessly — T = 1.65, h driven as −1.2·cos(2πu), 2.5 sweeps per frame over the game's ~14-second loop, starting from downward saturation, exactly as startHysteresisSweep sets it up:

The loop is wide open — at h = 0 the magnet still holds 97% of its magnetization, and it lets go between h = 0.27 and 0.52 on the way up, symmetrically on the way down. Nothing in the code draws that curve; it is the spins lagging the field. And the control proves it is ferromagnetism doing it: the identical sweep at T = 3.00, above Tc, produces no loop at all — m(h = 0) reads ±0.08 of noise on the two branches and the coercive crossing is h ≈ 0.01. Sweep ten times slower at T = 1.65 and the loop narrows, coercive field falling from 0.36 to 0.22: dynamic hysteresis, rate-dependent, exactly as in real iron. The game's own comment in the sweep code says why it forces Metropolis for this experiment — "Hysteresis requires Metropolis local barrier dynamics" — and the audit confirms the reasoning: a cluster engine would erase the barriers that make the loop.

Receipt 6: two engines, one magnet — and a false alarm, owned

At h = 0 the engines agree wherever both are properly equilibrated: at T = 3.0, ⟨|m|⟩ = 0.045 (Metropolis) vs 0.040 (Wolff) and ⟨E⟩/spin −0.818 vs −0.820. In a field, the audit's first runs disagreed — Wolff read ⟨m⟩ = 0.71 where Metropolis read 0.94 at T = 2.0, h = 0.15 — and the game's Wolff field correction went on trial. The arbitration, on 64×64 with 60,000 cluster moves: Wolff from an ordered start 0.9443, Wolff from a random start 0.9384, Metropolis from up and from down 0.9423 / 0.9420. The correction is right; the audit's first Wolff runs were simply under-equilibrated — in a field, most large cluster flips against h are rejected, so a random start crawls, and 8,000 clusters on 128×128 was not enough. The lesson records the false alarm because it is the most useful number in this section: it is a measured demonstration of how slowly Wolff-in-a-field converges, in the game's own engine.

Receipt 7: the antiferromagnet is the same law with the sign flipped

With J = −1 at the preset's T = 1.55, the staggered magnetization settles at 0.9832 against the exact value 0.9835 (the same Yang formula with |J|), net magnetization −0.0015, energy −1.9395 per spin. At T = 2.60 — above the Néel point, which for the square lattice is the same 2.269 — the staggered order collapses to noise. The Wolff growth rule is honestly generalized too: a bond counts as satisfied when J·si·sj > 0, so clusters grow along the checkerboard's own alignment, not the ferromagnet's.

Receipt 8: the trap — critical slowing down, measured on the auditor

One audit run deserves its own paragraph. From a random start at T = 2.0 on 64×64, Metropolis reported ⟨|m|⟩ = 0.390 after 4,500 sweeps — against the true 0.911 that an ordered start gives immediately. The lattice had frozen into two stripe domains whose walls, on a periodic lattice, have nowhere to go; single spin flips anneal them away only over geological time. This is critical slowing down and metastability, the genuine diseases of local Monte Carlo, and the game is built knowing it: the Critical preset pre-warms the lattice with 14 Wolff clusters before handing it to you, the Curie sweep cools slowly rather than quenching, and the Wolff engine exists precisely for this. But the trap is still armed for a player who drags the temperature into the ordered phase from a disordered screen and trusts the HUD a minute later — the numbers will be honest averages of a magnet that has not yet decided what it is.

How it's built — the coding choices

Tables where tables are exact

Two precomputations carry the speed. The neighbour indices for every site are built once into four arrays (up, down, left, right) with the wrap-around baked in, so the hot loop never does modular arithmetic. And because a spin's neighbour sum has only five possible values, the Metropolis acceptance probabilities for both spin directions live in two five-entry tables, rebuilt only when T, J, or h changes. A sweep at 128×128 is then 16,384 array lookups and random numbers — the audit's headless run sustained about 800 sweeps per second in Node. The random numbers come from a small xorshift32 generator seeded from the clock, chosen for speed in the tight loop; for sampling Boltzmann weights it is entirely adequate.

Clusters grown with a stamp, not a cleanup

The Wolff implementation grows clusters on an explicit stack and marks membership with a monotonically increasing stamp in a visited array — no clearing pass between clusters, the array is reset only when the stamp approaches 232. The field correction is applied once per finished cluster from its accumulated net spin. The antiferromagnetic case is handled inside the growth rule itself (the target neighbour spin flips sign with J), so one code path serves both couplings.

Presets are launch conditions, and the sweeps are honest about their needs

Each preset is just a choice of (T, h, J, engine, starting pattern) — the Critical preset's only sleight is its 14-cluster Wolff warm-up, openly commented in the code ("so fractal domains appear immediately"). The hysteresis sweep is stricter: it forces J = +1, forces Metropolis, forces T down to 1.65 if you were above 2.05, pre-saturates the lattice downward, and drives h on a cosine so the field lingers at the extremes and moves fastest through zero — a considered piece of experimental design, and the audit's Receipt 5 is its vindication. The file is fully self-contained: no external references, no network, no localStorage — the magnet and its whole laboratory are in the one file.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The transition on screen is a peak, not a point: everything exact in this lesson — 2.269185, m0(T), β = 1/8 — is a property of the infinite lattice. The game's lattice is finite, so its susceptibility peak sits ~0.02 above Tc at 128×128 (more at 64×64), its ⟨|m|⟩ at Tc reads 0.62 rather than 0, and its specific-heat peak is a rounded hill where the true Cv diverges logarithmically. The "Critical Point!" banner fires on proximity to the hard-coded number, not on any measured property of the spins. None of this is a defect — it is finite-size physics, and it is the same rounding a real Monte Carlo paper would report — but the sharp number on the slider is Onsager's, while the soft transition on the lattice is the game's own.

χ and Cv are window estimates, not converged measurements: the readouts average the last 120 frames — 120 consecutive sweeps at 1× — and near Tc successive sweeps are strongly correlated (that is what critical slowing down means), so the effective sample count is far smaller than 120 and the displayed peaks jump around and lag behind the temperature slider. The audit's own peak-finding used samples spaced four sweeps apart over 2,600 sweeps and still carries visible noise (its χ peak height, ~200, is best read as a lower bound). Treat the HUD's χ and Cv as instruments with a fast needle, not as data.

Wolff in a field is correct but slow: the acceptance correction rejects most large-cluster flips that oppose h, so with the field on, the cluster engine equilibrates far more slowly than its h = 0 reputation suggests — the audit needed roughly ten times its first budget of cluster moves to agree with Metropolis (Receipt 6). A player who switches to Wolff with a field applied and reads the HUD immediately is reading a magnet still on its way.

The hysteresis loop's width belongs to the sweep, not just the magnet: the loop is drawn at the sweep rate set by the Sweep Rate control (~14 s per loop at 1×, with 2.5 Metropolis sweeps per frame — brisker equilibration than the free-running 1 sweep per frame). Measured coercive fields: 0.36 at the game's rate, 0.22 at a tenth of it. That rate-dependence is real physics, but it means the loop on the plot is a property of (T, rate) together; there is no single "the coercive field" in this exhibit, and a real ferromagnet's quasistatic loop would be narrower still.

It is a 2D magnet: the exactness on offer — Onsager, β = 1/8, the logarithmic Cv — belongs to two dimensions. Bulk iron is three-dimensional, with β ≈ 0.325 and a finite Cv cusp; a one-dimensional Ising chain has no transition at all. The model also keeps only nearest neighbours, one coupling strength, no anisotropy, no dipolar fields, no defects, and spins with two states instead of quantum vectors. T is in units of J/kB: no kelvin appears anywhere in the simulation, and iron's 1043 K Curie point is a caption about a different (3D, multi-coupling) system that happens to share the phenomenon.

Small print: the temperature slider bottoms out at 0.40, so a true T → 0 quench is off the menu. Under Wolff, the MCS counter advances by flipped spins per lattice — a sweep-equivalent bookkeeping, not a count of cluster moves. The Curie sweep deliberately slows near Tc (a 0.52 rate factor within ±0.35) — pacing, not physics. The brush writes spins directly, an external intervention no thermal process performs. The Barkhausen crackle is a sonification: click rates driven by flip statistics, not one click per physical Barkhausen jump, and not simulated acoustics. The page's "60 fps" for the 200×200 lattice is a performance claim the headless audit cannot check — at 40,000 flip attempts per frame it will depend on the machine. And the static HTML pre-prints "−1.414" as the energy readout before the script starts — a placeholder that happens, by builder's luck or builder's wit, to be the exact energy per spin at Tc; the live value replaces it immediately.
