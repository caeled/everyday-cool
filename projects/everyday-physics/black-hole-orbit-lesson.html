Event Horizon — the lesson

Event Horizon is the most extreme room in Everyday Physics: a spacecraft in orbit around a black hole, with a thruster on your back and a clock that runs slow. It is the only exhibit in the collection where the gravity itself is relativistic — not a Newtonian field with a black-hole picture painted on it, but the real Schwarzschild orbit equation, running live. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that checked it against real general relativity — including the proof that its precession is not the textbook approximation but the exact value, a proof that its ISCO is a real knife edge, and a proof that its ship's clock reads the exact right time on a circular orbit and then goes generous on the way in.

Event Horizon was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

How to play

There is no score, no finish line, and no win screen. Event Horizon is a laboratory, and the outcomes are trajectories. The status pill in the top-left tells you which one you are on: Bound Orbit, Inside ISCO!, Plunge Trajectory, Escape Trajectory, or Signal Lost when the ship crosses the horizon or hits something. Everything else on the page is a tool for exploring what orbits can do near a hole.

  • Choose a scene. Six presets: GR Rosette (a precessing orbit that dives inside the ISCO), Neutron Flyby (a passing neutron star perturbs a circular orbit), Zoom-Whirl (an orbit that whirls around the photon sphere), Ion Spiral Out (continuous low thrust climbing away from just above the ISCO), Binary Chaos (a companion star makes a three-body system), and Tidal Shred (passing bodies torn apart near the hole).
  • Fly the ship. W/S thrust prograde/retrograde, A/D radial in/out, Space pauses. The drawer offers the same directions plus a manual angle, a thrust slider, Hold to Fire, Continuous, an Impulse Δv Kick, and Circularize. Finite Fuel (Rocket Equation) is off by default — turn it on and the tanks empty.
  • Set the launch. Start radius (2.4–16 rs), start speed as a fraction of circular speed, and launch pitch. Relaunch Craft with Params drops you in on those terms.
  • Throw things at it. Spawn a neutron star, star, planet, comet, or mini black hole with a chosen mass, speed, and impact parameter — or click and drag in space to slingshot-launch one by hand. Add Companion puts one into a circular orbit around the hole. Tidal disruption can be toggled on or off.
  • Change the hole. Black-hole mass from 0.4× to 2.4×, and the gravity model itself: Schwarzschild GR or plain Newtonian 1/r². Switching models is the best experiment in the room — everything that vanishes when you switch is a general-relativity effect.
  • Read the telemetry. Distance in Schwarzschild radii, local speed in units of c, time rate dτ/dt, predicted periapsis and apoapsis, propellant, ship time τ versus distant time t and the drift between them. The dotted prediction line is the orbit the ship is on right now.

The ship dies two ways: crossing the horizon (r ≤ 1.01 rs) or colliding with a passing body. A passing body dies three ways: horizon capture (r ≤ 1.03 rs), tidal shredding inside its Roche limit, or escaping beyond 3,600 world units.

The physics

Three radii that run the room

A black hole has no surface, but it has three distances that decide everything that can happen near it — and the game draws all three as rings on the canvas:

  • The Schwarzschild radius, rs = 2GM/c² — the event horizon. Nothing inside it, not even light, comes back out. For a hole of one solar mass it is about 3 km; for the 4.3-million-solar-mass hole at the centre of our galaxy it is about 12.7 million km. The game measures every distance in units of this radius.
  • The photon sphere, 1.5 rs — the only place light itself can orbit, in an unstable circle. A light ray there stays only until the tiniest nudge sends it spiralling in or escaping. Anything made of matter needs a speed above c to circle there, so nothing does — it is a boundary, not a parking spot.
  • The ISCO, 3 rs — the innermost stable circular orbit. Outside it, circular orbits are bowls: nudge the ship and it oscillates around the circle and stays. Inside it they are ridges: nudge the ship and it rolls off, inward, to the horizon. A real accretion disk ends at the ISCO; inside it, gas stops circling and plunges.

Those three numbers — 1, 1.5, and 3 — are the whole map of the room. Every preset in the game is a tour of one of them.

Orbit is falling and missing — and the ellipse refuses to close

Newton said an orbit is a falling object that keeps missing the ground, and that the path closes: the same ellipse, forever, periapsis at the same place in the sky every time around. Einstein keeps the falling and breaks the closing. Near a massive body, the ellipse itself rotates — each periapsis arrives a little further around the circle than the last. That is apsidal precession, and it is how Einstein explained Mercury's orbit in 1915: Mercury's perihelion advances 43 arc-seconds per century more than Newton predicts, and general relativity produces exactly that extra amount. The weak-field formula is:

Δφ = 6πGM / (c²a(1 − e²)) The advance per orbit, in radians. a is the semi-major axis, e the eccentricity. For Mercury the number is a hair — 43″ per century. For the rosette in this game it is more than a third of a full circle per orbit, because the orbit dives to 2.85 rs, where the weak-field formula stops being the right tool. The audit checks the game's number against the exact equation, not this approximation — see Receipt 1.

The effective potential — why orbits have a last circle

The cleanest way to see the ISCO is to draw the energy landscape a particle moves in. In Newton, the effective potential is the gravity well plus a centrifugal wall:

Veff = −GM/r + h²/(2r²) Newton: a well with a wall. A particle with energy below the rim sits in the bowl and orbits; the bowl has a bottom at every radius, so a circular orbit exists everywhere.

General relativity adds one term, and it changes the shape of the whole landscape:

Veff = −GM/r + h²/(2r²) − GMh²/(c²r³) The third term is the relativistic correction. At large r it is nothing; at small r it dives to −∞ and overwhelms the centrifugal wall. The bowl develops a second hump, and below a critical angular momentum the bowl has no bottom at all — no circular orbit exists. The last angular momentum that still has a bottom is the ISCO, at 3 rs. That same −1/r³ term is the force term the game actually integrates.

Time runs slow — two ways at once

A clock near a black hole ticks slower than a clock far away, for two separate reasons that multiply together. Standing still at radius r, your clock is slowed by the depth of the well: √(1 − rs/r). Moving, it is slowed again by its own speed, the familiar special relativity: √(1 − v²/c²). For a circular orbit the two combine to the exact result:

dτ/dt = √(1 − 3rs/(2r)) Ship proper time τ per unit of distant coordinate time t, on a circular geodesic. At 5 rs that is √0.7 = 0.837 — the ship's clock loses about a sixth of every second. At the ISCO, 3 rs, it is √0.5 = 0.707. At the photon sphere it would be zero — but no massive ship can circle there to find out. The game's telemetry shows exactly this number, and the audit checks it to five decimal places.

There is also the famous twin version of the story: the ship that falls in and the observer who stays far away disagree about what "now" means. The distant observer never sees the falling ship cross the horizon — its light is redshifted and frozen, piling up at the edge. The ship itself crosses in finite proper time. The game's banner gets the poetry right: "Event Horizon crossed — spacecraft signal redshifted & lost." The telemetry's Δt Drift column is the two clocks diverging in real time.

Tides: stretched by the difference, not by the pull

A black hole does not shred a star by pulling hard. It shreds it by pulling unevenly: the near side is pulled harder than the far side, and the difference — the tidal force — grows as 1/r³. When that difference exceeds the body's own gravity holding it together, the body comes apart. The distance where that happens is the Roche limit, and for a star falling into a black hole it is roughly the tidal radius:

rt ≈ R★ · (MBH / M★)1/3 The star's own radius, scaled by the cube root of the mass ratio. A real Roche limit depends on the densities of both bodies — a fluffy star shreds far out, a dense neutron star almost never. The game's version is a per-species constant in units of rs, which is the first thing the honest box has to say about it.

The real events are now observed: when a star wanders too close to a supermassive black hole, about half its debris falls back and lights up as a tidal disruption event, a flare that can outshine the whole galaxy for months. The game's Tidal Shred preset is a toy version of exactly that, with the star turning into a glowing stream instead of a point.

Light bends, and the disk is a lie your eyes will believe

Near a black hole, light does not travel in straight lines. A star behind the hole is lensed: its light bends around and arrives from a shifted direction, and a second, inverted image appears on the near side of the Einstein ring. The game's lensing uses the real point-mass lens equation for both images — the positions are genuine, only the Einstein radius is chosen for looks. The accretion disk is drawn with the same care for shape: its particles run at Keplerian angular speed, ω ∝ r−3/2, so the inner edge laps the outer edge, and the side coming toward you is Doppler-brightened. A real disk would be hotter and bluer inside, thinner, and it would not circle neatly below the ISCO — but as a picture of what a disk looks like in motion, the shape is right.

The math the game runs

The game works in geometric-ish units chosen for the screen: G = 1, c = 24, black-hole mass M₀ = 10,944, so that rs = 2GM/c² = 38.0 world units, the ISCO sits at 114, and the photon sphere at 57. One unit of speed is 1/24 of light speed, and the whole simulation runs at 10.5 simulated seconds per real second at 1× speed. The central acceleration is the Newtonian term plus exactly the term the Tab-3 note advertises:

a = −GM/r² − 3GMh²/(c²r⁴) Radial, inward. h is the specific angular momentum, r²φ̇. The second term is the exact Schwarzschild orbit-equation term: with it, the orbit shapes the game draws are the exact shapes of Schwarzschild geodesics — same differential equation, same solutions. This is why the audit can check precession against the exact equation and expect agreement, not just approximation.

The circular speed the game launches with is the one this force law implies:

vcirc = √(GM / (r − 1.5 rs)) Not the Newtonian √(GM/r), and not the textbook shell-observer speed √(GM/(r − rs)) either — this is the speed that closes a circle under the game's own force law. It is exact for that law: at 5 rs it is 9.071 units/s (0.378c as a coordinate speed), at the ISCO 13.856 (0.577c), and it reaches exactly c at 2 rs — which is why nothing circles below that in this parametrisation, and why the code clamps the denominator at 0.15 rs rather than letting it go to zero. See the honest box.

Time is advanced by a genuine RK4 step — four acceleration evaluations per step, coupled across the craft and every passing body, which also pull on each other. The substep count adapts to the closest approach: 6 per frame in open space, 14 inside 4.5 rs, 28 inside 2.6 rs. The frame's simulated interval is dtreal × 10.5 × warp, so a 60 fps frame at 1× is 0.175 simulated seconds split into those substeps. The time dilation the telemetry shows is stitched from the gravitational factor and the speed, in a way that is exact for circular orbits (the audit proves it) and approximate elsewhere:

dτ/dt = √((1 − rs/r) · (1 − β²)),   β = (u/c) / √(1 + (u/c)²) u is the ship's coordinate speed. The β conversion treats u as a proper velocity (celerity), γv — so the displayed speed never exceeds c even when the coordinate speed does, and for a circular orbit the whole expression collapses to exactly √(1 − 3rs/(2r)). For a plunge it does not, and the audit measures by how much.

Fuel, when the Finite Fuel toggle is on, follows a mass-ratio law. The craft's total mass is normalised to 1 — dry mass 0.35, fuel 0.65 — thrust acceleration is divided by the current mass, and fuel drains at a rate proportional to the throttle setting. That structure is the rocket equation in disguise, and the audit prices it exactly.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own physics functions out of the shipped file — same constants, same acceleration function, same RK4 step, same win and death checks — and ran them headlessly. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1: precession is exact GR — not the textbook approximation

Two orbits were flown through the game's own step and their periapsis advance measured, then checked against two independent standards: the weak-field formula 6πGM/(c²a(1−e²)), and a separate integration of the exact Schwarzschild orbit equation u″ + u = GM/h² + 3GMu²/c² with the same launch conditions:

The game's advance matches the exact equation to the printed digit in both regimes — the 0.04° residue in Newton mode is numerical noise, and it is the control that proves the 49.40° is physics, not integration error. The weak-field formula, the one in most textbooks, is the number that is wrong here: it underestimates the weak orbit by 21% and the rosette by 61%, because at these field strengths the higher-order terms are not small. The rosette's periapsis lands exactly where the preset's own comment says it should — 2.850 rs — and the Zoom-Whirl's too: 2.460 rs against the comment's "~2.46". A rosette whose periapsis advances 323.7° per orbit sweeps 683.7° of angle for every radial lap — nearly two full revolutions per orbit — which is what "zoom-whirl" means.

Receipt 2: the circles hold, and the energy does not leak

Circular launches at 4, 5, 6, 8, and 10 rs were flown for ten periods each through the game's own step. The radial wobble was below a ten-thousandth of a percent of the radius in every case, and a circular launch at 6 rs flown for 200 periods showed an effective-energy drift of −2.0 × 10−11% and an angular-momentum drift of −8.1 × 10−12% — RK4 doing its job. In Newton mode the same run drifts −8.1 × 10−12% in energy, and a circular orbit at 2 rs — inside the GR ISCO — holds for 20 periods, which is the control that proves the ISCO comes from the relativistic term and nothing else.

One honest wrinkle in the same receipt: the circles hold, but they run fast. The game's orbital periods are shorter than Schwarzschild coordinate periods by the factor √(r/(r − 1.5 rs)) — 26% fast at 4 rs (measured period 89.1 s against the coordinate 112.6 s), 19% fast at 5 rs (131.8 vs 157.3 s), 11% at 8 rs, 8% at 10 rs. The orbit shapes are exact Schwarzschild geodesics; the clock that parametrises them is the sim's own. See the honest box.

The Δv is identical at both throttle settings, which is the signature of a real rocket equation: the throttle changes when the Δv arrives, not how much there is. The logarithmic shape is exact — Δv = ve·ln(m0/mf) with mass ratio 1/0.35. But the number hiding inside it is not a chemical rocket: the implied exhaust velocity is 16.30 world units/s — 0.679 c. No chemical, nuclear, or ion engine exhausts at two thirds of light speed; the only real engine in that class is a photon drive. The "Rocket Equation" label is honest about the shape and silent about the substance. Two smaller prices, both measured: the Impulse Δv Kick costs 2.70% fuel for a kick that continuous burning would sell for 1.94% — a 1.39× surcharge for the convenience — and Circularize costs nothing at all (see the honest box).

The shredding is a stress meter, not a force comparison: while a fragile body sits inside its Roche limit, stress grows at (Roche/r) × 1.15 per second, and at 3.8 the body bursts into a stream. The 2.8 s in the first test is exactly what that rate predicts. Note the asymmetry the table shows: the ship can fly the same fatal depths as a shredding star and feel nothing — the game models tides for the scenery, not for you.

Receipt 8: the prediction line tells the truth — and the bodies pull each other

The dotted predicted trajectory is computed by re-stepping the craft (RK4) and the passing bodies (a cheaper Euler–Cromer step) 380 steps ahead. On the rosette preset, the periapsis it predicts — 2.850 rs — is exactly the periapsis the ship then flies, 2.850 rs. And the passing bodies are a genuine coupled system, not wallpaper: a companion star flown for 300 s with and without a second body present ends up 66.3 world units apart — the bodies genuinely perturb each other. One thing they do not do is move the hole: the black hole is fixed at the origin, and a body it accretes vanishes in a flare without changing its mass.

How it's built — the coding choices

One acceleration function as the source of truth

Everything in the game — the live ship, the passing bodies, the prediction line, the launch presets — flows through a single computeGravityAccel: central Newtonian term, the −3GMh²/(c²r⁴) relativistic term in GR mode, and the passing bodies' pull with a softening (soft = max(0.75 × radius, 4)). A secondary black hole even carries its own local GR term. The ship is a massless test particle: it feels everything and pushes nothing. That one-function discipline is why the audit could lift the physics out and run it headlessly, and why the prediction line agrees with the flown orbit to the printed digit.

The presets are honest orbits, not animations

Each preset is just a launch condition and, where needed, a body: the rosette is 8.4 rs at 0.82× circular speed; the zoom-whirl is 10.5 rs at 0.752×; the ion spiral is a circular launch at 3.6 rs with 0.25 g0 of continuous prograde thrust; the binary is a 26%-mass star on a circular orbit at 12.2 rs. Nothing is scripted after the start — the rosette precesses because the force law says so, not because an animation says so, which is why the audit's independent integration of the orbit equation reproduces it exactly.

Lensing and the disk are the real formulas, dressed for the screen

The star lensing solves the actual point-mass lens equation for both images — primary position ½(d + √(d² + 4θE²)) and the inverted secondary — with an Einstein radius chosen as θE = 1.95 rs on screen. The accretion disk's 420 particles advance at ω = 1.65/rNorm3/2, the Keplerian r−3/2 shape, with a Doppler-brightened approaching side. The warped grid is an inward pull formula, not a true embedding diagram. The file is fully self-contained: no external references, no localStorage, no network — everything you see is in the one file.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The orbit shapes are exact; the clock that parametrises them is the sim's own: the game integrates the exact Schwarzschild orbit equation, so precession and turning points match the exact theory to the printed digit — but its orbital periods are shorter than Schwarzschild coordinate periods by √(r/(r − 1.5 rs)): 26% fast at 4 rs, 19% at 5, 11% at 8, 8% at 10. The dτ/dt readout is exact for circular orbits anyway, because the stitching was built to make it so. A player timing an orbit against a real ephemeris would find the game's years short.

Speeds are stitched, and the stitch has a clamp: the coordinate speed of a circular orbit reaches exactly c at 2 rs and would exceed it below that, so the circular-speed formula's denominator is floored at 0.15 rs — below 1.65 rs the "circular speed" is a guarded number, not a derived one, and the displayed local speed there (0.877c at 1.6 rs) is the clamp's child, not the shell-observer value (0.913c). Outside that zone the displayed speed is exactly the shell-observer speed.

The clock goes generous on a plunge: dτ/dt is exact for circular orbits and increasingly generous for radial motion — at 3 rs on the way in it reads 0.729 where the metric says 0.535. The ship-time/distant-time drift column is the stitching's verdict, not the metric's, once the orbit stops being circular.

The rocket is a photon drive in a g0 costume: the fuel law has the exact Tsiolkovsky shape, but its implied exhaust velocity is 0.679c — no real engine. The "g0" on the thrust slider is a unit of 0.22 world units/s², not Earth gravity at any particular place. The Impulse Kick costs 1.39× the fuel of the same Δv burned continuously. And Circularize is free: it rewrites the velocity to circular instantly, with no fuel cost even in finite-fuel mode — the strongest engine in the game costs nothing. In GR mode it refuses below 1.65 rs while its message says "inside the Photon Sphere (1.5 rs)"; between 1.65 and 3 rs it will happily circularise an orbit that cannot hold, with no warning about the ISCO.

Tides are a stress timer, not a force balance: each species has a fixed Roche multiple of rs (star 2.55, planet 2.85, comet 3.1, neutron star 1.55), independent of the body's mass slider — a real Roche limit depends on the densities of both bodies, and the same star would shred at a different radius around a different-mass hole. Stress accumulates at (Roche/r) × 1.15 per second and shreds at 3.8, with no healing term: a dip that does not shred still leaves its stress on the books. And the craft is exempt entirely — it can fly the depths that shred a star and feel nothing.

The hole is nailed down and never gains weight: the black hole sits fixed at the origin and its mass never changes, even when it accretes a body up to 45% of its own mass — in reality both would swing around their barycentre. Passing bodies do pull on each other (measured), with softened gravity; the ship pushes nothing. An accreted body is a flare, not a meal.

The periapsis/apoapsis cards are predictions, not measurements: they come from a 380-step preview (craft RK4, bodies cheaper Euler–Cromer), covering roughly 53–236 simulated seconds ahead, and "Plunge"/"Escape" are the preview's verdicts — impact predicted at 1.02 rs against an actual death at 1.01 rs.

Warp changes accuracy, not just speed: substeps are chosen by radius only (6/14/28), so at 4× warp each step is four times coarser. Energy drift over 30 orbits at 3.2 rs grows from −1.7 × 10−12% at 1× to −4.2 × 10−9% at 4× — still negligible, but the sim is not warp-invariant.

Newton mode keeps the horizon: switching to Newtonian gravity removes the ISCO and the precession, but the ship still dies at 1.01 rs and the clock still dilates (the pure special-relativity factor). The toggle changes gravity, not the rules of the world — a Newtonian hole with an event horizon is a hybrid the page never quite says it is.

Small print: "Ion Spiral Out" is the same thruster at 0.25 g0 continuous — no separate ion physics. The disk's inner edge at 1.52 rs circles neatly where real gas would be plunging. The lensing's Einstein radius, 1.95 rs, is chosen for looks. The grid is a visual pull, not an embedding diagram. The start-radius slider bottoms out at 2.4 rs — inside the ISCO — so a full-speed launch from the bottom of the slider is a plunge with a view. And there is no win condition to beat: the only scores are the ones you choose to measure.
