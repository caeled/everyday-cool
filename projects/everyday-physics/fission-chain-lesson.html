Everyday Physics · The back door to Criticality

Criticality — the lesson

Criticality is a working nuclear reactor in one screen: 234 fuel nuclei in a lattice, neutrons flying between them, boron control rods you can drag, a moderator you can thicken or boil away, and a single number — keff — that decides whether the chain reaction dies, holds, or runs. It is the most ambitious simulation in Everyday Physics, and it rewards a hard audit, because its central readout turns out to be three different numbers wearing one label. This page is the other half of the game: the reactor physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that ran its own neutron ledger against it — including the proof that its neutrons-per-fission is exact, that its control rods are real geometry, that its flagship "steady-state critical" core is actually kept alive by its startup source, and that its meltdown clock is, underneath, a fission counter.

Criticality was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

How to play

There is no score. The outcomes are reactor states: a chain that dies, a chain that holds, a core that melts. The status pill in the top-left names the state you are in — Subcritical, Critical, Supercritical, Prompt Critical, Overheating, SCRAMMED, or Meltdown — and the telemetry beside it shows keff, the live neutron count (fast/thermal), thermal power, core temperature, the fissile fraction, and the Xe-135 poison count.

  • Pick a scenario. Six presets: Steady-State Critical (the default "PWR Thermal Core"), Prompt Supercritical (an uncontrolled transient), Subcritical Decay, Weapons-Grade HEU (94% enrichment, almost no moderator — a fast-spectrum core), Breeder Core (U-238 captures breeding Pu-239), and Xenon Poisoning (the core starts pre-loaded with Xe-135).
  • Work the rods. The Control Rod Insertion slider, the ↑/↓ arrow keys, or drag the rods themselves. X or the SCRAM button drops all five B₄C banks to 100% at emergency speed. Pull the rods back out to clear a SCRAM.
  • Fire neutrons. The burst button injects a ring of neutrons mid-core; click and hold anywhere in the vessel to stream neutrons in at that point.
  • Tune the core. Sliders for U-235 enrichment (5–96%), moderator density, primary cooling rate, and vessel reflector efficiency. The enrichment slider re-deals the lattice live — watch the fissile percentage move.
  • Switch the physics. Toggles for the Cf-252 auto-source, the boiling void coefficient, Xe-135 poison buildup, continuous refuelling, and delayed-neutron precursors. Turning the auto-source off is the single most instructive click in the room: it shows you what the chain reaction can do unaided.
  • Read the ledger. The Core & Fuel tab keeps a live isotope inventory — U-235, U-238, Pu-239, Xe-135, excited nuclei, depleted slots — plus running counters for total fissions and bred plutonium. The sparkline plots flux and power together.

The core melts at 1,100 °C: the lattice slumps into a corium pool at the bottom of the vessel and the run is over until you reload. Everything else — every temperature below that, every k above or below 1 — is yours to explore.

The physics

One number runs the reactor: keff

A fission splits a heavy nucleus and throws out, on average, between two and three fresh neutrons. If exactly one of those neutrons, on average, goes on to cause the next fission, the chain holds steady. That average is the effective multiplication factor:

keff = neutrons born in one generation ÷ neutrons lost from the last k < 1 is subcritical: each generation is smaller and the chain dies. k = 1 is critical: the chain sustains itself at constant power. k > 1 is supercritical: the population grows exponentially, at a rate set by how far above 1 and how long a generation takes. Every control in the game — rods, moderator, enrichment, reflector — is a lever on this one ratio, because each one changes either the births or the losses.

The losses are as important as the births, and the game models all of them: a neutron can be swallowed by a control rod, leak out of the vessel, be captured by U-238 or by the Xe-135 poison, or — the productive death — be absorbed by a fissile nucleus and cause a fission. The audit's ledger for the default core (Receipt 1) shows the four sinks running at comparable rates, which is exactly the balancing act a real reactor operator manages.

Why slow neutrons rule: cross-sections

A neutron does not fission every nucleus it touches. Each interaction has a probability, expressed as a cross-section in barns — an effective target area. For U-235 the numbers are extreme: a thermal neutron (0.025 eV, ambling at 2,200 m/s) sees a fission cross-section of about 585 barns; a fast fission-born neutron (~2 MeV) sees only about 1.2 barns. Slowing a neutron down multiplies its chance of splitting U-235 roughly five-hundred-fold. That single fact is why most reactors carry a moderator — water or graphite whose light nuclei bleed speed off the neutrons in elastic collisions (about 18 collisions in hydrogen to go from 2 MeV to thermal) without (mostly) swallowing them. It is also why the game's moderator slider is a reactivity control: no moderator, and a low-enriched core simply cannot sustain a chain, because its neutrons stay fast and mostly leak or get captured.

The same table explains the poisons and the breeding. U-238 barely fissions at any energy, but it captures — preferentially at epithermal resonance energies — and the capture product decays into fissile Pu-239. Xe-135, a fission product, has the largest thermal absorption cross-section of any known nucleus, about 2.6 million barns: a thermal neutron that wanders near it is simply gone. And boron-10, the working ingredient of the control rods, absorbs thermal neutrons at 3,837 barns — which is why sliding a few boron blades into the core can shut the whole reaction down.

Delayed neutrons: the only reason reactors are controllable

About 99.35% of U-235's fission neutrons are prompt — born within a trillionth of a second of the split. The remaining β ≈ 0.65% are delayed: they arrive seconds later, boiled off by radioactive fission fragments (the "precursors") with half-lives up to about 56 seconds. That small delayed tail stretches the reactor's effective response time from microseconds to tenths of a second — slow enough for machinery and humans. Cross one specific line, k = 1/(1 − β) ≈ 1.0065, and the chain no longer needs the delayed neutrons to sustain itself: that is prompt criticality, where the population can e-fold in microseconds and no control system on Earth can follow it. Every reactor excursion that ever hurt anyone went through that door. The game's runaway preset is built to take you there — and, tellingly, it ships with the delayed-neutron toggle off.

Breeding and the iodine pit

Two slow chemistries run underneath the fast neutron economy. Breeding: a U-238 nucleus that captures a neutron becomes U-239, which beta-decays (23.5-minute half-life) to Np-239, which decays again (2.36 days) to Pu-239 — a fissile isotope with an even richer neutron yield than U-235 (ν̄ ≈ 2.88 against 2.43). A core with fertile U-238 is quietly manufacturing a second fuel inside itself. Poisoning: fission also produces I-135 (6.6-hour half-life), which decays into Xe-135 (9.2-hour half-life) — the 2.6-million-barn absorber above. After a power change, xenon first grows (the iodine already in the core keeps decaying into it) before it burns away in the neutron flux: the "iodine pit" that can choke a restarted reactor for a day, and a contributing actor at Chernobyl. The game's xenon preset starts you inside the pit.

What a fission is worth

Each U-235 fission releases about 200 MeV of energy — roughly 3.2 × 10−11 joules, most of it (≈170 MeV) as the kinetic energy of the two recoiling fragments. The game's named split is a real channel, and it balances: Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3 neutrons = 236 nucleons = the 235 of U-235 plus the neutron that arrived. Per gram, fission fuel carries about three million times the energy of coal — which is why the power numbers on real reactors are so large: a 3 GW thermal plant runs about 1020 fissions every second. Keep that scale in mind when the game's power meter reads megawatts for a handful of fissions a second; the honest box prices the difference.

The math the game runs

The game is a particle simulation, not a formula with animations. Its 234 lattice nuclei (18 × 13, in six bundles of three columns, with five control-rod channels between them) hold isotope states; its neutrons are individual particles with a position, a velocity, and an abstract energy (1.0 = fast, ≤ 0.28 = thermal). On every physics step — nominally 60 a second — each neutron is slowed by the moderator, tested against the rods, the walls, and every nucleus it overlaps, with per-step dice rolled against this table:

EncounterThermal neutronFast neutron
Fission on U-2350.840.16

Absorption inside a rod channel0.960.62

Thermal neutrons also get a bigger target: the hit radius is 15.2 world units against 11.4 for fast. A fission rolls the neutron yield — 2 or 3 prompt neutrons (3 with probability 0.44 for uranium, 0.86 for plutonium), emitted fast — and, with the delayed toggle on, an 8.5% chance of queueing one delayed neutron to appear, thermal, 0.75–2.1 s later. The moderator is a continuous drain: energy falls at 2.15 × (effective moderator density) per second, and the neutron's speed is continuously re-matched to its energy between the fast speed (365 units/s) and the thermal speed (152).

And then there is the number on the badge. The displayed keff is a blend of two different quantities:

kdisplay ← smooth( w · kempirical + (1 − w) · kanalytic ), w = min(0.76, deaths in window ÷ 28) kempirical is the game's own event ledger — neutrons born at fission divided by neutrons lost, counted over a sliding 2.6-second window. kanalytic is a closed-form estimate from the core's composition: (thermalWeight · ffissile · 3.65 + (1 − thermalWeight) · ffissile · 1.72) × poison ÷ (1 + fXe · 7.8 · …) × (1 − rodFraction · (0.56 + 0.16 · thermalWeight)) × (0.72 + reflector · 0.38), with thermalWeight = min(1, moderator · 1.15). The display relaxes toward the blend at 2.8/s. When nothing has died recently, the display is the formula alone — a fact Receipt 1 makes vivid.

Power and temperature are a small thermodynamics of their own: thermal power is the fission rate (fissions in the last 1.25 s, divided by 1.25) × 22.5 MW, and the core temperature integrates heat in (power × 0.24) against heat out ((T − 270) × (0.08 + 0.0042 × cooling%)). Boiling starts at 330 °C: voids displace moderator (effective density × (1 − 0.8 × void fraction)), which is the game's negative void coefficient. At 1,100 °C the core melts.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit extracted the game's own script verbatim, ran it headlessly under node with a seeded random generator and DOM stubs, and instrumented the copy's event ledger — every neutron birth and every death, tagged by cause. The shipped file was not modified (sha256 2bdd92c6…). Every number below is measured from the game's own physics, at the game's own step rate.

Receipt 1: three different numbers wear the keff label

For the default Steady-State Critical preset, the audit measured k three ways: the game's analytic formula at the preset's settings, the game's own event ledger cleaned of outside interference (startup source switched off, a 12-second burn-in so every living neutron was born in the simulation, then 90 s of cumulative births ÷ deaths), and the value the display actually showed during those same windows:

Measurek for the default core
Analytic formula (what the display starts at)1.065
Clean event ledger, surviving run (58 fissions in window)0.957
Mean of the displayed value during clean windows0.30 – 0.50
Displayed value at the end of a 600 s run0.41 — while the core is still fissioning

The formula is optimistic and the display is pessimistic, and the true branching ratio of the shipped collision economy sits between them, just under 1. The display's pessimism is structural: its empirical window counts every death — including the deaths of startup-source neutrons, whose births are never counted — over a short 2.6 s window in a core where only a few dozen neutrons live, so the ratio sputters (samples ranged 0.10 to 1.09) and drags the blend down. The extreme case is the Subcritical preset after its chain dies: with zero neutrons alive, the readout sits pinned at exactly 0.285 — the formula's opinion, presented in the same digits as a measurement. The preset buttons' claims grade out as follows against the formula: "Balanced k ≈ 1.0" → 1.065 (fair); "k < 0.85" → 0.285 (comfortably true).

The same three-way split repeats across the controls. The formula's enrichment sweep at default settings runs 0.13 (5%) → 0.46 (20%) → 1.07 (44%) → 1.53 (65%) → 2.28 (96%), but the measured economy saturates: clean ledger values at 65% and 96% enrichment came out 0.98 and 1.00 — depletion, rod absorption, and leakage eat what the formula promises. Rods: formula 1.47 with rods fully out against a measured 0.89. The formula is a brochure; the collisions are the reactor.

Receipt 2: the default core lives on its startup source

With the ledger cleaned and the Cf-252 auto-source switched off, the flagship preset's population — a few dozen neutrons at best — dwindled and died in run after run, even in the run whose branching ratio measured 0.957: at this scale, a k of 0.96 is a slow death, and even a true 1.00 would be a coin flip against stochastic extinction in a core of tens of neutrons. As shipped (source on), the same core ran the full 600 s of the audit's long haul: 792 fissions, peak temperature 487 °C, never near meltdown, population sputtering between 4 and 31 neutrons — sustained indefinitely by the source's ~1–3 neutrons per second and the continuous refuelling of fissioned slots. That is, precisely, the behaviour of a real subcritical assembly driven by an external source. The death ledger for 180 s of the default core shows what the source is feeding:

Receipt 3: ν̄ is exact — and the code's own comment is off by a hundredth

Neutrons per fission, measured over the audit's runs and split by isotope:

FuelMeasured prompt ν̄Code's diceReal ν̄
U-2352.45 (306 fissions, runaway run)2 or 3, P(3) = 0.44 → 2.442.43

The dice are honest: a 2-or-3 distribution with the right means, where nature's real distribution also includes 0, 1, 4, and 5 with small probabilities (the game's support is narrower, the mean is what matters and it is right). One jewel for the small print: the code comment claims "~2.43 for U-235, ~2.88 for Pu-239" — the real values — but the dice as coded deliver 2.44 and 2.86. The comment quotes nature; the code approximates it; they differ by a hundredth in opposite directions.

Receipt 4: each isotope behaves as coded — measured one neutron at a time

The audit built pure lattices — all 234 slots one isotope, energies frozen (moderator 0), rods out — and injected single neutrons, 300 trials each:

Every qualitative claim on the page checks out: thermal neutrons are the fission-makers, Pu-239 is the richer fuel (higher fission share at both energies), U-238 breeds only from fast/epithermal captures, and Xe-135 eats only thermal neutrons — which is why a poisoned core can still be burned clean by its own flux. The quantitative bend is in the ratio: per physics step the code's thermal:fast fission dice are 0.84 : 0.16, a factor of 5.25 (about 9× once the larger thermal hit radius is counted), where nature's cross-section ratio is ~490×. The drawer's note says thermalization boosts the U-235 cross-section ">10×" — a fair description of the game's combined effect, and a large understatement of nature's.

Receipt 5: the rods are real geometry, rolled per step

In an emptied (all-depleted) core with rods at 100%, the audit fired 200 neutrons straight down a rod channel: 200/200 absorbed, thermal and fast alike. Fired down the gap between channels: 0/200. With rods at 0%: 0/200 down the channel line. The rods are not a global tax on k; they are five physical blades, 21.4 world units wide including their capture margin, and a neutron that never crosses one never feels them. Inside the band, absorption is a per-step roll — 0.96 thermal, 0.62 fast — so a fast neutron crossing the band in ~4 steps still has only ~2% survival. And because the roll is per step rather than per distance, rod strength is quietly coupled to the step rate: the same is true of every probability in the table above. The whole economy is calibrated for ~60 steps a second.

Receipt 6: the moderator is a drain, and its timing is exact

Single fast neutrons in an emptied, reflector-walled core; median time for the energy to fall to the thermal line (≤ 0.28):

The moderator does not simulate collisions; it drains energy linearly (with occasional random deflections), and the neutron's speed follows its energy down. Real slowing-down is multiplicative — a roughly constant fraction of energy per collision, fast at first and asymptotic at the end — where the game's is a ramp. What the drain buys is the essential behaviour: a controllable slowing-down time, a thermal population that the cross-section table then rewards, and a void feedback with teeth — boiling the moderator away genuinely un-thermalizes the core.

Receipt 7: meltdown is a fission counter wearing a thermometer

The runaway preset, run four ways (180 s runs in a companion experiment gave the same picture):

Every melting run melts after almost exactly the same number of fissions — ≈320 — because power is defined as fission rate × 22.5 and temperature integrates power: climbing the 785 °C from the preset's start to the 1,100 °C limit costs a fixed fission budget, and neither feedback can change the budget, only the (already fast) rate at which the core spends it. The void coefficient is real in the code — at full boiling it cuts effective moderator density to about a third — but at 78% enrichment with rods at 4%, the formula still rates the boiled core ≈1.75, and the collisions agree it stays hot. The delayed neutrons are real too, and equally outmatched. What actually saves the core is the one control that removes neutrons bodily: the rods. The Weapons-Grade preset tells the same story (≈11 s, ≈300 fissions). The lesson the preset teaches — feedback will not save you from a core this far out; only absorption will — is a true reactor lesson, arrived at by the game's own arithmetic.

Receipt 8: breeding and xenon both work — on a compressed clock

In the default core's 600 s run, the core bred 369 Pu-239 nuclei, and in the 180 s ledger run, plutonium supplied 173 of 498 fissions (35%) — the "PWR" core quietly becomes a third plutonium-fuelled within minutes. In the xenon preset's 180 s, Xe-135 burnout was a first-class neutron sink (307 deaths, more than U-238 capture), and the displayed k breathed between 0.12 and 0.93 as xenon built up from iodine decay and burned away in the flux — the iodine pit, oscillating on a cycle of a minute or so. Both chemistries are genuinely in the simulation. Their clocks are not nature's: the code turns U-239 into Pu-239 in 1.15–2.05 s (real half-life 23.5 minutes, and the real path passes through Np-239 at 2.36 days — the game skips neptunium entirely), I-135 into Xe-135 in 1.6–4.0 s (real 6.6 hours), and lets Xe-135 decay in 20–38 s (real half-life 9.2 hours). Everything chemical in the game runs roughly a thousand times fast, which is what makes the pit watchable.

How it's built — the coding choices

One lattice, one ledger

The whole reactor is two arrays and a step function. The 234 nuclei carry a type tag (U-235, U-238, Pu-239, U-239, Excited, Depleted, I-135, Xe-135) and a timer; the excited state is the elegant bit — an absorbed neutron doesn't fission instantly but marks the nucleus, which splits 0.08–0.145 s later, so cause and effect are visible as separate events on screen. Every birth and death also writes to the event ledger (kWindow) that feeds the empirical half of the k display — the same bookkeeping the audit instrumented. The file is fully self-contained: no external references, no network, no localStorage.

Presets are parameter sets, not scripts

Each scenario is one row of a table — enrichment, moderator, rods, cooling, reflector, five toggles, an initial burst, a starting temperature — loaded into the same machine. Nothing in the runaway run is scripted to explode; it explodes because 78% enrichment, rods at 4%, and no delayed neutrons multiply. That discipline is why the audit could vary one toggle at a time in Receipt 7 and learn something true about the machine.

Temperature is an integrator, not a lookup

The thermal model is two lines — heat in from the measured fission rate, heat out proportional to the temperature above the 270 °C coolant baseline — and everything thermal in the game (the void feedback, the warning colours, the meltdown) reads from that one integrated state. It is also why Receipt 7's fission budget exists: an integrator fed by a rate is a counter with a leak, and the leak (cooling at 22% in the runaway preset) is small against the inflow.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The displayed keff is a blend, not a measurement: it mixes a closed-form estimate with a short-window event ratio that undercounts births (source neutrons die counted but are never born counted), relaxes toward the mix at 2.8/s, and reads 0.3–0.5 in conditions whose true branching ratio is ≈0.96 and whose formula value is 1.07. With no neutrons alive at all it still displays the formula's 0.285. Treat the badge as the core's mood, and the isotope inventory as its state. Relatedly, the status pill declares "Prompt Critical" above a displayed 1.18 — real prompt criticality for U-235 begins at k ≈ 1.0065, and by the game's own delayed fraction it would begin near 1.035. The pill is a warning light with its own threshold, not the physics boundary.

The "PWR" runs on 44% enrichment: the default preset is badged "PWR Thermal Core," but a real PWR burns 3–5% U-235; 44% is highly enriched uranium, research-reactor and naval territory. (The Weapons preset's 94% is honestly labelled.) The enrichment percentage is also, mechanically, the probability that any given lattice slot is dealt as U-235 — the lattice is the enrichment, one nucleus per atom of statistics.

Cross-sections are per-step dice: every probability in the game is rolled per physics step while a neutron overlaps a nucleus, so all rates are calibrated to the ~60 steps/second the game runs at, and drift when the step size changes (slow frames, the 0.35× speed setting). Nature's probabilities live in barns and mean free paths, not in frames. Within that frame, the thermal:fast fission ratio is 5.25× per step (≈9× with the hit-radius difference) against nature's ≈490× — the game compresses the very contrast that makes thermal reactors possible, and still preserves its sign and its consequences.

Delayed neutrons are too many, too fast, and too cold: the game queues one delayed neutron after 8.5% of fissions — about 0.085 delayed neutrons per fission against nature's ≈0.016 for U-235 (β = 0.65% of ν̄) — emits it 0.75–2.1 s later (nature's precursors run to ~56 s half-life, mean delay ~13 s), and emits it already thermal (nature's delayed neutrons are born at ~0.5 MeV, softer than prompt but far from thermal). The game's delayed tail is roughly 5× too heavy and 10× too quick — a stronger, faster brake than the real one.

All the chemistry runs ~1000× fast, and neptunium is missing: U-239 → Pu-239 in ~1.6 s with no Np-239 step (nature: 23.5 min, then 2.36 days through neptunium); I-135 → Xe-135 in ~3 s (nature: 6.6 h); Xe-135 decay in 20–38 s (nature: 9.2 h half-life). The iodine pit breathes in a minute instead of a day. Fissioned slots also refuel themselves with fresh fuel in 3.2–6.7 s when the refuel toggle is on — the toy core cannot burn out, which real fuel (months to years per cycle) certainly can.

Power and temperature are display physics: the megawatt readout is fission rate × 22.5 — one fission per second "is" 22.5 MW here, where nature prices it at 3.2 × 10−11 W (200 MeV). The 1,100 °C meltdown line is in the neighbourhood of real cladding failure (Zircaloy bursts and oxidises violently around 800–1,200 °C); the UO₂ fuel itself does not melt until ≈2,865 °C. And the drawer's own note says voids form "above 320 °C" while the code starts boiling at 330 °C — a ten-degree disagreement between the page and itself.

Speeds are compressed past recognition, energies are not: on screen, fast neutrons move 365 units/s and thermal ones 152 — a ratio of 2.4. In nature the same pair (2 MeV vs 0.025 eV, both quoted correctly in the game's notes) moves at 2 × 10⁷ m/s and 2,200 m/s — a ratio of ~9,000. The screen could not show the real ratio and remain a screen; the energy labels carry the truth instead. Neutrons also die of old age at 9.5 s and can only be reflected before age 7 s — tidy bookkeeping rules with no physical counterparts, and the population is capped at 480 neutrons, beyond which births convert to an "overflow flux bonus" that still feeds the power meter.

Small print: the fission yield is only ever 2 or 3 neutrons (nature deals 0–5); the fragments are always Ba-141/Kr-92 (a real, nucleon-balanced channel — one of hundreds); the code comment quotes ν̄ as 2.43/2.88 while its own dice deliver 2.44/2.86; U-238's thermal capture (real, small: 2.7 barns) does not exist in the code, which captures only above the thermal line; and a neutron that fissions a nucleus consumes exactly one lattice slot, in a core of 234 slots standing in for ~10²⁵ real nuclei — which is why the game's core can be starved, poisoned, and refuelled on a human timescale at all.
