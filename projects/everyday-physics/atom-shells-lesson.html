Quantum Shells — the lesson

Quantum Shells is a bench, not a battleground: all 118 elements on one canvas, each atom built electron by electron in three views (3D orbitals, 2D Bohr, wave cloud), a photon gun that knocks electrons out and lets them fall back, and a two-atom lab where any pair of elements can be pushed together to see whether they transfer electrons, share them, pool them in a metallic sea, or bounce off each other. There is no score and nothing to win — the only counters are Expulsions and Recombinations. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the chemistry it teaches, the rules its bonding engine actually runs, and the audit that checked its 118-element database and its electron configurations against the real table. The headline is good news: the data is the most accurate of any guest exhibit so far — and the bends, where they exist, are in the bonding rules and the presentation, not in the table.

Quantum Shells was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction. This lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its data tables and bonding logic headlessly.

How to play

  • Pick an element. The slider, the Prev/Next buttons, the quick tiles (H, C, O, Na, Cl, Fe, Cu, Au, U, Og), the full periodic-table modal (key P), or the Tour button, which steps Z = 1 → 118 on its own. Arrow keys step one element at a time.
  • Change how you look. 3D Orbitals tilts every subshell into its own orbital plane; 2D Bohr flattens the atom into the textbook rings; Wave Cloud replaces the electrons with glowing probability shells. Drag the canvas to rotate, scroll to zoom.
  • Ionize it. Fire a UV photon (key E) to knock out an outer electron, or an X-ray for a core hit — watch the vacancy cascade and the fluorescent photon that carries the energy away. Expelled electrons curve back to a positive ion and recombine, emitting another photon. The Radiation slider makes spontaneous expulsions more or less frequent.
  • Bond two atoms. In the Two-Atom tab (or the bottom-bar button), pick any pair — the presets cover NaCl, H₂, O₂, N₂, HCl, Au–Au, He–Ne, and U–O — then drag the atoms together or press Approach & Bond. The pair is classified live: ionic transfer, covalent sharing on figure-8 loops, a metallic sea, or a noble-gas bounce.

The science

Shells, subshells, and why the order is strange

Electrons do not orbit like planets; they occupy subshells, labelled by a principal quantum number n = 1, 2, 3… (the shell — K, L, M, N… in the game's labels) and a letter s, p, d, f (the subshell shape). An s subshell holds 2 electrons, p holds 6, d holds 10, f holds 14 — the capacities 2(2l+1) that the game's filling table uses verbatim. If subshells filled strictly by n, chemistry would be dull and wrong: the 4s subshell fills before 3d, because what matters is roughly the sum n + l. That is the Aufbau principle with the Madelung (n + l) rule, and the game's filling order is exactly it: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p. The audit read the order straight out of the shipped AUFBAU array — it matches, term for term.

The exceptions are the interesting part — and the game knows them

The Aufbau order is a rule of thumb, and nature files exceptions where a half-filled or completely filled d subshell buys extra stability. Chromium "should" be [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴; it is actually [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ — one 4s electron promoted so the 3d subshell sits exactly half full. Copper does the same trick to reach a full 3d¹⁰: [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰. The game carries a hardcoded table of twenty such anomalies — Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm, Lr — applied over the plain filling. The audit checked every one of them against the experimental ground-state configurations, plus ten regular elements besides: 30 out of 30 match, including the subtle ones — palladium's empty 5s ([Kr] 4d¹⁰, the only element with no electron in its outermost s subshell), lanthanum starting the f-block era with a 5d electron instead of a 4f one, and lawrencium's last electron in 7p rather than 6d, which is the modern relativistic verdict. Gold's famous [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s¹ — the single 6s electron behind its colour and its chemistry — is in there too.

Why the table is shaped like that

The periodic table is the filling order drawn as a map. A row (period) is one pass of the filling; a column (group) collects elements whose outermost subshells rhyme, which is why the alkali metals (one s electron outside a noble core) all react alike and the noble gases (full subshells) barely react at all. The game's modal table places all 118 elements by its stored group and grid-row values, f-block included, and colours them by family — alkali, alkaline earth, transition, post-transition, metalloid, nonmetal, halogen, noble gas, lanthanide, actinide. Its telemetry also shows two numbers worth understanding:

  • Ionization energy — the price, in electron-volts, of removing the first electron. Small for the alkali metals (Cs 3.89 eV), enormous for helium (24.59 eV). It is the single best predictor of how eagerly an atom gives up an electron.
  • Electronegativity (χ) — how hard an atom pulls shared electrons toward itself in a bond, on the Pauling scale. Fluorine, 3.98, pulls hardest; the difference Δχ between two atoms is the game's whole bonding compass, below.

Four ways to hold together

The two-atom lab is built on the standard school taxonomy, and it is worth stating plainly because the audit grades the game against it. Ionic: one atom (usually a metal) hands electrons outright to another (usually a nonmetal with a much larger χ); the resulting ions attract electrostatically. Covalent: two atoms share pairs of electrons; how many pairs each atom "needs" is set by the octet rule — it wants 8 electrons in its outer shell (2 for hydrogen) — and the shortfall is the bond order: nitrogen, 5 outside, needs 3, hence the triple bond in N≡N. If Δχ is moderate, the sharing is lopsided and the bond is polar, with δ⁻ on the greedier atom. Metallic: metal atoms pool their outer electrons into a delocalized sea that glues the positive cores together and carries current. None: a noble gas, its shells full, has nothing to gain from any of this — push two together and the closed shells repel (Pauli repulsion) before any bond can form.

The rules the game runs

Every pair you push together is passed through one function, classifyPair, in this exact order — first match wins:

  1. If either atom is a noble gas → repel, order 0.
  2. If both atoms are metals (alkali, alkaline earth, transition, post-transition, lanthanide, or actinide families) → metallic.
  3. If one is a metal and the other is not, and Δχ ≥ 1.55 → ionic, with the transfer count below.
  4. Otherwise → covalent, with the bond order below; polar if Δχ ≥ 0.45.
transfer = min(donor's outer-shell electrons, acceptor's need, 2) The acceptor's need is 8 − (its outer-shell electrons), or 1 for hydrogen. Worked examples from the shipped code: Na + Cl — Δχ = 3.16 − 0.93 = 2.23 ≥ 1.55, transfer = min(1, 1, 2) = 1, and the banner reads Na⁺ Cl⁻. Ca + O — Δχ = 2.44, transfer = min(2, 2, 2) = 2: Ca²⁺ O²⁻. Both are exactly right. Where the cap of 2 bites, see the honest box.
bond order = min(needA, needB),   need = clamp(8 − valence, 1, 3),   H: need = 1 Checked against the presets: H + H → 1 (single), O + O → 2 (double), N + N → 3 (triple), H + Cl → 1 and polar (Δχ = 0.96 ≥ 0.45, δ⁻ on Cl). All five agree with the chemistry the page describes. The formula is the octet rule with the volume turned down: no bond order above 3, ever.

Ionization is simpler and more theatrical. A fired photon homes — its velocity is re-aimed at its target electron every frame — and on contact (within 16 px) the electron is always expelled, at 185 px/s for a UV hit on an outer electron, 260 px/s for an X-ray core hit. If the vacancy is in an inner shell, one electron from the next shell up drops in and a fluorescent photon carries off the difference — the game's version of X-ray fluorescence. Free electrons feel a Coulomb-style pull back toward any positive ion, F ∝ charge/(d² + 2200), and are captured on contact, emitting a recombination photon. Ionization energy enters only the ambience: the spontaneous-expulsion timer is stretched by clamp(IE/10.5, 0.55, 2.2), so caesium sheds electrons noticeably more often than neon — the right direction, at toy strength.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit extracted the game's database, its filling engine, and its bonding classifier from the shipped file and ran them headlessly. The game file carries no in-page honesty note, so the claims graded here are the ones its labels, banners, and notes actually make.

Receipt 1: the 118-element database — no errors found in the spot-check

Thirty-four elements spread across all seven periods and every family were checked field by field — symbol, standard atomic mass, group, and period — against reference values: hydrogen (1.008) through oganesson (294), including iron (55.845), gold (196.97), uranium (238.03), and the bracket-mass synthetics (Tc 98, Pu 244, Lr 266). Zero mismatches. The full table also passes two structural checks on all 118 rows: every element's subshell counts sum to exactly its Z, and every printed configuration string parses back to exactly Z electrons, noble-gas core included. Ionization energies spot-check the same way (F 17.42 eV, Fe 7.90 eV, Au 9.23 eV — all the reference values), and the note in the Ionization tab is accurate for the alkalis it names: Li 5.39, Na 5.14, K 4.34, Cs 3.89 eV, inside its stated ~3.9–5.4 eV.

The pattern is consistent: the classifier is a good school-level model with hard edges, and every wrong answer comes from an edge — the Δχ ≥ 1.55 gate, the transfer cap of 2, the bond-order cap of 3, or the noble-gas veto — not from bad data underneath.

Receipt 4: the numbers on the telemetry card

The neutron count is not stored data; it is computed as round(mass) − Z. That lands on the most abundant isotope for much of the table — Au "79p · 118n" is Au-197 exactly, U's 146 neutrons are U-238's — but it is arithmetic, not a census: copper's card reads 35 neutrons, which describes Cu-64, a rare radioactive isotope, while the copper in a wire is mostly Cu-63 with 34. The separation slider in the bonding lab is labelled in picometres and runs 110–580, but the value is world pixels — the Na–Cl pair settles about 151 units apart, and the readout calls them pm. (The real Na–Cl distance in rock salt is 236 pm; no scale connects the two numbers.) And there is no score to audit: the game promises none, keeps only its Expulsions/Recombinations counters, and that bookkeeping is exact — the counters increment in the same functions that expel and capture the electrons.

How it's built — the coding choices

One data table, everything derived

Each element is a single row — [Z, symbol, name, mass, family, χ, IE, group, grid-row] — and everything else is computed from it at load: subshells from the Aufbau array plus the exception table, shell populations by summing subshells per n, the configuration string by subtracting the largest noble-gas core, neutrons from the mass. One source of truth, no duplicated facts to drift apart — the Tech Bench way. The periodic-table modal is the same data again: each cell is placed by CSS grid using the stored group as its column and the stored row value as its row, which is why the f-block can live in the same grid (see the honest box for what those stored values really are).

Electrons as a rendering problem

Every electron is an object with a shell, a subshell, an orbital plane (inclination and node, dealt out by formula per subshell letter), a direction, and an alternating spin flag. Speeds follow (2.6 + min(2.4, 0.025·Z)) / n1.18 — inner shells faster, a gesture at the Bohr model's v ∝ Z/n, chosen for legibility rather than physics. Shared electrons in a covalent or metallic bond leave their shells entirely and fly a lemniscate — a figure-8 around both nuclei, phase-offset in pairs — skewed toward the more electronegative atom in a polar bond by an amount proportional to Δχ, capped at 0.28 of the half-distance. The nucleus is a golden-spiral packing of proton and neutron spheres, capped at 96 drawn spheres however many nucleons the atom really has. The whole file is self-contained — no external references, canvas and Web Audio only.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The transfer cap writes wrong ions: ionic transfer is min(donor valence, acceptor need, 2), so any bond needing three electrons stops at two and the banner prints the capped result as fact — "Al²⁺" for aluminium (really Al³⁺), "U²⁺" for the U + O preset (UO₂ is U⁴⁺). One-electron donors underpay two-electron acceptors the same way: K + S prints "S⁻" where the real sulfide ion is S²⁻.

The Δχ ≥ 1.55 gate is a cliff, not a slope: metal + nonmetal pairs just under the line — Zn + S (0.93), Cu + Cl (1.26), Na + H (1.27), Ga + Cl (1.35), Pb + S (0.25, and not even flagged polar) — are classified covalent, though several are substantially ionic in reality. Real bonding is a continuum; the code has four boxes and a threshold.

The noble-gas veto is absolute: any pair touching a noble gas repels, full stop. True for He and Ne; false at the heavy end of the family — KrF₂, XeF₄, and XeO₃ are real compounds, and the game's own data even gives Kr an electronegativity of 3.00 that the veto never lets it use.

Carbon always triple-bonds itself: the need formula gives carbon 8 − 4 = 4, capped to 3, so C + C is announced as a triple bond. Diamond and graphite — the carbon a player is most likely picturing — are single-bond networks. The same cap makes C + O a double bond (right for CO₂) and N + O a double bond (the real NO radical sits at 2.5).

The metallic sea conscripts core electrons: a metallic bond always delocalizes exactly 3 pairs — the six highest-n electrons across the pair, whatever they are. Sodium has one valence electron per atom; Na + Na's "sea" of six includes electrons pulled out of the n = 2 shell. For Au + Au the sea likewise draws on the filled 5d subshell. The picture is right; the headcount is choreography.

Photons cannot miss, and cannot be too weak: a fired photon steers itself onto its target electron every frame and expels it on contact, whatever the element. A "UV" photon ejects one of helium's electrons every time — in reality a UV photon carries at most ~10 eV and helium's ionization energy is 24.59 eV, so real UV does nothing to helium at all. Ionization energy in this game sets how often spontaneous expulsions happen, never whether a hit works.

The cascade is one step and keeps the wrong label: after a core expulsion exactly one electron drops, from the next shell up, and it keeps its own subshell letter as it falls — a p electron can land in an s vacancy and still be drawn as p. Real fluorescence is the same idea with selection rules, competing Auger electrons, and sometimes several steps.

Captured electrons always land in a "p" subshell: a recombined electron is placed in the atom's outermost shell with the subshell tag np, whatever the actual vacancy — Na⁺ regains its electron as "3p", though the hole it left was 3s. Charges and counts come out right; the address is wrong.

Stored "group" and "period" for the f-block are grid coordinates, not chemistry: the data comment promises period (1..7), but lanthanides are stored with period 9 and actinides with 10, and Lu/Lr carry "group" 17 — those numbers are the CSS grid row and column the modal table draws them at, nothing more. They are never displayed as a period or group, so the table you see is correct; the field names are the fiction.

Neutrons are arithmetic, not data: round(mass) − Z. Right for the most abundant isotope of many elements (Au-197, U-238), wrong for others — copper's card shows 35 neutrons (Cu-64) where common copper is Cu-63, 34. Treat the nucleon line as "a typical isotope," not the isotope.

Superheavy numbers wear false precision: for Z ≥ 104 the ionization energies (Ds 9.60 eV, Rg 10.60 eV, Cn 11.97 eV) and electronegativities are theoretical estimates — no one has measured Cn's ionization energy to two decimals — printed in the same format as the measured values beside them. Radon's χ of 2.20 is likewise an estimate, not a Pauling-scale measurement. Oganesson's χ, to the game's credit, is stored as "none" and displayed as —.

One superseded digit: the game's table prints argon's atomic weight as 39.948; CIAAW's current conventional value is 39.95. It is the only field in the table the audit found out of date — the rest checked exact.

Small print: the noble-gas line in the Ionization tab says noble gases cling at "~12–24.6 eV" while naming He, Ne, Ar — Ar is 15.76 eV; the 12 eV end of that range is xenon (12.13), which the sentence doesn't name. Configurations print in filling order (4s before 3d), not the conventional grouped order. The Wave Cloud's lobes rotate slowly and its shells are spherical for every subshell — real orbitals are stationary and mostly not spheres. Pull an ionic pair apart and the bond banner clears, but the transferred electrons never go home: the atoms stay ions until you change an element. And the "Target Separation" is in pixels labelled pm (see Receipt 4).
