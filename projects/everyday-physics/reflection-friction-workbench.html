Bay 1

Dual Cones — TIR vs. Friction

One angle, two boundaries. Left: a ray inside glass meeting air — inside the critical cone (θc = 41.8°) it escapes, outside it locks into total internal reflection. Right: a push on a block — inside the friction cone (θf = arctan μ) the block self-locks at any force; outside, it slides. Sweep θ and watch both verdicts flip at their cones. Hit Match θc = θf and they flip at the same angle — the twins, superimposed.

The self-lock test is the honest surprise of this bay: at 20° you can push with 500 N or 5,000 — the tangential component and the grip both scale with the same force, so the ratio never changes. Geometry outvotes muscle. It is why a Morse taper holds a drill chuck and why a wedge stays driven.

Bay 2

Oblique Bounce & the Superball

A ball strikes the floor at θ from the normal. The normal component always reflects (scaled by en); the tangential component is where the four contact laws part company. The bench runs one rigid-sphere grip impulse — Δvt = −(2/7)(1 + et)·u, with u the contact point's tangential velocity — and each mode is a setting of it: the mirror applies no tangential impulse at all; the billiard grips plastically (et = 0, which is exactly where the rolling sphere's 5/7 comes from); the superball grips elastically (et = 0.85) with backspin, and the rebound crosses the normal into the retro zone; heavy topspin makes the grip impulse push the ball forward.

Richard Garwin worked this out in 1969 to explain the Wham-O Superball: ultra-high normal restitution (en ≈ 0.94) plus an elastic tangential grip. Bounce one under a table and it comes back to your hand — try the geometry in Bay 2's numbers: with enough backspin the tangential velocity does not just shrink, it changes sign.

Bay 3

Tire Grip vs. Airfoil Flight — and Kamm's Circle

The same curve, twice. A tire's grip grows linearly as the tread deforms under a slip angle, peaks near 7.5°, then slides. A wing's lift grows linearly with angle of attack, peaks near 14.5°, then stalls as the flow lets go. Sweep the shared angle α and watch both build, peak, and break. Below, the tire's budget drawn as Kamm's circle: braking and cornering draw on the same friction — the resultant vector may not leave the circle.

Click the circle to set trail braking's balanced point — 71% braking and 71% cornering: √(0.71² + 0.71²) = the whole budget, nothing left over. At 100% braking the steering budget is exactly zero, which is the entire argument for ABS in one picture.

Bay 4

The Skipping Stone

Lydéric Bocquet's 20° optimum, live on the water. The bench's model, stated plainly: a skip survives while the stone meets the water at attack angle β ≤ 38° and keeps at least 3 m/s; each skip keeps a fraction r(β) = 0.94 − 0.0022(β − 20)² of its speed — best at 20°, wasteful at 6° where the flat stone drags, and at 43° there is no skip at all, only a plunge. The energy that does not survive leaves as splash and heat: friction's thermodynamic arrow, paid per bounce.
