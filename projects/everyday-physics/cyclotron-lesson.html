Cyclotron — the lesson

Cyclotron is a particle-bending bench with missions: curve a proton beam around a shield, sort isotopes by mass in a spectrometer magnet, split electrons from protons by charge, and spiral protons up a working mini-cyclotron whose radio-frequency gap has to be tuned to resonance before it will give up its energy. Everything in it runs on one equation — F = q(E + v × B) — integrated with the same Boris pusher real accelerator codes use. This page is the other half: the physics, the audit that flew the game's own integrator headlessly, and the receipts — starting with the sharpest test in electromagnetism: a magnetic field is not allowed to change a particle's speed, and over 200 full gyrations, this one doesn't.

Cyclotron was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

The physics

One force, two tempers

The Lorentz force is the whole game: a charge q moving at velocity v through electric field E and magnetic field B feels F = q(E + v × B). The two terms have opposite tempers. The electric part points along E (for a positive charge) and can point along the motion — so it can push a particle faster or slower. It does work. The magnetic part is a cross product: v × B is always perpendicular to v, by construction, on every instant of the flight. A force that is always perpendicular to the motion can never speed the particle up or slow it down — it can only steer. That single geometric fact is why magnetic fields curve beams into circles of constant speed, why a magnet alone can never be an accelerator, and why the audit's first test below is so unforgiving: any integrator that lets |v| creep inside a pure B field is inventing energy, and a spectrometer built on it is a scale that lies.

The circle is a scale: r = mv / |q|B

Balance the magnetic force against the centripetal requirement and the circle's radius falls out: r = mv / (|q| B), with the lap time T = 2πm / (|q| B). Read that formula the way a mass spectrometer does: at the same speed in the same field, the radius is a direct readout of m/q — mass per unit charge. Double the mass, double the radius. Double the charge, halve it. Flip the charge's sign and the circle curves the other way. That is the entire principle of Mission 2's isotope sorter and Mission 3's charge splitter, and the audit measured the game's circles against it for four species — the table is below, and the ratios come out at exactly 1 : 4 : 2 : −¼, theory's own numbers.

Electric fields are the only accelerator — and they pay exactly q·ΔV

Crossing an electric gap is a transaction with an exact price: the kinetic energy changes by the charge times the voltage crossed, ΔKE = q·E·L for a uniform field over a path L. No more, no less, and the sign follows the charge. Mission 4 is built on this arithmetic — its hint literally prints "ΔKE = q·E·L" — and the audit cashed the cheque three ways (below): a proton through the mission's own 400 kV/m × 0.18 m gate gained 72.1 keV against the 72 keV the formula promises, and an electron through a like field lost energy at the same rate per volt, because its q carries the opposite sign.

Resonance: the lap time doesn't care how fast you're going

Look at the period formula again: T = 2πm / (|q| B) has no v in it. In a uniform B field, a slow proton and a fast proton take the same time per lap — the fast one just draws a bigger circle. That is the whole trick of the cyclotron: an alternating electric gap flipping at one fixed frequency, fc = |q|B / 2πm, meets the particle at the gap on every half-lap with the push pointing the right way, forever, no retiming needed. Speed the particle up and its appointments with the gap stay exact; only its circle grows — the spiral of Mission 5. Swap in a deuteron (m = 2, same q) and the appointment rate halves: the sandbox preset's subtitle — "Try swapping to Deuteron and halving f_RF!" — is the formula talking. The audit verified the resonance numerically, including the control experiment: same RF, wrong B, and the energy gain collapses.

The math the game runs

The integrator is the Boris method — the standard pusher of plasma and accelerator physics since 1970 — run at a fixed physics step of dt ≈ 2.0833 × 10−10 s, eight substeps per animation frame. Each step is a half electric kick, an exact magnetic rotation, a half electric kick, and a drift:

v− = v + (q/m)(dt/2)·E  →  v+ = rotate(v−) by t = (q/m)(dt/2)·B, s = 2t/(1+t²)  →  v′ = v+ + (q/m)(dt/2)·E  →  x′ = x + v′·dt The rotation is the point. The t/s construction makes the magnetic step an exact rotation of the velocity vector — mathematically orthogonal, so |v| after the rotation equals |v| before it to rounding error, at any step size. A naive Euler step instead pushes v along the arc's tangent and adds a spurious radial component every step: the circle spirals outward and the speed grows, measurably, within a few laps. The physics core is real SI throughout — the exact 2019 constants e = 1.602176634 × 10−19 C and mp = 1.67262192369 × 10−27 kg, fields in tesla and volts per metre, speeds in metres per second, energies reported in keV. The toy is the clock: one animation second is 100 ns of physics, a slowdown of about ten million, so a 0.26 µs gyroperiod becomes a watchable 2.6-second lap.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own core out of the shipped file — constants, species table, borisStep, sampleFields, the energy and radius formulas, byte for byte — and ran it headlessly. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1 (the headline): in a pure B field, the speed does not move

Two hundred laps is where a leaky integrator confesses: an Euler pusher's speed error compounds every step and would be sitting in the percent range by now, its circle visibly spiralling. Here the fastest and slowest speeds seen across a quarter-million steps differ by three parts in 1014 — the width of floating-point rounding itself. The magnetic field in this game bends and only bends, exactly as Maxwell requires. The page's own code comment claims the rotation "preserves |v|² to IEEE-754 machine precision." For once, the comment is an understatement-proof receipt.

The measured gains run a consistent ~0.2% rich, and the audit can name the cent: fields are sampled at the position where each substep starts, so a particle collects up to one extra substep of push at the field's edge — an effective gap longer by v·dt ≈ 0.25 mm out of 180 mm. It is a known, bounded, one-step effect, identical in kind to the boundary treatment of every zone in the game, and it never compounds — but it is there, and now it's written down.

Receipt 4: the isotope sorter sorts — flown, not scripted

The audit rebuilt Mission 2 exactly as shipped — emitter at (105, 580) mm, the spectrometer magnet's rectangle, both collectors, the beam-stop wall — set the field to the hint's B = +0.160 T, and flew one particle of each species through the game's own field sampler and pusher. The proton curved up and landed in the p⁺ Mass-1 Collector (entering its face at (451, 188) mm after 0.282 µs); the heavy ion swept four times wider and landed in the X⁺ Mass-4 Collector (at (968, 213) mm after 0.407 µs). Nothing in the mission code steers them: the detectors simply count whatever real trajectory arrives inside their rectangle with the right species badge. Wrong species and low energy are counted as wrong hits by the same positional test — the win check reads positions and kinetic energies the simulation actually produced.

That control run is the receipt that matters. The gap flips at 3.81 MHz regardless; only when the magnetic field puts the proton's lap frequency on the same number does each crossing arrive in phase and pay out. Detune the magnet and the same gap gives with one hand and takes with the other. The resonance is in the arithmetic, not in a mission script.

How it's built — the coding choices

Fixed steps, substepped, fields sampled where the particle stands

The simulation advances in fixed animation frames, each split into eight Boris substeps of ≈ 2.0833 × 10−10 real seconds — so the physics is identical at any display rate, and a slow machine renders fewer frames rather than taking bigger, wronger steps. Fields are step-function zones (rectangles and one disc) sampled at the particle's position at the start of each substep: inside, the full field; outside, nothing. That is what makes the edges "sharp" in the honesty card's sense, and it is also the entire cause of Receipt 3's 0.2% — one design decision, both consequences, both written down.

Detectors are instruments, not triggers

A detector is a rectangle with an accept list and a minimum energy. Every substep, every live particle is tested against every detector's rectangle; a particle inside is removed and scored — a hit if species and kinetic energy (computed from its actual velocity, by the same ½mv² the telemetry shows) pass, a wrong hit otherwise. Mission completion is arithmetic over those counters. There is no path-checking, no "close enough," no scripted success: if the beam doesn't physically arrive, the counter doesn't move, as the off-resonance control run demonstrates from the losing side.

The cyclotron's emitter keeps the RF's appointments

Mission 5's ion source is slaved to its gap: it releases a proton once per RF cycle, at the phase where the gap's cosine peaks, so a correctly tuned machine starts every particle in step with the field it must ride. The inspector panel does the honest version of helping — it shows the proton's fc at the magnet's current B beside the gap's fRF and their percentage sync, the same numbers the audit checked, computed by the same formula the physics obeys. Progress — missions completed, stars, best scores — is kept in one localStorage key, cyclotron_progress_v1, and nothing else is stored.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate — the audit checked it line by line: non-relativistic (no γ anywhere in the core, and no synchrotron term — a circling charge in this game never radiates, and the Boris rotation wouldn't let it lose the energy anyway); no space charge (particles are stepped one by one through the field zones and never feel each other — verified in the loop, not just the card); sharp step-function field edges (exactly how sampleFields is written); strictly 2D motion (B is always the out-of-plane component, E always in-plane). All of that holds.

The electron is a fictional particle, disclosed: its mass is set to 0.25 u instead of the real 1/1836 u, and the honesty card says so and why — a true-mass electron at these speeds and fields would curl into a 0.07 mm circle and vanish from the bench. Everything the game then says about "the electron" — its 39.15 mm radius, its 0.1025 µs lap — is true of the scaled particle it actually flies, and the audit's tables label it as such. What the card doesn't add: at the energies shown, a real electron would also be mildly relativistic, which the scaled stand-in quietly excuses the game from modelling.

The Dee drawing is prettier than the field: the chamber is drawn as two D-shaped electrodes with a gap between them, but the magnetic field is applied over the full disc — which happens to be what a real cyclotron's magnet does — and the RF electric strip runs the disc's full height rather than living only between the dee faces. The physics that results is the physics the audit measured; just don't read the electrode outlines as a field map.

Small print: gap energies run ~0.2% rich from the one-substep edge sampling (Receipt 3). The RF frequency is rounded to 3.81 MHz against the exact resonance 3.8113 MHz — a 0.03% detune the spiral tolerates easily, as Receipt 5 shows. The clock is the toy: speeds, fields, and energies are real SI, but time is dilated ten-million-fold so the laps are watchable, and the chamber is a 1.2-metre tabletop instrument, not a facility. And at the game's highest proton energies (~136 keV in the audit's spiral), γ − 1 is about 10−4 — the non-relativistic card is honest at these energies; it would stop being honest in the MeV range, which this bench never reaches.
