Air Hockey & Curl-Shot — the lesson

One file, two games, and the same three ideas underneath both. Air Hockey pits you against an adaptive AI on a nearly frictionless rink, first to 7. Curl-Shot Shuffleboard trades the mallets for a drag-back launch and asks you to park stones in a bullseye 615 pixels away, across ice that changes its grip under you. Both run on sliding that barely slows, collisions that trade momentum along one line, and spin that bends the path. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples from its own numbers, and how it is built.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to Everyday Cool by Steven Powell. This lesson page was written for Everyday Cool to explain what is inside it.

The physics

The air cushion: friction turned down to a whisper

A real air hockey puck floats on a cushion of air blown through tiny holes in the table, so it almost never touches the surface — and with almost no contact, there is almost no friction to slow it or to forgive a bad angle. The game copies that with a velocity multiplier instead of a friction force: every sixtieth of a second, the puck's velocity is multiplied by a factor just under 1. On plain rink that factor is 0.9965, which compounds to keeping about 81% of your speed after one second — slow decay, but relentless, and it is the only thing besides the walls that ever takes speed out of the game. Because the decay is a percentage, a fast puck loses more pixels per second than a slow one, yet both halve on the same schedule.

The rink is not uniform. Two circular friction zones are painted on it: a Super Slick Ice patch where the factor rises to 0.9995 (97% of speed survives each second — the puck sails through almost untouched) and a High-Grip Felt patch where it drops to 0.988 (only about 48% survives a second — cross it and a breakaway dies at half pace). In Curl-Shot the same idea covers the whole sheet: the plain ice keeps only ~44% of a stone's speed per second, a Slick Ice Lane keeps ~66%, and the Heavy Pebble patch keeps ~21%. In the curl game, friction is not a detail — it is the opponent. The house sits 615 pixels from the launch pad, and whether your stone arrives at all is decided by which patches it crosses.

Collisions: only the line of centres changes hands

When two round things collide, the physics splits cleanly in two. Draw the line of centres — the line through both middles at the moment of contact. Along that line, the two objects exchange momentum: the approach speed is reversed and scaled by the restitution, the springiness factor. Along the perpendicular direction — the tangent — a smooth, frictionless contact changes nothing: each object keeps its sideways motion exactly. For two equal-mass discs, like the curling stones, the result is the famous near-swap: the striker hands almost its whole line-of-centres component to the target and keeps only its sideways drift, which is why a dead-centre hit stops the striker nearly still and sends the target away — the takeout — and why a glancing hit deflects the striker off at an angle while the target leaves along the line of centres. The game uses restitution 0.94 for these contact collisions, so the swap is close to perfect but not quite: a little speed is spent on the clack.

The mallets play by a cousin of the same rule. A mallet is a moving wall: the collision is computed in the mallet's own frame, where the puck's approach component along the line of centres is flipped with the same 0.94 restitution and the mallet itself never recoils. That is why a mallet moving into the puck hits so much harder than a stationary one — the puck leaves with the flipped approach speed plus the mallet's motion handed back to it. The walls of the rink are the simplest case: the velocity component into the wall reverses, scaled by the Wall Restitution slider (default e = 0.92, adjustable 0.55–1.00).

Spin: the slice writes ω on the puck

The puck carries an angular velocity, ω — how fast it is spinning, drawn on its face as the dark line so you can watch it rotate. Spin is born at the mallet. The collision code measures the tangential relative velocity at contact — how fast the mallet was sliding sideways across the puck's face at the instant of the hit — and converts a slice of it into spin: ω += vt × 0.015, clamped to ±18 rad/s. A head-on hit has no sideways slide, so it adds no spin. A deliberate slice — catching the puck while your mallet is already moving sideways — is a spin shot. Once written, ω fades on its own schedule, multiplying by 0.992 every sixtieth of a second: about 62% remains after one second, a half-life of roughly 1.4 seconds. Spin is a short-lived asset. Spend it soon.

The cushions read the spin too. When a spinning puck bounces off the top or bottom wall, the game adds English: the rebound picks up a sideways kick of ω × 6 pixels per second — at full spin, ±108 px/s of sideways jump written into the bounce. It is the table-tennis player's trick in code form: the spin you carried into the cushion comes back out as direction.

The Magnus curve: spin bends the road

A spinning ball moving through air drags a thin layer of air around with it; the air deflects one way, and by Newton's third law the ball is pushed the other — the Magnus effect, the reason curveballs curve and topspin drives a tennis ball down. In the hockey game it is implemented as a true Magnus-form force: an acceleration perpendicular to the velocity, proportional to both spin and speed, a = 0.095 × ω × v (times the Magnus slider in the Physics Lab). Perpendicular acceleration doesn't speed the puck up or slow it down — it steers it, bending the path into an arc whose radius is set by the ratio of speed to that acceleration. Double the speed and the same spin bends you half as hard per metre travelled; double the spin and the arc tightens twofold.

Curl-Shot uses a simpler cousin of the same idea. A stone's spin produces a constant sideways acceleration of 14 × ω pixels per second squared, whatever the stone's speed — as long as it is moving at all. You set the spin before the shot (slider or Q/E keys, −10 to +10), and it bends the whole slide: negative spin curls the stone upward on the screen, positive downward. Constant sideways acceleration means the sideways drift grows with the square of the sliding time — which is why a long, slow approach through the slick lane curls so much more than a quick one. Time under spin is the currency.

The math the game runs

Four formulas run both games. Each is stated the way the code states it, then used with the game's own numbers.

v ← v × f(60·dt) Friction, as coded in frictionAt(): each step multiplies velocity by the zone factor f raised to the step's share of a second. Per full second the factors compound to f⁶⁰ — hockey plain ice 0.9965⁶⁰ ≈ 0.81, slick ice ≈ 0.97, grip felt ≈ 0.48; curl plain sheet 0.9865⁶⁰ ≈ 0.44, slick lane ≈ 0.66, heavy pebble ≈ 0.21. The zones can be switched off in the Physics Lab, which flattens the rink to one factor (0.9965 hockey, 0.986 curl).
j = −(1 + e) · vn,   e = 0.94 The collision impulse along the line of centres, from the relative velocity's normal component vn. The mallet applies the whole impulse j to the puck (the mallet is a wall and does not recoil). Two equal stones split it — each takes j × 0.5 in opposite directions — which is exactly the momentum swap of two equal masses. The walls use the slider's e instead (default 0.92): the into-wall component reverses at 92% of its size.
aMagnus = 0.095 · ω · v   →   R = v / (0.095 · ω) The hockey puck's curve: acceleration perpendicular to travel, proportional to spin ω and speed v (both read live; the Physics Lab slider scales the 0.095 from 0 to 2.5×). A perpendicular acceleration bends the path into a circle of radius R = v²/a, which simplifies to v / (0.095·ω): at fixed spin, faster means a wider arc. Spin itself decays as ω ← ω × 0.992(60·dt) — ×0.62 per second.
aside = 14 · ω   →   drift = ½ · aside · t² The curl stone's bend: constant sideways acceleration while the stone moves. At the slider's maximum ω = 10 that is 140 px/s²; over a two-second slide the sideways drift is ½ × 140 × 2² = 280 px before friction's say in it — wider than the whole house (outer ring diameter 250 px). At a gentle ω = 4 it is 112 px over the same two seconds: enough to walk a stone in from outside the 1-point ring to the 3-point ring.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Every number below is computed from the constants in the shipped code, the same way the proof-checked lessons in this room present theirs.

Example 1: the takeout, and the glancing blow (Curl-Shot)

A stone slides in at 400 px/s and strikes an identical stone dead centre. The relative normal velocity is −400; the impulse is j = 1.94 × 400 = 776, split evenly: the striker gives up 388 and the target gains 388. The striker creeps away at 12 px/s; the target leaves at 388 px/s — 97% of the incoming speed, the missing 3% being the restitution's tax. Now the same shot at 45°: only the line-of-centres component, 400 × cos 45° ≈ 283 px/s, enters the trade. The striker's normal component falls to 8.5 px/s, its untouched tangential component stays 283, so it deflects away at nearly full speed, almost perpendicular to the target's path — while the target leaves along the line of centres at 274 px/s. Aim a stone's centre at the edge of another and you can predict both exits before you let go.

Example 2: why a moving mallet hits above its weight (Air Hockey)

Drive your mallet straight into a stationary puck at 500 px/s. In the mallet's frame the puck approaches at 500; the impulse flips that with e = 0.94, j = 1.94 × 500 = 970, and the puck leaves at 970 px/s — nearly double the mallet's own speed, from a puck that wasn't moving. The (1 + e) factor is the whole story: the puck keeps the mallet's motion and gets its approach handed back. It is also why the game caps puck speed at 1,350 px/s — chained mallet hits at full stretch would otherwise run away. And the slice: catch that same puck while your mallet is sliding sideways at 600 px/s across its face, and the tangential term writes Δω = 600 × 0.015 = 9 rad/s of spin in one touch — half the maximum, enough (next example) to bend a fast puck across the rink.

Example 3: how tight a curve can spin draw?

Take ω = 12 on a puck travelling 600 px/s. The Magnus acceleration is 0.095 × 12 × 600 = 684 px/s², and the turning radius is R = 600 / (0.095 × 12) ≈ 526 px — about the full playing height of the rink (560 − 2 × 26 padding = 508 px). Even a strong spin needs the whole rink to come around. Push to the clamp, ω = 18, on a slower puck at 350 px/s: R = 350 / (0.095 × 18) ≈ 205 px — a genuine hook inside your own half. The lesson the formula teaches: spin curves slow pucks hard and fast pucks gently, so the time to slice is when the puck is already dawdling, not when it's flying.

Example 4: the AI's launch speed is tuned, not round (Curl-Shot)

The AI launches its stones at 535 px/s (give or take 22.5). On the plain sheet, friction multiplies speed by 0.4424 every second — an exponential decay with rate λ ≈ 0.816 per second — so a stone's total stopping distance is v₀/λ = 535 / 0.816 ≈ 656 px. The house centre is 615 px from the launch pad. The AI's "round-feeling" speed is in fact measured: a straight, zone-free shot dies right around the button. Your own hardest drag is capped at 165 px of pull × 4.4 = 726 px/s, a stopping distance of ≈ 890 px — through the house and out the back if nothing intervenes. And if your line crosses the Heavy Pebble patch, the same 535 px/s shot stops in ≈ 338 px, barely halfway. In this game you don't aim at the house; you aim at a stopping distance, and the zones redraw the map.

How it's built — the coding choices

Both games live in one HTML file with no libraries, sharing their plumbing: one frictionAt() that answers "how slippery is it here?", one particle system for the collision sparks, one WebAudio voice whose clack rises in pitch and volume with impact speed. Three choices do most of the work.

Eight substeps a frame, so fast pucks can't tunnel

The game loop clamps each frame to at most 0.03 s and then runs the physics in 8 substeps. At the 1,350 px/s speed cap, one whole frame would move the puck 40 px — more than its own diameter of 38 — and it could jump clean through a mallet between frames. In substeps it moves 5 px at a time, and the overlap test (distance < r₁ + r₂) always catches. Whenever a simulation has things moving faster than their own size per frame, substepping is the cheap, honest fix — cheaper than swept collision tests, and it makes the friction and Magnus terms more accurate at the same time, because they are applied in smaller, fresher slices.

Mallets are kinematic: position from the pointer, velocity from the motion

Your mallet is not pushed by forces. Each substep it eases toward your pointer (fast — the tracking rate is dt × 34, so it nearly arrives within a frame), and its velocity is then measured from how far it actually moved, v = Δposition / dt. That one decision makes the whole game feel right: the puck responds to how fast your hand was truly moving at contact, including the flick, because the collision math runs on relative velocity. The AI mallet is built the same way — it picks an aim point, moves toward it under a speed cap, and its velocity is measured too, so the AI hits by exactly the rules you do. Its aim is an intercept prediction: it estimates when the puck will reach its side, t = (its x − puck x) / (|puck vx| + 250), capped at 0.28 s, and aims where the puck will be — discounted by its difficulty's reaction factor. The three difficulties are a small table of caps: Chill (speed 340, reaction 0.55), Pro (560, 0.78), Ace (790, 0.95). When the puck gets behind it, the AI doesn't cheat-teleport; it sweeps around the side, which is why a puck slipped past an Ace still sometimes scores.

The curl AI is simpler and more human: it waits 0.75 s to "think," picks a random spin between −4.5 and +4.5, then compensates its aim for that spin — shifting its target sideways by −9 px per unit of spin, because it knows the stone will curve the other way — and launches at the tuned 535 px/s of Example 4. It can be beaten the way you'd beat a person: its compensation is a flat rule, and the friction zones make flat rules wrong.

The aiming preview is a sketch, not a rehearsal

While you drag a curl shot, the dotted preview line ahead of the stone is a quick private simulation: 45 steps of 0.025 s, applying the same 14·ω sideways curl — but with a flat friction of ×0.975 per step, ignoring the zones entirely. It shows the shape of your curve honestly and its length only approximately; a shot whose dots end in the house can still die early in the Heavy Pebble patch, because the preview never felt it. Compare Docking, elsewhere on this table, whose prediction line is its real physics core run forward — two games, two defensible choices about what a preview promises. This one promises direction, not arrival.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: no air is simulated anywhere. The hockey curve is the Magnus effect's formula — a coded force perpendicular to travel, proportional to spin × speed — not airflow around a spinning puck; and the curl stone's sideways push is a constant 14·ω acceleration that doesn't scale with speed at all. Real curling stones, for the record, don't curl by Magnus: their bend comes from friction behaving differently at the front and back of a rotating stone on pebbled ice, a mechanism still argued about in the literature. The game borrows the name for a push with the same feel, and the feel is right. The mallets are infinitely massive — they never recoil, and a mallet can be eased through the puck's space between substeps if you move the pointer violently enough. Contact restitution is fixed at 0.94 whatever the slider says; the slider governs walls only, as its label states. Friction is a per-zone velocity multiplier with hard circular edges, not a contact model — a puck half in the felt is judged by its centre point alone. Stones keep their spin through stone-on-stone collisions (no spin is exchanged or lost in the clack), and the puck's spin affects nothing but the curve and the cushion English. Curl scoring counts every stone resting in the rings (5/3/1 by ring), where real curling scores only the stones closer than the opponent's best — this is shuffleboard scoring, as the game's name admits. One dormant detail, in the open: the AI difficulty table carries an "aggression" value (0.45 / 0.72 / 0.92) that the movement code never reads — the difficulty you actually feel is the speed cap and the reaction factor. The numbers are the game's own units throughout.
