Falling Sand — the lesson

Falling Sand — Element Lab is a box of 36,000 tiny cells and fifteen materials: sand, water, oil, acid, lava, fire, steam, gunpowder, and their neighbours. There is no physics engine in it — no velocities, no forces, no equations of motion at all. Each cell knows a handful of tiny local rules, and dunes, waterfalls, burning oil slicks, and a working water cycle fall out of those rules on their own. This page is the other half of the game: the rules it actually runs, the numbers coded into them, and how it is built.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied by Steven; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool, from reading the game's own code.

The idea — rules instead of physics

A cellular automaton

The game is a cellular automaton: a grid where every cell holds one material, and on every tick each cell looks at its immediate neighbours and applies a small rule — if the cell below me is empty or lighter than me, swap places with it. Nothing anywhere in the code knows what a dune is, or a wave, or a fire. Those things are what thousands of cells doing their tiny swaps look like from a distance. This is the collection's clearest working example of a claim Steven makes in his thesis: the behaviour lives in the pattern of exchange between the parts, not inside any single part.

Density is a pecking order, not a number

No element in the code carries a density value. "Heavy" is encoded directly in the swap rules: a falling cell may trade places with certain materials below it and not others. Sand swaps down through water, oil, and acid, so sand sinks in all of them. Water swaps down through oil but not through acid, so water sinks in oil and pools on acid. Oil swaps down through no liquid at all, so oil floats on everything wet. The stratification you see in the Density Lab preset — sand at the bottom, then water, then oil on top — is just that pecking order settling itself, one swap per cell per tick.

Heat is contact, not temperature

There is no temperature anywhere in this world — no cell stores a number of degrees, and heat never averages between neighbours. What the game calls heat is a set of contact reactions with probabilities: lava touching water turns to stone and steam in a single tick; fire touching wood gives the wood a 4% chance per tick to catch; ice quietly freezes an adjacent water cell with a 0.4% chance per tick. Fire spreads at the speed of those dice rolls, which is why oil (35% per tick next to fire) flashes over almost instantly while a wooden beam (4%) smoulders for a visible while before it goes.

Lifetimes make the weather

Fire, steam, smoke, and spark carry a countdown in a side array called life. Fire is born with 18–41 ticks to live; steam gets 120–229; smoke 45–89; a spark just 7. When a steam cell's countdown ends near the top of the box (row above 35) it has a 55% chance of condensing back into a water cell — which then falls as rain. Lava meets water, steam rises, steam cools at the ceiling, rain falls back into the lake: the full water cycle in the Volcano preset is four small rules and a countdown, wearing a trench coat.

The math — there is almost none, and that's the lesson

Docking runs on three formulas. Falling Sand runs on tables: who may swap with whom, and what the dice say when two materials touch. Here are the game's real tables, read out of its code.

Two honest wrinkles in the table: sand's diagonal slide (the move that builds piles) may swap through water, oil, steam, and smoke — but not acid or fire, which it can only enter straight down. And sand and lava never swap with each other at all, in either direction: poured onto lava, sand just sits on it. The pecking order has a couple of ties and one refusal, and the game is more interesting for them.

A heat-spread step, worked by hand

Take three cells in a row: Fire | Wood | Wood. There is no warmth to conduct — the left-hand wood cell simply rolls its 4% ignition die once per tick while it touches fire. The chance it is still unburnt after n ticks is 0.96n: after 10 ticks about 66%, after 25 ticks about 36% — so its expected wait to catch is 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25 ticks, roughly half a second of play. Only once it catches (and becomes a fire cell with its own 18–41 tick life) can the second wood cell start rolling its own die. Fire therefore creeps through timber at roughly one cell per 25 ticks, while through oil — 35% per tick, expected wait under 3 ticks — it runs about nine times faster. That difference in feel between a burning beam and a burning oil slick is two numbers in a table, nothing more.

cells = 240 × 150 = 36,000 The grid is 240 cells wide and 150 tall. Every tick, the simulation visits all 36,000 cells and asks each one its rules — and then the renderer visits all 36,000 again to colour them. At the 3× speed setting that is 108,000 rule-checks per frame, before drawing.

Worked examples — traced through the code

Example 1: one grain of sand falls through a water tank

Drop a single sand cell above the Density Lab's tank, where the water layer is 27 cells deep (rows 108–134 of the preset). The grain's whole journey is one rule, applied once per tick:

  1. Through air: the cell below is empty, so the grain swaps down — one cell per tick, no acceleration, no gathering speed. Falling here is a queue, not a plunge.
  2. At the surface: the cell below is now oil (the preset floats 25 rows of oil on the water). Oil is on sand's swap list, so the grain trades places with the oil cell — the oil cell pops up into the grain's old spot. Sinking and floating are the same swap seen from the two sides.
  3. Through the water: same rule, same one-cell-per-tick pace, all the way down — about 52 swaps and 52 ticks, just under a second at normal speed, to cross both layers. It arrives at the stone floor at exactly the speed it entered. There is no terminal velocity because there is no velocity.
  4. On the floor: straight down is blocked by stone, so the grain tries a diagonal — one step sideways in a randomly chosen direction, one step down — and slides off any grain already resting there. Thousands of grains doing this is what an angle of repose is: a pile at roughly 45°, because the only slide the rules allow is one-across, one-down.

Example 2: what a spark does to a wire — and to water

The Spark & Fuse preset lays a metal wire into a gunpowder charge. A spark cell lives for 7 ticks. While its life is 4 or more, every tick it converts each touching metal cell into a spark with 5 ticks of life — and stamps it with a secret marker (shade = 99) so that when the spark dies, the cell turns back into metal instead of vanishing. The charge travels the wire at up to a cell per tick in all eight directions at once, which is why the fuse appears to light everywhere almost simultaneously, and why the wire is still there afterwards. The gunpowder at the end detonates with radius 10: every cell in the disc becomes fire (65%) or smoke (35%), except metal, which survives each cell with 70% odds. But run that same spark through water instead and the story changes: water conducts only 25% of the time, the spark it spawns lives just 3 ticks, and when that spark dies the cell becomes empty — the water is consumed. Electricity in this world is free along metal and expensive through water, and the price is coded in two small numbers.

Example 3: lava's checkerboard

Lava is the one liquid with a speed limit, and it is a strange one: a lava cell only moves on ticks where (tick + x + y) is even — a checkerboard in space and time, so any given cell flows every second tick. Its sideways spread is also capped at 1 cell per move, against 3 for water, oil, and acid. Together those two numbers are the game's whole model of viscosity: lava oozes at a quarter of water's effective pace without a single viscosity formula anywhere. Note, though, that its reactions never take a tick off — a resting lava cell still checks its neighbours for water, ice, and fuel every single tick.

How it's built — the coding choices

Bottom-up scanning, so nothing falls twice

The simulation loop walks the grid from the bottom row upward. That ordering is load-bearing: a sand cell that swaps into the row below lands in territory the scan has already passed, so it cannot be visited again and fall a second cell in the same tick. Scan top-down instead and a grain could be re-processed at its new position and teleport to the floor in one tick. On top of the ordering, a per-tick updated flag marks every cell that has moved or been moved, and flagged cells are skipped — belt and braces guaranteeing the one-move-per-tick rule that the whole feel of the game depends on.

In-place updates with flags, not a double buffer

Many cellular automata keep two grids and write the next world into the second one. This game keeps a single grid (grid, a typed array of bytes) plus three side arrays of the same size: life for countdowns, shade for each cell's colour variation (and, secretly, the spark's "I was metal" marker), and updated for the movement flags. Swaps exchange all three side values along with the material. It uses a quarter of the memory of a naive object-per-cell design and — because everything is typed arrays — it is fast enough to run 36,000 cells several times per frame in a browser tab.

Randomness does the symmetry-breaking

Whenever a cell can move two ways — diagonal left or diagonal right, spread left or right — the code flips a coin (Math.random() < 0.5) rather than always preferring one side, and the horizontal scan direction alternates row by row and tick by tick. Without that, piles would lean, liquids would creep preferentially one way, and the world would develop a visible handedness. The coin flips are not decoration; they are what keeps a deterministic rule set from looking mechanical. Even the painting brush scatters: powder, fire, and spark strokes randomly skip 35% of their cells so a poured stream looks poured, not printed.

Drawing is a census in disguise

Rendering writes colours straight into an ImageData pixel buffer — one 32-bit integer per cell, packed from red, green, and blue — and hands the whole buffer to the canvas in a single call per frame. Because the render pass already visits every cell, it also counts them: the Matter Census panel (solids, liquids, gases, fire/spark) is tallied inside the drawing loop and refreshed every 8 ticks. The prettiest trick in the file is that the statistics are free — they ride on work the renderer had to do anyway.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there is no thermal conduction in this game, despite what its Table card says — no temperature field exists, heat never diffuses or averages between neighbours, and everything thermal is the discrete contact reactions tabulated above. There is likewise no stored density number: density is entirely the swap-permission lists, with ties (water and acid) and a non-swap (sand on lava) baked in. Nothing has momentum or velocity, so nothing accelerates, splashes, or arcs; every move is one cell per tick. Liquids never push sideways against pressure — a liquid spreads horizontally only into empty cells, so there is no true water pressure and no U-tube levelling beyond the spread rule. Gases rise only into empty cells and cannot bubble up through liquid. Steam condenses only near the top rows, by rule, not by any cooling calculation. Smoke cannot be painted — it exists only as a reaction product. Acid is consumed as it dissolves, but fire is never consumed by what it ignites, and a spark travelling through water deletes the water it passes through. Stone, wood, plant, ice, and metal are perfectly static: they never fall, even with nothing beneath them.
