What you are looking at

Four exhibits about one idea: resonance — a system with a natural rhythm absorbs energy greedily when you push it at that rhythm, and mostly shrugs when you push at any other. Everything on this page is the same equation wearing four costumes: m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = F₀·cos(2πf·t). The mass–spring is driven by a force; the pendulums are driven through a string; the beats are two rhythms interfering; the radio circuit is the equation again with inductance for mass, resistance for damping, and 1/capacitance for the spring.

Panel 1 — the driven oscillator

A mass on a spring, pushed by F = F₀·cos(2πf·t) with F₀ = 10 N (fixed, stated). Sliders set the drive frequency f, the mass m, the stiffness k, and the damping — presented as Q, where the damping coefficient is c = m·ω₀/Q and ω₀ = √(k/m). The natural frequency is f₀ = (1/2π)√(k/m). The chart draws the analytic steady-state curve A(f) = (F₀/m) / √((ω₀²−ω²)² + (ω·ω₀/Q)²). Press Sweep & measure and the page drives the simulated mass at one frequency at a time, waits for the transient to die, and measures the real amplitude out of the simulation with a DFT — those dots are measurements, not the formula, and the proofs at the bottom check that they land on the curve. The phase readout is the lag of the mass behind the drive: near 0° far below f₀, exactly 90° at f₀, approaching 180° far above. The motion is integrated with RK4 at a fixed 1 ms step; the drawing scales the motion to fit the canvas (the numbers are the true metres).

Panel 2 — Barton's pendulums

The classic 19th-century demonstration. A heavy driver pendulum (amber) swings at its own frequency and wiggles the shared string; each light pendulum feels that wiggle as a drive at the driver's frequency. Each pendulum is modelled as θ″ + (ωᵢ/Q)·θ′ + ωᵢ²·θ = κ·ωᵢ²·Θ·cos(ω_d·t) with Q = 10, coupling κ = 0.12, driver amplitude Θ = 0.30 rad held steady (a real driver slowly loses energy to the string; here it is held, and that is stated). A pendulum's natural frequency is (1/2π)√(g/L) — set by length alone. Before you press Restart, guess: which one sings? The one whose length matches the driver's. The others receive exactly the same pushes and only jiggle, because their steady answer to an off-rhythm push is small. Amplitudes build over ~15 s (the matched one's time constant is 2Q/ω_d ≈ 6 s); the ×3 speed button exists because pendulums are in no hurry.

Panel 3 — beats

Two pure tones added together: cos(2πf₁t) + cos(2πf₂t). The identity = 2·cos(π(f₁−f₂)t)·cos(π(f₁+f₂)t) says the sum is a fast tone whose loudness is modulated by a slow one — the wah-wah you hear when two guitar strings are nearly in tune. The beat rate is |f₁ − f₂| per second. The measured rate on the panel is not that subtraction: the page squares the summed signal, smooths away the fast carrier, and counts how often the surviving slow wave crosses its own mean — the same measurement the proofs use. Play the sum uses WebAudio (two oscillators, modest volume); browsers require the button press, which is why there is a button.

Panel 4 — the electrical twin

A series RLC circuit driven by V = 10 V AC: L·q″ + R·q′ + q/C = V·cos(2πf·t), current I = q′. Resonance at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)), peak current V/R, quality factor Q = (1/R)√(L/C), bandwidth Δf = f₀/Q = R/(2πL). The Sweep & measure button integrates the circuit's own equation at each test frequency and measures the current — the dots are measurements of the shipped simulation, exactly as in Panel 1. The test-frequency slider runs one steady-state integration and draws the current waveform it settles into.

Honest bends, stated

The amplitude peak is not exactly at f₀: for the displacement it sits at f₀·√(1 − 1/(2Q²)) — at Q = 8 that is 0.4% low, and the proof measures precisely that peak and reports its distance from f₀. What is exact at f₀ is the 90° phase and the velocity peak. The Barton driver is held at constant amplitude. The beats envelope drawing uses the trig identity (it is a drawing); the beat measurement does not. The spring's drawing is scaled to fit; the readouts are not scaled. Tacoma Narrows, in the story strip, is the famous case where the textbook word "resonance" is itself the bend — read the correction there.

Resonance — the physics of vibration

Push a system at its own rhythm and it drinks the energy; push at any other rhythm and it mostly shrugs. One equation, four costumes — a spring, a row of pendulums, two tones, and the tuner in every radio.

The one idea

Every system that can vibrate has a natural frequency — the rhythm it chooses for itself: a mass on a spring, a pendulum on a string, a circuit with a coil and a capacitor. Drive it from outside and the response depends almost entirely on how your drive frequency compares with that natural one. Far away, the system barely answers. Close, the answer grows. Exactly on it, each push arrives in step with the motion and adds its energy to all the pushes before — the amplitude climbs until the losses per cycle exactly eat what each push delivers. That balance point is resonance, and how high it climbs is set by one number, Q: how many cycles the system would ring on its own before dying away. High Q, tall sharp peak. Low Q, low broad hill. Everything below is that paragraph made touchable.

2 · Barton's pendulums — which one sings?

One heavy driver (amber) swings and wiggles the shared string. Six pendulums hang from that string, all receiving the same wiggle at the driver's rhythm. Only one has the driver's length — and a pendulum's rhythm is set by its length alone. Guess which one builds the big swing, then watch. It takes its time: resonance is accumulation, not impact.

4 · The electrical twin — this is a radio tuner

A series RLC circuit is the same mathematics in wires: inductance L is the inertia (the mass), resistance R is the damping, 1/C is the spring. Its current peaks at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) and nowhere else. Tuning a radio is this panel: every station sits at its own frequency, and turning the dial moves this peak — in an old set, by turning a variable capacitor — until the peak sits on the station you want. The peak's width is the selectivity: set R low and the peak is a needle — crisp rejection of the neighbours, but it can clip the station's own sidebands; set R high and the peak is a hill that lets the neighbours bleed in. The story everyone tells: Tacoma Narrows, 1940

On 7 November 1940, in a wind of about 40 mph, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisted itself apart and fell into Puget Sound. Generations of textbooks — and this page's author was raised on them — explained it as resonance: gusts of wind, shed as vortices, supposedly pushed the bridge at its natural frequency until the oscillation grew beyond bearing.

The correction, because this room teaches it: modern analysis (Billah & Scanlan, 1991, and the wind-engineering literature since) says it was aeroelastic flutter, not simple forced resonance. The vortex-shedding frequency that morning did not match the bridge's torsional frequency, and no orderly rhythm of gusts was needed: once the deck began to twist, its own motion through the air generated forces that fed the twist — a self-excited instability, the bridge pumping energy out of a steady wind the way a flag flutters. Resonance is a real and dangerous thing, but this bridge is a lesson in a subtler one: a structure can be its own drive. The honest sentence is "the wind destroyed it through an aeroelastic instability" — less quotable, more true.

The party trick: shattering a wine glass

A wine glass has a natural frequency you can hear — flick it and it rings at its own f₀, typically a few hundred hertz. Sing (or play) exactly that note, loudly, steadily, and the glass is a driven oscillator: each cycle of sound pushes in step, the rim's vibration grows, and if the strain at some flaw in the glass exceeds what the material can take, it shatters. This is panel 1 with a rim for a mass and air pressure for the pushing hand.

Honestly: it is real, and it has been done on camera with an unaided human voice (a vocal coach, Jamie Vendera, in 2005) — but it is much harder than the films suggest. It takes a thin glass, a note matched to within a hertz or so, sustained at roughly 100 dB or more right at the glass, and a voice (or speaker) that does not wander. Most glasses, most singers, most days: the glass just rings. Which is also the physics — off by a few hertz, you are back on the shoulder of the curve in panel 1, and the response collapses.
