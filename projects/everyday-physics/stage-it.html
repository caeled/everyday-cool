Why rockets fall apart on purpose

Real rockets are built in pieces that fall off — and this game is about why. To lift fuel, a rocket must burn fuel, so most of what it lifts is its own tanks. The rocket equation is a tyrant: it pays you in Δv (delta-v, change in velocity) and it pays grudgingly. Staging is the cheat: the moment a tank runs empty, you throw the tank away instead of hauling it all the way up.

Your decisions: how to split the rocket between its two stages, and — once you're flying — the moment to let the empty stage go. Orbit, in this scaled world, means reaching the orbit line with the mission's orbital speed at the same time: the two ingredients of orbit. (Real orbit also needs that speed pointed sideways. This game flies straight up, and says so.)

Simplifications, stated plainly: a 1-D vertical ascent — no steering and no gravity turn, so “orbital speed” is the speed ingredient of orbit taught on its own; gravity is a constant 9.8 m/s²; exhaust velocity is a constant 3,000 m/s; drag is light and quadratic; each stage's structure and engine split in the same proportion as its fuel; stage separation and upper-stage ignition are instant. The Δv figures, though, are the real Tsiolkovsky equation, computed live from the masses on the table.
