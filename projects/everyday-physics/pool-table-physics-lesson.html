Pool Table Physics — the lesson

The game calls itself the SVG Pool Table — Angles & Force Physics Lab, and "lab" is the honest word: there are no turns, no score, and no opponent. There is a table (800 × 400 pixels, standing in for a 2.54 × 1.27 m bed), a set of teaching presets — a 30° cut, a 60° thin cut, a straight-in force-and-spin shot, a bank, a combo, a six-ball break — and a physics engine that draws its own working while you aim: ghost ball, cut-angle arc, tangent line, spin curve, live energy split. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples from its own numbers, and how it is built.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied to Everyday Cool by Steven Powell. This lesson page was written for Everyday Cool to explain what is inside it.

The physics

The line of centres runs everything

When two balls touch, draw the line of centres — the line through both middles at the instant of contact. The collision splits the cue ball's velocity into two independent parts: the component along that line, and the component across it (the tangent). The across part is untouchable — a smooth contact has no grip sideways, so the cue ball keeps every bit of its sideways motion. The along part is traded. Pool balls all weigh the same, and for two equal masses a perfectly elastic trade is a swap: the striker hands its whole line-of-centres component to the target and takes the target's (usually zero) in return. The game's balls are equal by construction — there is no mass variable anywhere in the code; the collision impulse is simply split half and half — and the trade is scaled by the restitution, the springiness factor e, which the Ball Elasticity slider sets (default e = 0.96, adjustable 0.65–1.00).

Head-on: why the cue ball stops dead

Aim dead centre and the geometry collapses to one dimension: the tangent component is zero and the line of centres is the direction of travel, so the swap is total. With e = 1 the cue ball would stop exactly still and the object ball would leave at the full incoming speed. At the game's default e = 0.96 the split is 98% / 2% — the object ball takes 98% of the speed, the cue ball keeps a 2% creep — close enough to dead that the game's own Force & Spin preset uses a straight-in shot to demonstrate it. That leftover 2% is not sloppiness; it is what e < 1 means. Momentum is conserved either way (the impulse on the two balls is equal and opposite); it is kinetic energy that pays the tax, about 4% of it in a head-on clack at default settings.

Glancing: the 90° rule — and its two-degree secret

Cut the shot and the swap is partial. The object ball leaves along the line of centres carrying the traded component; the cue ball leaves with what's left: a sliver of the normal component plus its entire untouched tangent component. When e = 1 the sliver is zero, the cue ball exits purely along the tangent, and the two paths make exactly 90° — the rule every pool player learns, and the game draws it as a magenta tangent line with a little right-angle square at the contact point. But the code implements the rule with its own restitution in the formula, so at e = 0.96 the sliver is not zero: ½(1 − e)·cos θ of the incoming speed survives along the line of centres, and the real split at a 30° cut is 88.0°, the cue ball drifting two degrees forward of the drawn tangent line. The line on the screen is the ideal; the sim plays the truth, and the difference is small enough to aim by and real enough to measure.

The cloth takes two taxes, not one

Between collisions, the cloth is always billing you — but it bills in two different currencies depending on whether the ball is sliding or rolling. A ball is sliding whenever its surface speed at the contact patch doesn't match its travel speed: a centre-struck ball starts with no spin at all, so it slides; a draw shot's ball travels forward while its surface runs backward, so it slides hard. Sliding friction (μslide = 0.20 in the code) works on the slip — the mismatch — slowing the travel while spinning the ball toward a match, and it is the only force that can change a ball's spin on the open cloth. Once the mismatch closes, the ball is rolling, spin locks to travel, and the cheaper tax takes over: rolling resistance (μroll = 0.030) subtracts a constant 0.294 m/s² — a steady deceleration that doesn't care how fast you're going, which is why stopping distance grows with the square of speed. Both coefficients scale together with the Cloth Friction slider (0.4×–2.2×; 1.0× is the "Tournament" default). This two-regime model is the whole secret life of the table: nearly everything interesting — stun, follow, draw — is a story about which regime the cue ball is in when it arrives.

Spin is stored motion

The cue tip's contact point writes spin onto the ball as a surface speed: strike above centre (follow) and the ball leaves spinning forward at up to 1.45× its travel speed; strike below centre (draw) and the surface runs backward at up to 1.45×; strike dead centre and the factor is zero — pure slide. Here is the engine's most important design decision, stated in its own code comment: a ball-to-ball collision changes the translational velocity abruptly and leaves the spin untouched. So after a head-on hit the cue ball's velocity is nearly zero but its spin is fully alive — and then sliding friction goes to work on the mismatch. Follow spin drags the cue ball forward after the object ball (the chase); draw spin hauls it backward (the draw-back); a rolling arrival's spin matches its leftover tangent drift and bends it off the tangent line. That bending is the amber curve the game predicts for you: the magenta line is where a spinless ball would go, the amber curve is where your spin will actually take it.

Side spin — English — lives a quieter life. It is written at the strike (up to 0.42× the launch speed), decays exponentially on the cloth (×e−0.55t, a half-life of about 1.3 seconds), and does nothing at all in open play: this engine has no Magnus effect, so a ball with pure English rolls dead straight. English only wakes up at a cushion, where it fires a tangential kick of 0.85 × the surviving spin into the rebound — opening or closing the reflection angle — and spends most of itself doing it (the spin is cut to 0.35× by the contact). The bank preset exists to make that one moment visible.

The math the game runs

Five formulas run the table. Each is stated the way the code states it, then used with the game's own numbers. The table's scale is 800 px = 2.54 m, so 1 m = 314.96 px and the code's gravity is g = 9.81 × 314.96 ≈ 3,090 px/s².

vobj = ½(1 + e) · v · cos θ   ·   vcue, normal = ½(1 − e) · v · cos θ   ·   vcue, tangent = v · sin θ The collision split, exactly as computePrediction() states it. θ is the cut angle between the incoming line and the line of centres; v is the cue ball's speed at contact. The object ball takes the first term along the line of centres; the cue ball keeps the second term along that line plus all of the third along the tangent. At e = 0.96 the factors are 0.98 and 0.02. Square the speed shares to get energy shares — at θ = 30°: object 0.98² × cos²30° ≈ 72%, cue 0.02² × cos²30° + sin²30° ≈ 25%, and the missing ~3% is the restitution's cut.
d = v² / (2 · μroll · g) Stopping distance on the open cloth, the telemetry card's own formula. Rolling resistance is a constant deceleration μroll·g = 0.030 × 9.81 ≈ 0.294 m/s², so distance scales with v²: double the speed, quadruple the roll. At the game's maximum launch speed of 4.9 m/s (100% force, "≈11 mph, a realistic controlled break" per the code's comment): d = 4.9² / (2 × 0.030 × 9.81) ≈ 40.8 m — sixteen table lengths, taking about 16.6 s to die. At the default 55% force (2.50 m/s) it is ≈ 10.6 m, about four tables.
slip closes at (1 + 2.5) · μslide · g Sliding friction spends each step's budget dv = μslide·g·dt twice over: the travel speed moves by dv and the surface spin by 2.5 × dv, in opposite directions, so the slip between them closes at 3.5 × dv. The 1 : 2.5 split is not arbitrary — it is the solid sphere's ratio (a uniform ball's spin is "cheaper" to change than its travel, by exactly the factor in its moment of inertia), and it reproduces the textbook result: a centre-struck ball sliding from speed v settles into natural roll at exactly 5/7 v. At the default 2.50 m/s launch that is a 0.36-second, 0.78 m slide, arriving at the far end rolling at 1.79 m/s — if nothing interrupts it first.
r = κ · v,   κ = −tipY × 1.45   ·   wz = tipX × v × 0.42 What the tip writes at the strike. tipY runs from −1 (top of the ball, follow) through 0 (centre) to +1 (bottom, draw); κ > 1 means the surface outruns the travel — overspin, which sliding friction converts into a forward surge. Side spin wz is stored the same way, as a surface-speed equivalent, and is spent at cushions as a tangential impulse of 0.85 × wz.

The ghost ball, in one passage. The white dashed circle the game draws is the cue ball's future address: the spot where its centre must be at contact, exactly one ball diameter (2R = 26 px) from the object ball's centre, on the far side from where you want the object ball to go. Aim your centre at the ghost and the line of centres at contact is guaranteed — the object ball must leave along the line from ghost through its centre, because that is the only direction the trade can push it. The cut angle θ is the angle between your incoming line and that line of centres, and it is also a coverage fraction: at 30° your ball's edge crosses the object ball's centre — the "half-ball hit" of the preset's name. The engine finds the ghost by raycasting: it projects every ball onto your aim line, keeps the ones whose perpendicular distance is under 2R, and solves for first contact with a square root. Geometry first, simulation second — the prediction never has to guess where the hit happens.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Every number below is computed from the constants in the shipped code.

Example 1: the dead stop, priced (Force & Spin preset, head-on)

Fire the cue ball at the default 55% force — launch speed 2.50 m/s (788 px/s) — straight into a stationary ball, and suppose it arrives still carrying that speed. The split at θ = 0, e = 0.96: the object ball leaves at 0.98 × 788 ≈ 772 px/s (2.45 m/s); the cue ball keeps 0.02 × 788 ≈ 16 px/s (0.05 m/s) — barely a creep. Energy: 96.0% crosses to the object ball, 0.04% stays, and ≈ 3.9% is spent in the clack. Now drag Ball Elasticity to 1.00 and replay: the swap is perfect, the cue ball's share is exactly zero, and "stops dead" stops being an approximation. Drag it the other way, to 0.65, and the object ball takes only 82.5% of the speed while the cue ball follows through with 17.5% — the thud of cheap balls, available as a slider.

Example 2: the 30° cut, audited (the opening preset)

The game's banner for this preset quotes the textbook split: cos²30° = 75% of the energy to the object ball, sin²30° = 25% to the cue. Those are the e = 1 numbers. Run the code's own fractions at its default e = 0.96 and the audit reads: object ball 72.0%, cue ball 25.0%, restitution tax ≈ 2.9% — the object's share is what shrinks, because the (1 + e) factor sits on its side of the trade. The exit geometry shifts with it: the cue ball's surviving normal sliver is 0.02 × cos 30° = 0.017 of the incoming speed against a tangent share of sin 30° = 0.5, so the two balls separate at atan(0.5 / 0.017) ≈ 88.0°, not the 90° the tangent line draws. Two degrees and three percent: small, honest, and exactly the size of the difference between "elastic" as a label and e = 0.96 as a number.

Example 3: how far is a break? (the cloth as a budget)

Nothing to hit, full 100% force: the cue ball leaves at 4.9 m/s and the rolling tax of 0.294 m/s² needs ≈ 40.8 m to stop it — sixteen lengths of the table it started on. That is why a break shot is never in danger of dying mid-table, and why the same formula at the Cloth Friction slider's extremes is a different game: at 2.2× ("Slow Club") the break dies in ≈ 18.5 m; at 0.4× ("Fast") it would roll ≈ 102 m. The telemetry card runs this arithmetic live under the force slider — d ∝ v² is printed right on it — so the cheapest experiment in the lab is to halve the force and watch the promised roll distance quarter.

Example 4: draw is a perishable good (Force & Spin preset)

The preset parks the object ball 260 px away and presets a draw tip (tipY = 0.75, so κ = −1.0875). At the preset's 50% force the cue leaves at 2.25 m/s with its surface running backward at 771 px/s — but sliding friction spends the whole 260 px trip closing the slip, and the ball arrives with only −62 px/s of backspin left. After the head-on swap it converts that into a rolling return of ≈ 12 px/s: the cue ball essentially stays at the scene of the hit. Crank the same shot to 85% force and the trip is over before the cloth can collect: the ball leaves at 4.07 m/s, arrives still holding −1,066 px/s of backspin, and once its velocity is swapped away the stored spin drags it back into a ≈ 288 px/s (0.91 m/s) return that rolls out for roughly 1.4 m of draw-back — over half the table. Same tip, same distance, same preset: force is what buys spin a future. (Follow is the mirror: at 85% with a follow tip the cue ball arrives overspun, and after the swap its stored surface speed hauls it forward into a ≈ 391 px/s chase.)

How it's built — the coding choices

The lab is one HTML file: the table is a hand-drawn SVG (wood rails, brass castings, diamond sights, three felt colours) and the physics is a few hundred lines of plain JavaScript with no library. Four choices do most of the work.

Spin is stored as a speed, not an angle

Each ball carries its position, its velocity (vx, vy), its rolling surface velocity (rx, ry) — the velocity-equivalent of its topspin/backspin — and one scalar (wz) for side spin. Storing spin in the same units as velocity is what makes the cloth model three lines long: the slip is just v − r, friction nudges both toward each other at the 1 : 2.5 rate, and "rolling" is the state where they're equal. Follow, draw, stun, and the post-collision curves all fall out of that one comparison; there is no special case for any of them anywhere in the file. The cue ball's little red dot, orbiting as it rolls, is drawn from the same (rx, ry) — the spin you see is the spin the physics has.

Ten substeps a frame, and an impulse, not a spring

Each frame (clamped to at most 0.033 s, scaled by the slow-motion control) runs the physics in 10 substeps: integrate and apply cloth friction, test cushions and pocket jaws, then test every ball pair. Ball collisions are resolved the textbook way — first push the pair apart along the line of centres so they no longer overlap (each takes half the correction), then, if they're still approaching, apply the equal-mass impulse j = −(1 + e) × vnormal × 0.5 in opposite directions. No springs, no stiffness constants to tune: restitution is the only personality a collision has. Cushions are the same idea against a wall — the normal component reverses at erail = 0.84 (so a bounce keeps 0.84² ≈ 71% of its into-rail energy, as the telemetry card reports) — with the English kick added, and with twelve small "jaw" circles (r = 5 px) standing guard at the pocket mouths, bouncing at a deader e × 0.85 so balls rattle the jaws the way real balls do.

The prediction is a rehearsal, not a sketch

The aim guides are computed, not drawn hopefully. A raycast finds the first contact exactly; the split uses the same restitution fractions as the live collision; and a small analytic integrator (evolvePreImpactState, stepping the same slide/roll rules at 0.002 s) fast-forwards the cue ball's speed and spin along the trip, so the amber curve can start from the spin state the ball will actually have at contact — that is how the preview knows your draw will have died before arrival on a long shot. The spin curve itself is then stepped with the same friction code as the real simulation, at 1/180 s. Even the bank preview's English correction is honest bookkeeping: it shifts the rebound by tipX × v × 0.36, which is the live engine's 0.42 (written at the strike) × 0.85 (spent at the cushion) = 0.357, rounded. Where the preview does stop short: it follows the object ball through one combo hit and one cushion, and no further — a rehearsal of the first act, not the whole play.

Pockets by capture radius, and no rules at all

A ball is pocketed when its centre comes within the pocket's capture radius (23 px at the corners, 22 at the sides — against a ball radius of 13, a generous mouth), and the rail collision is switched off inside the pocket mouths so a ball on the lip can actually fall. A scratched cue ball is quietly re-spotted on the head spot. Beyond that there is deliberately nothing: no turns, no fouls, no score, because the presets are the curriculum and Replay — a snapshot of every ball taken before the shot, restored exactly — is the lab's real control. Change one variable, replay, compare. That loop is the scientific method with a Shoot button.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: "Elastic" on the tin means e = 0.96 at default — near-elastic, with ~4% of head-on energy spent per collision; perfect elasticity lives at the top of the slider. The 90° tangent line is drawn at the e = 1 ideal while the sim plays 88° at a 30° cut, and the preset banner's 75/25 energy split is likewise the textbook figure (the code's own telemetry computes the real 72/25). All balls are one mass — there is no mass in the code at all — and ball-to-ball contact is frictionless: no "throw," no spin is transferred to or induced in the object ball, and a cut never twists the target. Side spin never curves a ball in open play (no Magnus effect); English exists only at cushions. Spin is a surface-speed vector plus one scalar, not a full 3D rotation — there is no masse shot and no airborne ball, ever. The cloth's two friction coefficients are constants, with no speed dependence and no worn spots; rolling resistance is a constant deceleration, which is exactly why the v² stopping formula is exact here and only approximate on real cloth. The balls are drawn big for visibility — 26 px across on a 315 px/m scale is ≈ 83 mm, against a real ball's 57 mm — and the pockets capture by centre distance alone, with no lip, drop, or rattle-out physics beyond the jaw knuckles. Cushions are flat walls that read spin only through the English term and a fixed spin-damping factor. The numbers are the game's own throughout: speeds in m/s are converted at the table's stated scale, and the maximum launch of 4.9 m/s is a controlled break — real power breaks run two to three times faster.
