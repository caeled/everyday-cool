Wind Tunnel — the lesson

Wind Tunnel is a working fluid solver wearing a lab coat: a 160×90 grid of air, a wing you can pitch, camber, slim, invert, or replace with anything you draw, and a telemetry panel that reads out lift, drag, and stall. Its own lesson line, printed over the chamber, is the honest one — lift isn't magic: a wing stays up by shoving air downward, and every bit of lift pays a drag tax. This page is the other half of it: the physics the solver actually runs, and the audit that checked the telemetry against the flow field — including the one question that decides whether a wind tunnel is an instrument or a costume: where do the lift and drag numbers come from?

Wind Tunnel was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its solver headlessly (the game exposes its solver class for exactly that).

The physics

Air is stuff, and it cannot overlap itself

The solver runs the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in words: every parcel of air keeps its momentum unless pushed (by pressure or friction), and — the constraint that shapes everything — air cannot be created, destroyed, or compressed. Whatever flows into a cell flows out. That second rule is why flow speeds up over a wing's curved back and slows beneath it, why pressure falls where speed rises, and why a blocked tunnel is a different tunnel. Incompressibility sounds like bookkeeping; in a wind tunnel it is the plot.

Lift is a shove, not a suction spell

The page never says "Bernoulli," and the audit can confirm it doesn't need to. A wing at an angle, with camber, meets the oncoming air and turns it: the stream that arrived horizontal leaves angled downward. Newton's third law collects the other half of that transaction — air pushed down, wing pushed up. The pressure field is how the shove is delivered (lower pressure above the wing, higher below is the *mechanism of the push*, not a rival explanation), and the audit measured the deflection itself, below: downstream of a lifting wing, the air in this tunnel is measurably moving downward. The drag tax is just as literal: turning air costs energy, some air refuses to follow the wing's curve at all (that's separation, next), and every parcel that leaves in a swirling wake carries away momentum the wing paid for.

The boundary layer, separation, and the stall

Air touching the wing must stop — friction (in this sim, partly numerical, partly scripted; see the honest box) holds the first layer still, and the layers above it shear past in a thin boundary layer. As the wing pitches up, the flow over the back must climb an increasingly hostile pressure hill toward the trailing edge. Past a critical angle it gives up: the boundary layer separates, the suction over the back collapses, and the wing stops being a wing and becomes a barn door — still pushed by the wind, still making some force, at a ruinous price in drag. That collapse, not a number on a dial, is the stall. The audit pitched this tunnel's wing through it and recorded what the pressure field did, below.

Vortex streets: the wake keeps time

Bluff bodies — a cylinder, a flat plate — can't keep their boundary layers attached at all. The separated shear layers roll up alternately, top, bottom, top, bottom, shedding a staggered double row of vortices: a Kármán vortex street. The shedding is clockwork with a dimensionless rate, the Strouhal number St = f·D/U — shedding frequency times body width, divided by wind speed. For a real cylinder it sits near 0.2 across a huge range of conditions (≈0.16–0.17 at the modest Reynolds numbers a coarse grid can honestly reach). A simulated wake that sheds at the right Strouhal number is a wake whose dynamics are right; the audit clocked this one.

Why 2D lift is subtle — and why this sim gets away with it

In frictionless theory, flow around a wing has a free parameter — the circulation — and 2D ideal flow famously predicts zero drag and undetermined lift until you impose the Kutta condition by hand: the flow must leave the sharp trailing edge smoothly. A time-marched viscous simulation needs no such decree. Start the tunnel from still air and the wing sheds a starting vortex; the boundary layer and that vortex between them select the circulation the Kutta condition would have dictated, and lift emerges. Nothing in this code imposes a circulation value or a Kutta rule — the audit read the force code line by line — so whatever lift the tunnel reports, the flow earned it. The price is that the "viscosity" doing this work is mostly the grid's own numerical dissipation, which is why this tunnel lives at modest Reynolds numbers and says so on its honesty card.

The math the game runs

Each step (fixed dt = 0.34) is the classic stable-fluids sequence, upgraded to a staggered MAC grid — velocities stored on cell faces, pressure and vorticity at cell centres:

advect → confine → project → integrate forces Advect: every face velocity is traced backward along its own streamline (RK2 midpoint backtrace) and resampled — Jos Stam's semi-Lagrangian trick, unconditionally stable, and dissipative by construction: each resample is a weighted average, and averaging is where fine detail goes to die. The smoke ribbons get a sharper MacCormack scheme so they stay ribbons. Confine: a Fedkiw-style vorticity-confinement force (slider, 0–0.5) pushes energy back into the swirls the advection just smoothed. Project: a Gauss–Seidel SOR pressure solve (over-relaxation 1.74, 26 iterations) subtracts the pressure gradient that makes the velocity field divergence-free — this is incompressibility, enforced, not asserted. There is no explicit viscosity term anywhere: the diffusion the honesty card confesses to is the advection's own.

And the forces — the part the whole audit turns on — are integrated from the flow, face by face, over the body's stair-stepped boundary:

F = Σexposed faces pfluid · n̂  +  Σ μskin · utangent,   μskin = 0.016 For every solid cell of the test body, each face touching fluid contributes that fluid cell's pressure (from the projection's pressure field — the same field the Pressure view draws), pushing the correct way per face, plus a skin-friction term from the tangential velocity one cell out. No angle of attack appears anywhere in this function. No coefficient table. The one theatre prop is the final multiplier: the raw sum is scaled by 0.24, a constant whose own code comment says it was chosen so typical lift reads "10..28" — the shape of every number is physics; the size is a display calibration.

The solver class lifts cleanly out of the shipped file (the game itself exposes it for headless checks), so every number below was produced by running the game's own code — the same step function, the same force integration — and measuring its fields.

Read it carefully, because it's better and worse than the textbook picture. The rise to 8° is a real lift curve. At 12° the pressure integration feels the suction side let go: lift falls by a third — a genuine stall break, in the field, not in a formula — and drag keeps climbing. But then lift recovers at 16–20°, at two to three times the drag: the separated wing is now a bluff plate, and a plate at 20° really does make lift — at an L/D that has collapsed from 5.04 to under 2. So the field tells the full, untidy truth (attached wing → stall break → barn door), while L/D — the number a pilot actually lives by — degrades monotonically and honestly the whole way. Two calibration receipts flank the table: a symmetric wing at 0° makes lift −0.089, i.e. zero — the integrator invents nothing — and doubling the wind from 1.1 to 2.2 multiplied lift ×3.2 and drag ×4.7, in the neighbourhood of the dynamic-pressure U² law (not exact, because the grid's numerical viscosity doesn't scale with the wind, so changing speed also changes the effective Reynolds number).

Receipt 4: the air really is shoved downward

At 8°, with the wing making lift 23.9, the audit sampled the wake 70–100 cells downstream: the air's mean vertical velocity there is +0.34 to +0.40 (down-screen) across both halves of the wake band, against a freestream of 2.2 — a coherent downwash, not a fluctuation. (The near wake, one chord behind the trailing edge, is a recirculating tangle with no clean direction — which is also true in real tunnels, and why real force balances measure at the body, exactly as this sim does.) The lesson line over the chamber is the correct account of its own physics.

How it's built — the coding choices

A MAC grid, because faces are where honesty lives

Velocities live on cell faces (u on the left face, v on the bottom), pressure at centres — the marker-and-cell layout from 1960s Los Alamos, still the right choice: divergence is a exact difference of the four faces, solids block faces cleanly, and the pressure solve knows precisely which faces a wall closes. The wing itself is a true NACA four-digit profile — camber line, thickness distribution, rotated about a point near the quarter chord — rasterised into the mask cell by cell, so the "stair-stepped faces" of the honesty card are literal: the force loop walks those steps.

Anything you draw is a body

The brush and the preset stamps (cylinder, plate, teardrop, spoiler) write into the same custom mask the wing uses; a compose step merges masks into the solver's solid/obstacle fields, and the force integration walks obstacle cells only — tunnel walls excluded — so a sculpted blob gets the same honest pressure sum as the NACA wing. The challenges read the same smoothed telemetry the HUD shows (lift, L/D, drag, stall flag, shed index), held for three seconds: the game cannot be passed by a number the player can't see.

Diagnostics with their ears open

The stall detector doesn't watch the lift curve — it walks the suction surface column by column and counts cells where the near-surface flow has reversed, slowed below 18% of the wind, or filled with vorticity: separation measured where separation lives. The shed meter listens to a probe in the wake, counting zero-crossings of the transverse velocity and gating on wake vorticity. Both ideas are sound; both tunings bend, as the honest box records.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate as far as it goes: 2D incompressible grid, no spanwise flow or wingtip vortices; numerical viscosity from the coarse 160×90 grid holding it to moderate Reynolds numbers; constant density, no compressibility; and the boundary-force line — "pressure & shear are integrated across stair-stepped grid faces rather than a body-fitted mesh" — which the audit verified literally, face by face. It is the rare card that undersells nothing it mentions. What follows is what it doesn't mention.

The force units are calibrated, not physical: the integrated sums are multiplied by 0.24, chosen (the code comment says so) so typical lift reads 10–28; drag is floored at 0.15 so L/D never divides by nothing, and the displayed numbers are exponentially smoothed (84% history, 16% new). Treat lift and drag as a well-shaped instrument in display units: comparisons, ratios, and the stall break are the field's; the absolute values are set design.

Two invisible hands help the flow: vorticity confinement pumps energy back into swirls the advection dissipated — physical in intent (it stands in for the sub-grid turbulence a coarse grid can't hold), but it is an energy source with a slider, and at maximum settings it flatters the wake. And inside the velocity solver, faces touching the body are damped ×0.94 every step — a scripted boundary-layer brake, separate from the shear term in the force sum. The boundary layer here is half simulated, half stage-managed.

The tunnel walls slide: the top and bottom boundaries are slip walls moving at freestream speed (u set to U on the wall rows), not the no-slip walls of a real tunnel. The wing feels no wall boundary layers and the blockage is gentler than a real 44-cell chord in an 88-cell stream would suffer.

The stall badge is part measurement, part rule: its separation count is genuine field data (though generous thresholds push its score to 1.00 by 12° and 0.66 even at 0°), but it also adds scripted penalties that exist only above 15–16.5°, and the STALLED flag is forbidden by construction below |AoA| = 13.5°. In the audit's sweep the field stalled at 12° — lift down a third — while the badge, by its own gate, said nothing. Trust the L/D readout over the badge; the field stalls a few degrees before the badge is allowed to agree.

The Vortex Shed meter under-reads the street it watches: it counts zero-crossings in a 96-step history ring — but one shedding period at wind 2.2 is ≈130 steps, longer than the ring, so the rhythm term starves; during a measured St ≈ 0.169 street the meter showed 25%. Challenge 4 (Vortex Shedder) inherits this: at its own starting setup (teardrop stamped, wind 1.15) the audit's cylinder run peaked at 8% shed and 1.08 wake vorticity against thresholds of 62% and 1.6 — unreachable there; push wind and confinement to their maxima and the same challenge pegs the meter at ~100% and passes everything. The physics sheds early and often; the meter only applauds at full throttle.

Small print: pressure is the projection's pressure field, reset each step — the right field for forces and for the Pressure view, in the same calibrated units. Velocities are clamped at 3.4× the wind speed for stability. Challenge 4's text says "place a blunt obstacle" while its setup stamps a streamlined teardrop — the worst shedding shape on the shelf — as your starting point. Progress and best L/D persist in one localStorage key, wind_tunnel_aero_progress_v1.
