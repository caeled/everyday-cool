Chladni Lab — the lesson

Scatter five thousand grains of luminous sand on a steel plate, sweep a driver frequency, and at certain pitches the sand leaps off the shaking ground and files itself into lace: crosses, stars, rings, lattices. Nothing is drawing those figures. The plate is only allowed to vibrate in certain shapes — its eigenmodes — and the sand is simply reporting where the surface stands still. This page is the other half of the lab: the physics of standing waves, the audit that checked the game's modes, sand, and scoring against that physics, and the receipts — including five thousand grains herded onto the true nodal lines of the (2, 3) figure, measured grain by grain.

Chladni Lab was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its field and sand engines headlessly.

The physics

A surface may only vibrate in shapes that fit

Strike a plate and it does not wiggle arbitrarily. Like a guitar string — which can only sustain waves whose ends behave — a plate sustains only standing waves: patterns where every point oscillates in place, some points swinging through the full amplitude (the antinodes) and others never moving at all (the nodes). The allowed patterns are the plate's eigenmodes — the shapes it is permitted to sing in — and each comes with exactly one natural frequency. Drive the plate at some other pitch and you get a feeble shudder; drive it at an eigenfrequency and that one shape swells until it owns the surface. That swelling is resonance, and it is why the lab's frequency slider feels like a radio dial: static, static, then a station — suddenly — at full strength.

Chladni, 1787: drawing sound with sand

Ernst Chladni published his Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges in 1787 with plate after plate of figures he produced by sprinkling sand on metal plates and drawing a violin bow down the edge. The bow excites one eigenmode; the sand is thrown off the vibrating antinodes and comes to rest along the motionless nodal lines, tracing the mode's skeleton in grains. The figures were so startling that Chladni was summoned to demonstrate them to Napoleon in 1809 — and the Academy's prize for the mathematical theory of plate vibration pushed Sophie Germain to the work that became plate theory itself. Every figure in this lab is one of Chladni's, computed the modern way: the nodal lines are the zero set of a mode shape, and the sand finds them the same way his did — by being unable to rest anywhere else.

Why the square plate needs a ± button: degeneracy

On a square plate, the mode with n stripes one way and m the other has a twin: swap n and m and the frequency is identical (the formula below is symmetric in the two). Physicists call this degeneracy — two different shapes at one pitch — and a real driven plate rings in a mixture of both. Add the twins and you get one family of figures; subtract them and you get the mirror family, diagonals flipped. That is literally what the lab's Mode Symmetry switch does: it chooses cos(nπx)cos(mπy) + cos(mπx)cos(nπy) or the same with a minus. On the circular plate the same switch does something humbler: cos(mθ) versus sin(mθ) is the identical rosette rotated by a fraction of a turn. One button, two genuinely different pieces of physics, depending on the plate.

A plate is not a drum

A drumhead is a membrane: a stretched skin whose rim is clamped, so its modes are sine products — zero at the edge, by construction — and its frequencies climb gently, as √(m² + n²). A Chladni plate is stiff and its edges are free: the rim is an antinode, not a clamp, and bending stiffness makes the pitch climb as m² + n² — much faster. The lab models the plate, not the drum: its square modes are cosine products, whose slope vanishes at the edges and whose corners swing at full amplitude (the audit measured a corner sitting exactly at the field's peak, |A| = 1.000 — a drum would read 0 there). The honest caveat, which the game's own card states and the audit confirms below: a real free plate's modes are built from beam functions — cosines married to hyperbolic cosines — and the full truth is the biharmonic plate equation. The cosine products are the classic textbook idealization of Chladni's figures: the right family, the right frequency law, simplified ancestry.

The math the game runs

The square plate

A(x, y) = ½·cos(nπx)·cos(mπy) ± ½·cos(mπx)·cos(nπy),   f = 20·(n² + m²) Hz Sixteen curated modes, from (1, 2) at 100 Hz to (5, 8) at 1780 Hz. The audit divided every listed frequency by its (n² + m²): the ratio is 20.000 for all sixteen — the plate law f ∝ n² + m² holds exactly, not approximately. The constant 20 is a calibration: it absorbs the plate's size, thickness, and steel, which the game does not model — the ratios between pitches are the physics. (Under a membrane's √(m² + n²) law, (2, 3) would sit at 1.612× the (1, 2) pitch; here it sits at 2.600× — 13/5 — because this is a plate.)

The circular plate

A(r, θ) = Jm(kmn·r) · cos(mθ),   f = round(9·kmn²) Hz Fourteen modes, where Jm is the Bessel function and each kmn is a zero of its derivative, J′m(k) = 0 — the free-edge condition for a disk. The audit differentiated the game's own Bessel code at all fourteen tabulated k values: the worst |J′m(k)| is 1.9 × 10−8. The table is exact. Frequencies follow f ∝ k² — the ratio f/k² is 9.00 for every mode — the Kirchhoff plate scaling. (Chladni's own empirical law for round plates, f ∝ (m + 2n)², counted in nodal diameters and circles, does not fit this table — f/(m + 2n)² wanders from 5.25 to 33.0. The game uses the derived law, not the 1787 fit; Chladni's law was an approximation to a plate he couldn't yet compute, and it is a mark in this build's favour that it didn't paste the famous formula where the truer one belongs.)

Between the stations: a Lorentzian crowd

The live field is never a single mode. At any dial frequency, every mode of the plate answers, weighted by a Lorentzian resonance curve centred on its own pitch:

w = γ / √( (f − f0)² + γ² ),   γ = max(7.5, f0/68) At the mode's own frequency w = 1; one γ away it has fallen to 0.707. The corresponding quality factor is Q ≈ 34 at the top of the dial. Off-station, the field is a genuine wash of neighbouring modes — the audit measured the plate's overall resonance strength at 0.29 when tuned to 300 Hz, between the 260 and 340 stations, and 0.26 at 165 Hz. One bend, named in the honest box: before summing, the code cubes each weight, sharpening the dominant mode beyond what the Lorentzian alone would give. The stations are a little more sovereign here than physics makes them.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit extracted the game's own engines from the shipped file — its Bessel function, its mode tables, its 144 × 144 field grid, its resonance weights, its sand stepper, its challenge scorer — and ran them headlessly. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1: the sand really does find the nodes

The sand rule, read from the code: each grain samples the field amplitude A at its position. If A² exceeds a whisper (0.00018), the grain receives a random vibrational kick whose size grows with |A|² — the physical mechanism, since a real grain on a shaking patch is thrown, and on a still patch is not — plus a gentle deterministic drift downhill in A², straight toward the nearest nodal line. It is not a cheat that teleports grains to the lines, and it is not pure physics either: the drift is a patient hand real grains don't have. The game's honesty card discloses both terms. What matters is whether the shipped rule, run as shipped, assembles Chladni's figures out of the mode's own mathematics. It does:

Square (2, 3)+ — after 30 simulated seconds99.6% within |A| < 0.10; 98.9% within ≈0.025 of an analytic nodal line

That control row is the one to keep. Run the same sand under a frequency that excites no clean mode and it does not drift onto the (2, 3) cross — it settles onto the weak wash's own ragged nodes. The figures in the lab are the modes' figures, delivered by the sand, not a picture the sand was told to draw.

Receipt 2: the match scorer is a sharp instrument

"Match the Mode" does not compare your sand to a picture. It converts both the live field and the target mode into nodal-density maps — exp(−A²/0.24²), which lights up only near nodal lines — and computes their Pearson correlation over the grid, then multiplies by a resonance gate so a lucky shape at a dead frequency cannot score. Measured against Challenge I's target, the (2, 3)+ cross:

The win demands correlation ≥ 0.90 with the drive up, held while a 1.4-second lock fills. The sand's own settled fraction — grains physically resting on the target's lines — only sets how fast the lock fills (rate 0.35 + 0.65 × settled). So the field decides whether you've found the mode; the sand decides how long you must hold your nerve. Wrong symmetry scoring 0.003 is the receipt that the ± switch is a real physical choice in this game, not a mirror cosmetic.

Receipt 3: the fingertip is a damper, not a clamp

The "Touch / Dampen" tool multiplies the field by a Gaussian footprint, 1 − 0.94·exp(−d²/2σ²) with σ = 0.16 of the half-plate, ramping in and out as you press and lift. At full press the field at your fingertip is multiplied by 0.060 — 94% suppressed, but not the exact zero a clamp would pin; one σ out, 43% survives; two σ out, 87%. It is, deliberately, a finger: press a real vibrating plate and you get exactly this — a soft, local kill-zone that reshapes the figure while you hold it (the sand reads the damped field, so grains genuinely re-file under your touch) and lets go when you do. If you came expecting a clamp that forces a permanent node at a point, what the lab gives you is 94% of one, temporarily, with rounded edges. The audit counts that a fair trade wearing the right label — the button says "Touch / Dampen," not "Clamp."

Receipt 4: the tone is the frequency

Turn the audio on and the fundamental oscillator is set to state.freq — the dial number, unscaled (its 60–2200 Hz safety clamp never binds; the slider runs 70–1860). At 260 Hz you hear 260 Hz. Riding on it are two quieter voices: a true octave (2f, gain that grows with resonance) and an inharmonic shimmer at 2.756f — not a harmonic, and not derived from the mode table; real plates do ring inharmonically, and this is the synth's nod to that, at dressing level. Volume blooms as resonance^1.6, so the station swells as you tune into it: a loudness metaphor for Q, clearly felt, honestly labelled nowhere — it is listed here.

How it's built — the coding choices

Everything is precomputed except the physics

The field lives on a 144 × 144 grid. The square modes' cosines are tabulated once (cos(kπx) for k = 0…8 along each axis), the circular modes' Bessel profiles Jm(k·r) are baked into a cache per mode, and the angular cos(mθ)/sin(mθ) maps are precomputed per m — so a frequency sweep is just re-weighting and re-summing finished shapes, fast enough to feel continuous at 60 fps. The Bessel function itself is real work: a convergent power series for |x| ≤ 18 and Hankel's asymptotic expansion beyond it, accurate enough that the audit's J1 zero came back at 8 × 10−11.

Five thousand grains, one shared field, no grain-grain lies

Grains are independent test particles in typed arrays, stepped twice per frame against the one live field, with positions bilinearly interpolated from the grid. They never interact with each other — no collisions, no piling, no friction — which is why the settled figures are crisp one-grain-deep lace rather than drifts. A deterministic xorshift PRNG throws the kicks, so the sand's statistics are stable run to run. Grains whose local A² falls below threshold simply stop: stillness is the settled state, rendered as the calm ivory shade you see crystallize along the lines.

The target is a field, not a photograph

Each challenge's target is generated by the same pure-mode function that builds the live field, and the little target thumbnail is rendered from that field's nodal density — the same quantity the scorer correlates. Target, thumbnail, and judge are one object seen three ways, so the game cannot accidentally demand a figure its own physics can't produce. Progress — solved challenges and best times — persists in one localStorage key, chladni_lab_progress_v1.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate — the audit checked all three bullets: the modes are analytical superpositions of idealized free-edge modes, not an integration of the biharmonic plate equation ∇⁴w + ∂²w/∂t² = 0 ✓; the sand takes random kicks ∝ |A|² plus a gentle drift and omits grain collisions, friction thresholds, electrostatic clumping, and air drag ✓; the frequencies follow f ∝ n² + m² (square) and f ∝ k²mn (circle) without thickness, Young's modulus, or bolt stiffness ✓. It is a short card and an honest one.

What the card doesn't mention: the resonance weights are cubed before the modes are summed, so the dominant mode rules the live field more completely than a true Lorentzian response would allow — between stations, a real plate's mixture is more democratic. The summed field is then peak-normalized and scaled by a resonance envelope, so the field's absolute amplitude is presentational: the pattern is the physics, the brightness is theatre. And the sand's downhill-in-A² drift, disclosed on the card, deserves its weight felt: real grains are ballistic — thrown by antinodes, landing where they land — while these grains also walk toward the nodes. The walk is why the figures complete so satisfyingly; the kick term alone would get there more raggedly, as Chladni's own plates did.

The model under the modes: cosine products are the textbook idealization of a free plate, not its true eigenfunctions — a real free plate's modes are beam-function hybrids with Poisson-ratio corner behaviour no cos(nπx)cos(mπy) reproduces, and a real Chladni plate is also held at a centre bolt, which pins a node the sim doesn't impose (the centre of the (2, 3)+ figure here is near an antinode, |A| ≈ 1.0). The frequencies are calibrated toys in absolute terms — 20·(n² + m²) and round(9k²) — with true ratios. Chladni's empirical (m + 2n)² law, the one with his name on it, fits neither table and is not used.

Small print: the dampen tool's 6% residual and soft σ = 0.16 footprint (Receipt 3); the audio's 2.756f shimmer is an invented overtone, and its loudness follows resonance, not radiated power; modes contributing less than 3% weight are skipped in the live sum, an invisible economy; and the challenge's settle percentage is a pace-setter for the lock timer, not a second judge — a player who finds the mode with messy sand still wins, just a beat slower.
