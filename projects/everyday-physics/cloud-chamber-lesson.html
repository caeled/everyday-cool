Everyday Physics · The back door to Half-Life

Half-Life — the lesson

Half-Life is a cloud chamber you can reach into: six radioactive sources, a sample of hundreds of nuclei decaying one honest dice-roll at a time, and the vapor trails their radiation leaves behind — thick straight alpha tracks, thin beta spirals curling in a magnetic field, positrons that annihilate into twin gamma flashes, and a live graph plotting the populations against the theory they are supposed to follow. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that checked it — including the proof that its decay dice are exactly memoryless, that its chain populations reproduce the Bateman equations to within shot noise, that its chain table is the real uranium series link by link, and the places where the compression quietly changes the phenomenon: an equilibrium that never forms, a beta spectrum that leaks past its own endpoint, and a Geiger counter that counts pictures instead of decays.

Half-Life was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its decay engine and track generators headlessly.

How to play

There is no score. The chamber is the instrument, and the experiments are the ones the controls invite: change the field and watch the spirals reverse, slide a lead plate into the beam and count what gets through, or just let a uranium sample burn down its whole family tree into lead while the graph keeps score against theory.

  • Choose a source. Six: Uranium-238 (the full 14-step cascade to stable Pb-206), Radium-226 (the classic alpha showcase, through radon gas), Carbon-14 (pure β⁻, the radiocarbon clock), Sodium-22 (positrons and their 511 keV annihilations), Americium-241 (the smoke-detector alpha source), and Cobalt-60 (β⁻ plus twin gammas, Compton recoils and pair production). The chain list shows every isotope in the family with its real half-life; the simulation runs on compressed clocks, in seconds.
  • Handle the sample. 600 nuclei by default (slider), as a point source in a draggable capsule or dispersed as a mist. Click anywhere in the chamber to drop a fresh cluster of unstable nuclei; drag the source capsule to move the sample.
  • Read the tracks. Thick, bright, nearly straight lines are alphas. Thin, wandering spirals are betas — cyan β⁻ curling one way, magenta β⁺ the other. Dashed rays are gamma photons (a display device — see the honest box), which occasionally end in a Compton recoil electron or an e⁻/e⁺ pair V. Long, straight, faint tracks crossing the whole chamber are cosmic-ray muons.
  • Work the field. The magnetic field slider runs −2 T to +2 T, perpendicular to the chamber. Sweep it through zero and every spiral reverses; the Flip B button does it in one press. Heavy alphas barely bend; light betas corkscrew.
  • Work the shield. A plate of paper, aluminum, or lead can be dragged into the radiation's path (scroll over it to rotate, slider for thickness). Paper stops every alpha, lead eats most gammas, and the info line tells you what each material is doing.
  • Run time. Speeds of 0.25×, 1×, 3×, and 8×, plus an activity slider that scales every decay constant. The graph plots the live populations of the key isotopes against the dashed theoretical parent curve, with markers at each parent half-life. Keys: Space pause, R reset, F flip field, G Geiger audio, H zen mode.
  • Listen, optionally. The Geiger-Müller audio is synthesized live and muted by default: deep resonant clicks for alphas, crisp ticks for betas, high pings for gammas and muons.

The physics

Decay is a dice roll with no memory

An unstable nucleus does not age. A uranium-238 atom that has sat unchanged for a billion years is exactly as likely to decay in the next second as one made a moment ago — the probability per unit time, λ, is a constant of the isotope, not a property of the atom's history. That single fact forces the whole mathematics of radioactivity: with N nuclei each rolling the same memoryless dice, the population must fall exponentially,

N(t) = N0 · e−λt,   t½ = ln 2 / λ The half-life is just the time at which the exponential reaches one half. After one half-life half remain; after two, a quarter — not zero, because the survivors' dice are as fresh as ever. Carbon-14's 5,730-year half-life is what makes radiocarbon dating a clock: the fraction of ¹⁴C left in once-living material reads off the time since death directly from this curve.

It also means no single decay can ever be predicted, while the population curve is essentially exact — probability at the level of the atom, certainty at the level of the crowd. The game's graph draws both at once: the jagged live counts of a few hundred dice-rolling nuclei, and the smooth exponential they are collectively obeying.

Chains: daughters are born radioactive

Uranium-238 does not decay to stability in one step. It sheds an alpha particle and becomes thorium-234, which is itself radioactive, and so on down a family tree — the decay chain — fourteen steps of alpha and beta decays before the line rests at stable lead-206. Each link has its own half-life, from 4.47 billion years at the top to 164 microseconds for polonium-214 near the bottom. The populations of a whole chain obey the Bateman equations: each isotope is drained by its own decay and fed by its parent's.

Chains have a famous long-run behaviour. When the parent is far longer-lived than every daughter — true of real uranium, where the parent outlives its quickest daughter by a factor of 1018 — each daughter's population grows until its decay rate equals the parent's rate. That is secular equilibrium: every member of the chain decaying in lockstep, populations proportional to half-lives. It is why a lump of old uranium ore is far more radioactive than the uranium alone would suggest, and why radon-222 — a gas in the middle of the chain, constantly regenerated by the radium above it — seeps into basements. Watch for whether the game's chain does this; the audit did, and the answer is one of the interesting ones (Receipt 3).

Three radiations, three signatures

  • Alpha (α) — a helium nucleus, charge +2, thousands of times heavier than an electron. It ionizes ferociously and stops fast: a few centimetres of air, or a sheet of paper. In the chamber that means a short, thick, straight, bright track, with a final flare of ionization — the Bragg peak — just before it stops. Its range in air grows steeply with energy, roughly as E3/2 (Geiger's rule).
  • Beta (β⁻) — an electron born in the nucleus when a neutron turns into a proton. The decay energy is shared with an antineutrino that escapes unseen, so beta electrons come out with a continuous spectrum of energies from zero up to a hard endpoint — the decay's Q value. Being light, a beta scatters constantly and curls tightly in a magnetic field: a thin, wandering spiral. β⁺ is the mirror process — a positron, curling the opposite way — and when a positron slows to rest it annihilates with an electron into two 511 keV gamma rays fired exactly back-to-back. That annihilation flash is the entire basis of PET scanning.
  • Gamma (γ) — a photon, no charge, no mass. It does not ionize as it goes and leaves no track of its own; it is seen only when it interacts, all at once: knocking an electron out in a Compton scatter, or — if it carries more than 1.022 MeV, twice the electron's rest energy — converting into an electron-positron pair near a nucleus, pair production. In air a gamma's mean free path is of order a hundred metres; shielding is a matter of dense material and exponential attenuation, the Beer-Lambert law I = I0e−μx.

The chamber itself, and reading a track

A cloud chamber is supersaturated alcohol vapor, chilled until the air holds more vapor than it should. Vapor condenses preferentially onto ions — and a passing charged particle leaves a trail of ions — so the particle's path appears as a line of droplets. C. T. R. Wilson built the first one in 1911; in 1932 Carl Anderson found the positron in a cloud chamber, recognising it because its track curled the "wrong" way in the magnetic field. That is the chamber's real superpower: a track's curvature is a measurement. A charged particle in a field B moves in a circle whose radius is set by its momentum,

R = p / (qB),   p = √(E² + 2E·mec²) Radius grows with momentum and falls with charge and field. The direction of curl gives the charge's sign; the radius gives p. Anderson's positron had an electron's radius and a positive charge's curl. In this game B points out of the screen at +1.2 T by default, so β⁻ (cyan) and β⁺ (magenta) spiral in opposite senses — exactly the discrimination a real chamber performs.

The math the game runs

The decay engine is one Bernoulli draw per nucleus per frame, with the probability taken from the exact exponential rather than the linear approximation:

p = 1 − e−λΔt = 1 − 2−Δt/t½,   λ = (ln 2 / t½) × activity Δt is the frame's simulated step: the real frame time (clamped to 1–50 ms) times the speed setting (0.25–8×). Because the probability is compounded exactly, the statistics are right at any frame rate and any speed — a nucleus can still only advance one chain step per frame, but the draw itself is never approximated. A decay moves the nucleus exactly one link down its chain table and fires the corresponding particle.

The chain tables carry the real decay mode and energy for every link, with the half-life replaced by a compressed simulation value in seconds. The uranium table, as coded:

Tracks are generated whole, at the moment of decay, by three little integrators. The alpha walks a pre-set range, 42 + 10.5·E1.38 px, curving gently (κ = B·0.00145/√E, growing as it slows), its width shaped by a Bragg curve peaking at 91% of the range, with decorative delta-ray stubs on 28% of its points. The beta random-walks with true Lorentz curvature κ = qB·0.036/max(0.14, p) per pixel — p the relativistic momentum √(E² + 1.022E) — plus Coulomb scattering that grows as 1/p, losing energy continuously as it goes. The gamma ray-marches in 6 px steps: inside a shield it faces a Beer-Lambert absorption draw per step (μ = 0.002 paper, 0.012 aluminum, 0.068 lead, per step); in open vapor it faces a Compton draw of 0.0022 per step and, above 1.022 MeV, a pair-production draw of 0.0019×(E − 1.022) per step.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own data tables, decay engine, and track generators out of the shipped file — same constants, same probability formula, same integrators — and ran them headlessly, with only the audio object and the history recorder stubbed. Every number below is measured from the shipped code, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1: the dice are exactly memoryless, and the half-life is the stated one

120,000 carbon-14 nuclei were run through the game's own engine and the survivor count fitted at ten successive 7-second marks (the stated sim half-life is 14.0 s):

The nuclei do not age, the curve is the exact exponential, and the dashed theory curve the graph draws over the live counts is the curve the engine actually obeys.

The stochastic engine is an exact sampler of the chain mathematics — the "Bateman" label on the population panel is earned.

Receipt 3: but secular equilibrium never forms — the compression forbids it

Those same activity ratios carry the honest surprise. In the real uranium series, secular equilibrium pins every daughter's activity to the parent's: ratio 1.00, for geological ages. In the game the ratios climb straight past 1 — radon at 9× the parent's activity by 60 s, the bottom of the chain at 60× by 90 s — because the parent is burning away almost as fast as its daughters decay. The compressed half-lives span a factor of only ~31 from top to bottom of the chain (11 s to 0.35 s) where reality spans ~1018, so no member is ever "long-lived" enough to anchor an equilibrium. What emerges instead is a decay wave: a pulse of activity travelling down the chain, overshooting as it goes, with the whole sample resting as Pb-206 by about three minutes. The mathematics is exact (Receipt 2); the phenomenon is the compression's, not nature's — and it is arguably the better show, because you can watch the wave move.

Receipt 4: the chain table is the real series, link by link

Every link of the coded uranium chain was checked against the evaluated nuclear data. The sequence, the decay mode of every step, and the stable Pb-206 terminus are exactly the real 4n+2 series — all fourteen transitions, eight alpha and six beta. The energies are the real ones, not round numbers: U-238's alpha is coded 4.20 MeV, which is the real dominant emission line (4.198 MeV), not the 4.27 MeV Q value; Po-214's 7.69 against the real 7.687; Bi-214's beta endpoint 3.27 against 3.269; C-14's 0.156 against 0.1565; Na-22's positron endpoint 0.546 against 0.5457. The real half-lives printed in the chain list are the true values throughout. The gamma multiplicities that were measured match their coding exactly: Na-22 fires its 1.275 MeV gamma in 0.900 of decays, Co-60 fires both of its real gammas (1.33 and 1.17 MeV) in every decay — 2.000 gammas per decay over 1,541 decays — and Am-241 fires its 59.5 keV line at 0.262 per decay against a coded 0.25.

Receipt 7: gamma interactions respect their thresholds, and annihilation is exact

Two thousand gammas at each energy were flown across the 1,600 px chamber. At 0.511 MeV — below the pair-production threshold — zero pair productions in 2,000 flights, with 39.8% Compton-scattering in the vapor (the code's Compton mean free path of 2,727 px predicts ~42% over the flight, less the margin where interactions are switched on). At 1.33 MeV, 50.9% interact and 10.7% of all gammas pair-produce; at 2.0 MeV, 63.1% and 29.8% — the pair channel opening exactly at 1.022 MeV and growing with E − 1.022, as coded and as physics requires. Positrons: 200 of 200 slowed and annihilated, and across the whole audit 1,918 of 1,918 annihilation gamma pairs were exactly back-to-back — π apart to the last bit of floating point, two 511 keV rays conserving momentum the way the real annihilation does.

Receipt 8: the Geiger counter counts spawns, and at 8× it starts dropping them

A 600-nucleus uranium sample was run for 240 sim-seconds at 1× and at 8× with every click counted. Both runs record exactly 8,400 decays (600 nuclei × 14 steps — the whole family tree, a nice integrity check in itself). At 1×: 9,804 clicks, 1.17 per decay — more than one per decay because each gamma ray clicks too, and the 119 cosmic muons click in the gamma voice (1,264 gamma clicks + 119 muon clicks = the 1,383 'gamma' clicks recorded, to the unit). The busiest frame holds 8 decays, under the engine's cap of 9 new tracks per frame, so at 1× nothing is dropped. At 8× the busiest frame holds 27 decays against that same cap: only 6,439 tracks are spawned for 8,400 decays — secondaries included in the 6,439 — so at least 23% of decays leave no track and no click, and the click rate falls to 0.87 per decay. The engine's own statistics counters never drop a decay; the chamber's display, and its voice, sample the busiest moments instead of censusing them.

How it's built — the coding choices

One draw per nucleus, and the chain is a table

The whole simulation is the SOURCES table plus a loop: each frame, each nucleus draws once against its isotope's exact exponential probability, and a success moves it one row down the table and fires that row's particle with that row's energy. There is no separate physics for "the chain" beyond the table itself — which is why the audit could verify the engine and the data independently, and why both came out clean. The graph is the same honesty applied to display: populations sampled every 0.35 sim-seconds, drawn against the theoretical parent exponential and half-life markers, so the player can watch the dice agree with the theory in real time.

Tracks are computed whole, then revealed

Nothing in the chamber actually travels. At the instant of a decay, the particle's entire path is integrated in one pass — every scatter, the whole spiral, the Bragg-shaped width — and stored as a point list; what animates is a reveal, the condensation front sweeping along the stored path, after which the points drift with a convection field and fade on the supersaturation lifetime (alpha ≈ 2.6 + 7.8×supersaturation seconds, beta ≈ 2.1 + 6.5×). Caps keep the scene legible: at most 260 tracks alive, at most 9 new tracks per frame — the second cap being the one Receipt 8 catches binding at 8×. Positron annihilation is the one genuinely deferred event, scheduled with a real timer so the twin flashes arrive after the track has played out.

The Geiger counter is a synthesizer with a dead time

Each click is a 14 ms burst of filtered noise, voiced by particle type: alphas get a 1,050 Hz bandpass plus a 210→65 Hz thump, betas a 2,550 Hz tick, positrons 3,100 Hz, gammas and muons a 4,300 Hz ping, at type-specific gains. A limiter enforces 11 ms between clicks — the code's comment calls it a "realistic GM tube dead-time limiter," and it does mimic the way a real tube goes briefly blind after each discharge, though a real tube's dead time is nearer 100 µs and this one is chosen for pleasant audio. The file is fully self-contained — no external references, no storage, no network; the only output device it touches beyond the screen is the Web Audio context, muted until you enable it.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The clocks are compressed, and not uniformly: real half-lives in the uranium chain span 4.47 billion years down to 164 microseconds — a factor near 1018. The sim's span 11 s down to 0.35 s, a factor of ~31, and the compression is not even order-preserving: Pb-210, really 22.3 years against Ra-226's 1,600 (72× shorter-lived), is coded at 5.5 s against Ra-226's 5.0 — slightly longer-lived. Rn-222 sits nearly equal to its grandparent (4.8 vs 5.0 s) where reality puts them 150,000× apart. Consequences already measured in Receipt 3: no secular equilibrium, and equilibrium-style populations (which in nature run proportional to half-life) are illustrative only. The decay law is exact; the family's internal time ratios are a diorama's.

The same isotope keeps different time on different cards: Ra-226's sim half-life is 5.0 s inside the uranium chain but 10.5 s as the radium source; Rn-222 is 4.8 vs 6.2 s, Pb-214 3.4 vs 4.2, Bi-214 3.8 vs 4.5, Po-214 0.35 vs 0.30, Po-218 2.0 vs 2.2. Each source card was tuned to play well on its own; the price is that switching sources changes the physics of the isotopes they share, by up to a factor of two.

The chain is the main line only: real branches are pruned away. Protactinium-234m's 0.16% isomer transition, Po-218's 0.02% beta branch, and Bi-214's 0.021% alpha branch (through Tl-210) are absent — small, but they are the reason real chains are trees, not lines. Sodium-22's missing branch is not small: 9.6% of real Na-22 decays go by electron capture and produce no positron at all; in the game every Na-22 decay makes one.

Two chain ends are editorial: the Ra-226 showcase stops at Pb-210 and labels it the stable end of the line — Pb-210 is radioactive (22.3 yr), and the game's own uranium chain decays it onward through Bi-210 and Po-210 to Pb-206. Americium-241's daughter Np-237 is likewise frozen as the end; its real half-life (2.14 Myr) is honestly printed on the card, but in the sim it never decays, and its own 4n+1 family is not in the room.

The beta spectrum is too hard, and it leaks: the sampler draws each beta's energy as Q × (0.22 + 0.78·U·V + 0.25·W) with U, V, W uniform — measured over 60,000 draws: mean 0.539 Q, minimum 0.220 Q, maximum 1.243 Q, and 1.9% of electrons come out above the endpoint, which is impossible: the endpoint is the whole decay energy. Real allowed spectra peak at low energy, average roughly 0.35–0.4 Q, and stop dead at Q, the antineutrino carrying off the (here unmodeled) remainder. The code's comment claims an average of "~40% of Q-value"; the sampler as shipped delivers 54%. The sampler's 0.06 MeV floor also collides with the softest beta in the game: Pb-210's endpoint is 0.064 MeV, and 96.6% of its betas come out clamped to exactly 0.060 MeV — a nearly monoenergetic line where nature has a soft spectrum.

Beta ranges — and radii — are squeezed to fit the box: calibrate the pixel from the alphas (150 px ↔ 4.1 cm in air) and a Q = 1 MeV beta's mean path measures 161 px ≈ 4.4 cm, where a real electron of the same mean energy (0.54 MeV) runs about 1.5 m of air — roughly 35× farther. The beta's curl is also about twice as wide as that scale gives nature (measured R 19.1 px at 0.5 MeV against ~9 px), even though its κ ∝ q/p law is exact and the alpha's curl lands within 7%. Alphas are squeezed too, but gently: their range law E1.38 with a +42 px offset gives measured range ratios 1 : 1.29 : 1.85 across 4.20/5.49/7.69 MeV against the real E3/2 ratios 1 : 1.49 : 2.48 — high-energy alphas travel relatively shorter than they should. (The code's range comment, "4.2 MeV ~ 95 px," is stale besides: the shipped formula gives ~118 px, measured 116.)

Gammas are visible, and far too sociable: a real gamma leaves no track; the game draws it as a dashed ghost ray — a labeled toggle, and the only way to watch shielding work — and gives it a Compton mean free path of 2,727 px in vapor, where nature's is of order 100 metres of air: the interaction rate is boosted more than a hundred-fold (the code comment concedes the rates are "boosted slightly for museum visibility"). When a Compton event fires, the photon simply ends — no scattered photon continues at reduced energy, and the recoil electron's energy is a flat 25–70% of the photon's with no tie to a scatter angle, where the real Compton formula binds energy and angle together. Pair production conserves neither: the pair's kinetic budget is coded (E − 1.022)×0.9 + 0.35, so a 1.33 MeV photon's pair receives 0.63 MeV of motion where physics allows 0.31 — the +0.35 is minted from nothing, presumably so the V-spiral is lively enough to see.

Shield μ is energy-blind, and most gamma energies are a default: one attenuation coefficient per material serves all photon energies. It is calibrated near 1.3 MeV (lead: 85% absorbed in-game against roughly 80% for a real 25 mm at 1.33 MeV) but at 511 keV real lead absorbs ~99% where the game still absorbs ~85%, and the game's aluminum takes 19% where 4 mm of real aluminum at 1.33 MeV takes ~6%. The energies themselves: unless a line is explicitly coded (Bi-214's 1.76, Co-60's pair, Na-22's 1.275, Am-241's 0.06), every prompt gamma is drawn at a flat default of 0.85 MeV — Ra-226's real 186 keV line (3.6% of decays) becomes a 14–16% chance of an 0.85 MeV ray, and Th-234's real ~63–92 keV lines become 12% at 0.85. Early-chain gamma probabilities are museum-inflated (U-238: coded 5% against a real ~0.06% for its 49.5 keV line); the bottom of the chain is closer to life, though Bi-214 fires a single 1.76 MeV gamma where a real decay emits two to three gammas, the strongest at 0.609 MeV.

The counter counts pictures, and the pictures have a quota: Receipt 8 is the whole story — clicks follow spawned tracks and rays (1.17 per decay at 1×, muons clicking in the gamma voice, annihilation rays silent), and above the 9-tracks-per-frame cap, decays happen unheard and unseen while the statistics stay exact. At 8×, at least 23% of decays are display-only-in-the-counters. What the ear gets at high speed is a fair sample, not the census — the numbers in the HUD are the census.

Annihilation is eager, muons are weather: positrons annihilate the moment their energy falls to 0.045 MeV, with an extra 0.8%-per-step in-flight chance — 200 of 200 measured annihilated in view, where a real positron's in-flight annihilation over its whole track is a few-percent affair and the rest annihilate at rest in the material, often out of sight. The flashes themselves are exact (Receipt 7). Cosmic muons arrive at the coded 0.48 per second (measured 0.50) from all four edges; real cosmic muons rain downward only, at roughly one per cm² per minute — several per second through a chamber this size — so the game's sky is about ten times too quiet and rains sideways. Their near-straight tracks, at least, are honest: a labeled 3 GeV muon barely notices a 1.2 T field, here or anywhere.

Small print: delta rays on alpha tracks are decorative stubs — 28% of alpha points sprout one — not simulated knock-on electrons with their own little tracks. A nucleus can advance at most one chain step per frame, so at 8× a Po-214 nucleus (sim t½ 0.35 s) waits up to a frame extra before its own draw; the statistics stay exact, the cascade is imperceptibly polite. Supersaturation in a real chamber decides whether droplets nucleate at all; here it sets only how long finished tracks linger — and the slider is honestly labeled "Trail Life." The chamber's convection drifts finished tracks sideways, which real vapor does too, though seldom so picturesquely.
