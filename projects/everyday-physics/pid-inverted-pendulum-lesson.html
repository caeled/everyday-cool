Stabilizer — the lesson

Balance a broom on your palm and you are running the oldest hard problem in control theory: the upright position is an equilibrium, but an unstable one — every lean grows unless something measures it and pushes back, continuously, forever. This exhibit puts a PID controller on that problem three ways — a cart–pole, a quadcopter holding altitude and station, and a bare step-response bench — with pokes, wind, actuator limits, and a swing-up assist for when the pole has already fallen. This page is the other half: the physics of feedback, the math the loops run, and the audit that drove the shipped exhibit through its own presets and measured where its claims hold — and the two places they don't.

Verdict: The dynamics are the exact nonlinear Lagrangian model and the metrics engine is honest — but Swing-Up & Catch never catches, and the zero-order-held force quietly eats Kp·dt/2 of damping, so P-only ringing decays some 13× too slowly.

The physics

An equilibrium that falls away

A pendulum hanging down is stable: disturb it and gravity pulls it back. The same pendulum balanced upright is in equilibrium too — the forces balance exactly at θ = 0 — but the equilibrium is unstable: disturb it and gravity pulls it away, the lean feeding itself. Nothing passive can hold it there. What holds it is a loop: measure the angle, compute a correction, apply a force, measure again — hundreds of times a second, with no finish line. That loop, and nothing else, is the difference between a pole that stands and a pole that falls, which is why the inverted pendulum is control theory's benchmark plant: it cannot be faked, averaged, or open-looped.

Three terms, three tenses

A PID controller builds its force from the error — the gap between where the system is and where it should be — in three tenses. The proportional term answers the present: push in proportion to the lean, now. The integral term answers the past: sum the error over time, so a small stubborn offset — a steady wind, a spring's pull — eventually accumulates enough force to cancel it; without it, a proportional loop settles at a permanent offset, exactly the size theory predicts (Receipt 3). The derivative term answers the future: the lean is growing at some rate, so push against the rate too, arriving early. Each term has a characteristic failure the exhibit lets you trigger: proportional alone rings, integral winds up against an actuator limit and overshoots when it unwinds, and derivative taken on the error kicks violently when the target itself jumps.

Saturation, windup, and the catch

Real actuators have a ceiling — here, a force limit umax. While the loop is saturated, the integral term keeps summing error it cannot act on; when the pole finally comes back, that stored sum fires all at once as overshoot. That is integral windup, and anti-windup is the discipline of not summing what you cannot spend. And when the pole has fallen altogether, no PID tuned for the upright neighbourhood can help: recovery needs a different controller entirely — swing-up, pumping energy into the swing (the Åström–Furuta energy method) until the pole rises into the small window where the balancing loop can catch it. Two controllers, a handoff, and a window: |θ| < 20.6°, |ω| < 4.2 rad/s in this build. Whether the shipped swing-up ever delivers the pole to that window is Receipt 5.

The quadcopter is the same loop, nested

The drone mode stacks the idea: an altitude PID (with gravity feedforward, so the loop starts from hover thrust rather than discovering gravity by error) drives the total thrust, while a horizontal PID asks for a pitch angle, and an inner attitude loop delivers that pitch through per-rotor mixing. Loops inside loops — a cascade — each one a stabilizer for the variable the next one out treats as its actuator.

The math the game runs

The plant: exact nonlinear cart–pole dynamics

Lagrangian dynamics of cart + point-mass pole, integrated by RK4 at 1/240 s substeps Not a linearization around upright — the full nonlinear equations from the mass-matrix Lagrangian derivation, for M = 1.0 kg (cart), m = 0.35 kg (pole mass), L = 1.15 m. The audit integrated the same derivation independently and compared a free fall checkpoint by checkpoint (Receipt 1). The speed buttons scale the simulation clock exactly: 60 frames at 2× advance the sim by 2.00 s.

The controller

u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt,   |u| ≤ umax With the exhibit's switches choosing the derivative's source (on error, or on measurement — the difference is Receipt 4's 689.7 N kick versus 45.4 N) and anti-windup clamping the integral while saturated. On the bench, with no integral term, theory fixes the steady-state error against a spring of stiffness k and target r at exactly k·r/(Kp + k) — the audit's cleanest single number is in Receipt 3.

The hold between samples

u held constant across each substep (zero-order hold) ⇒ negative damping ≈ Kp·dt/2 The PID force is computed once per 1/240 s substep and held constant across the RK4 stages. A held force lags the motion it is meant to oppose, and that lag is worth a negative damping of Kp·dt/2 — ≈ 0.075 N·s/m at bench defaults. Nobody wrote that term into the physics; the sampling architecture writes it in by itself. Its measured cost is the honest box's headline, and Receipt 2's replica integrations pin it to four decimals.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit loaded the shipped page headlessly and drove it through its own interface — preset buttons, gain sliders, toggles, pokes — while reading the page's own telemetry, and checked the behaviour against an independent mass-matrix Lagrangian integrator and analytic control-theory results. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1: the dynamics are the exact nonlinear model

With all gains zeroed and swing-up off, the falling pole was compared against the independent integrator over 1.0 s of free fall through 154°: agreement of Δθ ≤ 0.6° and Δx = 0.00 m at every checkpoint. The plant the controller fights is the real cart–pole, not a cartoon of it. (The simplifications that ride on top of the exact core — point-mass pole, added friction, the wind split — are named in the honest box.)

Receipt 5 — FALSE: Swing-Up & Catch never catches

The dedicated "Swing-Up & Catch" preset — and the "Auto Swing-Up & Catch Assist" generally, which is also the recovery path offered after every fall — was driven for 90 s. In every 5 s window, the pole's closest approach to upright was 99.1°. The swing amplitude plateaus at ~81° in a limit cycle — nowhere near the catch window of |θ| < 20.6°, |ω| < 4.2 rad/s — while the cart wanders ±1.6 m. The same result came from recovering out of Drop with the assist toggled on mid-run: never caught. The Åström–Furuta energy formula is implemented as written in the code, but with the shipped pump gain (4.6), centring term (11.5/6.8), and friction, the pumped energy plateaus exactly where input balances losses — about 81° of swing, forever short of the top. The headline feature of the preset does not work as shipped. What still stands: with the assist off, Drop correctly stays Fallen at ±179°, and ordinary PID recovery from pokes and gusts is unaffected — this failure is specific to the swing-up path, and it is total there.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The zero-order hold eats damping — ≈ Kp·dt/2 of it: the PID force is computed once per 1/240 s substep and held constant across the RK4 stages, injecting negative damping of Kp·dt/2 ≈ 0.075 N·s/m at bench defaults. Verified to four decimals by replica integration: with per-stage force recomputation the bench decays at exactly theory — 0.175/s at c = 0.35 and 0.040/s at c = 0.08 — while as shipped it decays at 0.1375/s and 0.0027/s. The consequence lands on a named preset: the P-Only Wild Osc preset's "sustained ringing" is accidentally true. Its damper (c = 0.08) should eat 58% of the amplitude in 21 s; as shipped the amplitude falls only ~5% in 21 s (peaks 1.324 → 1.26 m over 40 s), because the hold artifact cancels the damper. The oscillation's frequency is exact (period 1.02 s, against a predicted 0.98 Hz); only the decay is wrong, by ~13×, in the "more sustained" direction. The bug is in the control hold, not the plant — the plant's own damping measured clean in Receipt 4.

"Critically Damped Drone" overshoots and has a long tail: on an altitude step 1.2 → 3.2 m the measured peak is 3.28 m — 4.0% overshoot (the page's own readout says 3.9%). "Locked Hover" status arrives at 1.6 s, but the page's settling-time readout for the same step says 12.16 s: a snap by the lock criterion, not critically damped by the response metrics.

Bench regime names are gain nicknames, not response classes: "Critical" (toast: "near-zero overshoot") measures 7.7–8.0% overshoot. "Overdamped" (toast: "sluggish crawl, zero oscillation") measures 9.9% overshoot — an overdamped response by definition has none; the hump is integral-driven. It is correctly the slowest to settle (9.65 s vs 3.92 s underdamped).

Anti-windup "fix" overstates: the windup preset's toast says toggle anti-windup ON "to fix" the overshoot; measured, it goes 60.7% → 18.7%. A 42-point improvement, not a fix — saturation at umax = 10 N still costs ~19%.

Stormy Wind Hover leans but cannot hold station: mean pitch −18°…−33°, leaning into the wind as claimed, and it never crashes — but over 60 s the mean 2D position error wanders 0.6–3.1 m from window to window, with status "Saturated" most of the time. The ±35.5° pitch cap is at or below what the gust peaks demand; steady wind alone needs ~31° of lean. "Hover" is doing heavy lifting in that preset's name.

Model simplifications under the "exact" banner: the Lagrangian core is exact for a point-mass pendulum — no rod inertia. Shipped extras: cart friction bx = 0.10, pivot friction bth = 0.012, and wind entering through ad-hoc splits (0.25 × Fwind on the cart, 0.45 × Fwind·L·cosθ as bob torque). Reasonable game physics; not an aerodynamic model.

The flagship preset's own overshoot readout is 53.2%: the angle channel treats the deliberate lean used to move the cart 1.2 m (θ swings to −8.4°) as overshoot. The balance itself is genuinely crisp — Balanced at 1.75 s — but the metric framing makes the tuned preset look sloppy on its own scope.
