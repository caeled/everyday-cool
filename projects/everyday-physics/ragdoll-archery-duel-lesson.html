Ragdoll Archery — the lesson

Two archers on stone towers, 690 pixels apart, taking turns with a bow. Every arrow flies a true parabola under one gravity and one wind; every archer is a skeleton of nine points held together by ten sticks, which is why a hit doesn't just subtract health — it shoves the body, staggers it, and at zero health lets the whole figure fold. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked shots computed from its own numbers, and how it is built.

The game was built with Google Antigravity and supplied by Steven; this lesson was written for Everyday Cool, from the game's own code.

The physics

An arrow is two motions wearing one coat

Once the arrow leaves the bow, its flight splits cleanly in two. Sideways, almost nothing touches it: there is no air resistance in this world, so the horizontal speed you launched with is the horizontal speed it keeps — the only sideways influence is the wind, a constant push. Vertically, gravity adds downward speed every instant, at the game's gravity of 560 px/s², whether the arrow is rising, hovering at the top of its arc, or falling. A speed that never changes, plus a speed that always grows: plotted out, that pair of motions draws a parabola, every time, with no exceptions in the code.

The consequence every duelist learns in their thumbs: the arrow is always falling from the moment it leaves. Aim straight at a distant archer and the arrow arrives below their feet. You aim above the target — you throw the arrow up a hill it will slide down, and arrange for the target to be standing where the falling half of the curve comes through. The longer the shot, the higher the aim, because drop grows with the square of the flight time: twice as long in the air is four times the fall.

Wind is a sideways gravity

Each turn the game rolls a wind — anywhere from −130 to +130 px/s², shown in the Duelists panel — and applies it to the arrow exactly the way gravity applies downward: a constant sideways acceleration for the whole flight. It does not gust and it does not fade, and it pushes a rising arrow exactly as hard as a falling one. A maximum wind acting over a long, high lob can shove an arrow sideways by nearly 190 px — more than a quarter of the Towers gap — which is why the flat, fast shot is the wind-proof one: it simply doesn't stay in the air long enough to be pushed around. (The archers themselves feel no wind at all. Only arrows do. See Simplifications.)

The ragdoll: a body that only knows lengths

Each archer is nine points — head, neck, pelvis, two knees, two feet, two hands — joined by ten sticks. Nothing in the body knows what a "body" is. Each point obeys gravity and keeps a memory of where it just was; each stick knows exactly one thing, its own length, and pulls its two ends back to that length whenever they drift apart or squeeze together. A knee bends because two sticks share a point. A body crumples convincingly because every joint is that ignorant: hit the head and the neck stick yanks the pelvis after it a moment later, the leg sticks fold at their shared points, and the collapse propagates through the skeleton link by link, the way a real fall does. There is no animation of falling anywhere in the file. Falling is what the points and sticks do when nobody holds them up.

What holds them up, while an archer is alive and unstunned, is a posture spring: every point is gently pulled toward its home position in the standing pose, 18% of the way there each physics step. A hit stuns — 0.6 seconds for a body hit, 1.15 for a headshot — and during the stun the spring switches off, so the skeleton staggers as an honest ragdoll until the timer ends and the pose reasserts itself. At zero health the spring never comes back on, and the figure folds for good, with a theatrical shove from the killing arrow to start the fall.

Penetration: if the tip touches, it sticks

Arrows in this game never bounce. The collision test is a single point — the arrow's tip — checked against circles around the body's nine points and against the stone platforms. The instant the tip touches, the arrow stops dead and embeds: in a platform it pins to the stone where it entered; in a body it records its offset from the struck point and rides that point from then on, travelling with the archer as they stagger, stumble, or fall off their tower. There is no speed threshold and no deflection — an arrow that barely lobs its way onto a foot sticks exactly as firmly as a full-draw shot. Speed decides how much damage company the hit brings (the knockback below), never whether the arrow stays.

The hit also transfers momentum, Verlet-style. A Verlet point's velocity isn't stored anywhere — it is the gap between where the point is and where it just was. So to push a point, the code reaches into its past: it moves the struck point's remembered previous position backward against the arrow's flight by a fraction of the arrow's velocity — 4.2% for a headshot, 2.8% for anything else, plus a small upward pop. Moving the past is how you push the future. A 623 px/s arrow (Example 1, below) shoves a struck head's past by about 26 px, which the skeleton experiences as a violent snap of the head — and the sticks then drag the rest of the body into the argument.

The math the game runs

The game's gravity is GRAVITY = 560 px/s², its bow turns a drag of up to 175 px into launch velocity at 5.2 px/s per pixel of pull — a maximum arrow speed of 910 px/s, with shots weaker than 90 px/s refused entirely. The whole flight is these formulas, which are also literally what the aiming guide draws:

x(t) = x₀ + vx·t + ½·wind·t²   ·   y(t) = y₀ + vy·t + ½·g·t² The arrow's position t seconds after release. The x-line has no gravity term and the y-line has no wind term — the two motions never mix. The dotted aim guide is this pair of equations evaluated at 26 instants, t = 0.045 s apart: the math itself, drawn as dots.
drop = ½·g·t²,   with t = distance / vx How far an arrow falls below its launch line. Over the 690 px Towers gap at a flat 460 px/s sideways, t = 1.5 s and the drop is ½ × 560 × 1.5² = 630 px — more than the whole 580 px height of the arena. That is the hole your upward aim has to fill.
R = v²·sin(2θ) / g The range of a shot launched at speed v and angle θ, landing at its launch height. Two angles always give the same range — a low road and a high road, sin(2θ) being equal for θ and 90° − θ. At full draw (910 px/s) and 45°, R = 910² / 560 ≈ 1,479 px: the bow can throw an arrow clear across the 1,000 px arena and out the other side.

Constraint satisfaction, in one paragraph. After gravity and momentum have moved the nine points wherever they want to go, the solver restores the body. It walks the list of ten sticks; for each stick it measures the current distance between its two points, compares it with the stick's rest length, and slides the two ends along the line between them — each end covering half the error for a rigid stick, or 18% of it for the two soft cross-links (head-to-pelvis, knee-to-knee, which exist to keep the spine from folding flat and the legs from scissoring). Fixing one stick disturbs its neighbours, so the whole walk repeats — 5 iterations per physics step, and the physics steps 4 times per frame. Every stick in your body is re-satisfied 20 times a frame, about 200 stick-fixes per archer per frame, and the crispness of the skeleton is bought with exactly that repetition.

Damage is arithmetic, not drama. Each archer has 100 HP, and the code's prices are flat — they do not scale with the arrow's speed:

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Example 1: the Towers shot, solved the way the AI solves it

Castle Towers puts both archers at the same height, 690 px apart. The AI opponent doesn't guess: it picks a flight time — its code chooses T between 1.35 and 1.70 s — and then solves the two position formulas backwards for the launch velocity that lands on your neck at exactly T. Take T = 1.5 s and no wind:

  • vx = 690 / 1.5 = 460 px/s sideways.
  • vy = (0 − ½ × 560 × 1.5²) / 1.5 = −630 / 1.5 = −420 px/s — upward, to pre-pay the 630 px of drop.
  • Launch speed = √(460² + 420²) = √388,000 ≈ 623 px/s, at 42.4° above horizontal — about 120 px of the 175 px maximum pull.
  • The arc peaks 420² / (2 × 560) = 157.5 px above the launch point, halfway across, then falls the rest of the way home.

That 42° lob is the honest shape of this duel: at these distances and this gravity, archery is artillery. The AI then spoils its own solution on purpose — it jitters the solved velocity by up to ±5.5% sideways and ±4.5% vertically, "human-like inaccuracy so it's fun & fair," in its own comment's words.

Example 2: the flat shot that can't work

Pull to the maximum — 910 px/s — and aim dead level at the other archer's head. The arrow crosses the 690 px gap in 690 / 910 ≈ 0.758 s, and in that time gravity pulls it down ½ × 560 × 0.758² ≈ 161 px. The whole archer, feet to crown, is about 82 px tall. The fastest arrow the bow can throw, fired flat, lands two full body-heights low — into the tower stone well beneath their feet. There is no draw strength that fixes this; only angle does. This is why the telemetry panel leads with the angle, not the velocity.

Example 3: two roads to the same archer

At 800 px/s toward a same-height target 690 px away, the range formula asks for sin(2θ) = 690 × 560 / 800² = 0.604, which has two answers:

RoadAngleFlight timeDrift in a max wind (130 px/s²)
Low road18.6°0.91 s½ × 130 × 0.91² ≈ 54 px
High road71.4°2.70 s½ × 130 × 2.70² ≈ 474 px

Same bow, same target, same landing point on a calm day. But the high road spends three times as long in the air, and wind drift grows with the square of that time — a strong crosswind moves the lob by more than half the gap, while the flat shot shrugs off 54 px. Choosing between the two roads is the game, and the wind badge in the corner is the price list.

How it's built — the coding choices

Two integrators in one file, each where it belongs

The arrows and the bodies are moved by different machinery, on purpose. Arrows carry explicit velocities (vx, vy) advanced by semi-implicit Euler: add the accelerations (wind sideways, gravity down), then move. An arrow's path is a clean parabola and Euler's error on it is invisible. The bodies use Verlet integration instead: no velocity is ever stored; each point's new position is its old position plus its displacement since the last step, damped by 0.985, plus gravity written directly as a displacement (g·dt²). Verlet is the right tool for a constraint skeleton — velocities emerge from positions, so when the stick solver moves a point, its velocity corrects itself for free, and the knockback trick of editing a point's remembered past becomes available. A body built on stored velocities would need every solver correction mirrored into the velocity by hand.

Substeps sized by the fastest thing in the world

The frame loop runs the physics 4 times per frame, and that number is not decorative. A full-draw arrow travels 910 px/s, which at 60 frames a second is 15 px per frame — wider than a limb's hit circle (radius 9 px) is across. Tested once per frame, arrows would tunnel clean through arms and legs. At 4 substeps the fastest arrow moves about 3.8 px per check, comfortably smaller than the smallest target, so the tip-point test can't skip over a body. The substep count is set by asking: what is the fastest object, and what is the smallest thing it must not pass through?

The AI is the lesson, wearing a opponent's colours

The computer archer contains no aiming heuristics at all. It picks a flight time, solves the same two kinematic equations printed above — including the wind term, vx = (dx − ½·wind·T²) / T — fires, and deliberately jitters the answer. Which means the duel is asymmetric in an instructive way: the AI plays by solving the formulas; the human plays by learning to feel them. The aim guide narrows the gap — it draws the exact parabola the equations predict, 26 dots over the first 1.17 s of flight — but it can't choose your road for you, and it stops drawing long before a high lob comes down.

Stuck arrows are bookkeeping, not physics

When an arrow embeds in a body, the code stores three things: which archer, which point, and the arrow's offset from that point at the moment of the hit. Every frame after, the arrow's position is simply set to the point's position plus the offset. No spring, no constraint, no simulation — the arrow has been demoted from a physical object to a label pinned on a bone, which is exactly why it rides the body so faithfully through a collapse. The cheapest solutions in a physics engine are the ones that stop simulating.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there is no air resistance on arrows — horizontal speed changes only by wind, never by drag. Wind is a single constant acceleration for the whole arena and the whole flight; it affects arrows only, never the bodies, and the drifting wind streaks on screen are decoration. Penetration has no speed threshold: any tip contact sticks, in bodies and in stone, and nothing ever bounces or passes through. Damage is flat per body part and ignores the arrow's speed entirely — speed buys knockback, not damage. (The hint bar's multipliers are rounded for display: it says 2.5× / 1× / 0.6×, where the code's actual prices, 68 / 32 / 18, work out to 2.125× / 1× / 0.5625×. The table above uses the code's numbers.) Only the arrow's tip is collision-tested; the shaft can overlap anything harmlessly. All nine body points have equal mass; joints have no angle limits, so knees and elbows can bend backwards, restrained only by the two soft cross-links. The decorative blood-and-spark particles fall at 420 px/s², not the world's 560 — a small cosmetic cheat so they linger. Gravity is one uniform number everywhere, and the world is a flat 2D plane throughout.
