01 / THE POOL TABLE

Angles in, angles out — and momentum, handed over.

A real pool shot is two physics lessons in one. The ghost line shows where the cue ball is headed: bounce it off a cushion and the incoming and outgoing angles are drawn and labelled — always equal. Hit another ball and the dashed gold line shows the direction the struck ball will take (along the line between the two balls' centres), while the fainter line shows what the cue ball keeps: only the sideways part of its speed. After every hit, the box below the table says in words what just happened.

Aim with the slider or by tapping the table where you want the cue ball to go, set the power, and shoot. Try a cushion first: aim up-right and watch the two angles come out equal.

The honest simplifications, stated plainly: there is no spin (no "english" on the cue ball) and no throw between balls; friction is a steady slow-down, the same share of speed lost each moment, rather than a real cloth's behaviour; and all the balls weigh the same, which is what makes the momentum hand-over so clean. Balls can be potted — a potted cue ball (a scratch) comes back to the head spot — but there are no turns, fouls, or scoring: the teaching overlays are the game here. Speeds are in the table's own units (pixels per frame), and the page says so rather than dressing them up as metres.

02 / THE BALLISTICS LAUNCHER

Every flight is the same curve.

Set the angle, set the speed, launch. The flight draws itself dot by dot and stays on the range when it lands, so you can compare shots. A stack of crates waits at 45 metres. The plain truths the launcher teaches: gravity bends every flight into the same curve, whatever you throw; at the same speed, 45° goes farthest; steeper trades distance for height; and 30° and 60° — partners around 45° — land in the same place. This range is drawn in real metres and seconds, with real gravity (9.8 m/s²) and no air resistance — stated plainly, because a real ball would be slowed and shortened by the air, and this room's world has none.

Set the angle and speed, and launch. A hint for the crates: at 20 m/s and 45°, the sums promise about 40.8 metres — close, but not quite. What does it take?

03 / FLIGHT

Paper planes: the glider's bargain.

The launcher above throws things, and gravity bends every flight into the same curve. A paper plane is that curve with wings on — and no engine. Once your hand lets go, nothing pushes it, so it pays for every metre it travels with a little of its height: a paper plane is a glider, trading height for speed from launch to landing. The fold sets the price of the trade. Fold all three planes below, weigh their wings on the bench, and test them the way the paper airplane lab prescribes — three flights, one change, the middle score.

The Dart

Fast, straight, and short

  1. Lay the sheet portrait — long edges at the sides. Fold it in half lengthwise, crease hard with a thumbnail, then unfold. That centre crease is the ruler every later fold answers to.
  2. Fold the two top corners in to the centre crease. The top becomes a point — the nose.
  3. Fold the two new slanted edges in to the centre crease as well. The point grows longer and sharper.
  4. Fold the whole plane in half along the centre crease, with all the folds on the outside.
  5. Fold one side down to make a wing: the fold runs so the wing's edge meets the bottom edge (the spine). Turn the plane over and fold the second wing to match the first.
  6. Open the wings until they sit level, or tilt up just a touch. Sight along the nose: the wings should be twins. Throw it smooth and level, about as hard as you would toss a dart at a board.

What it does well: the fastest, straightest flier of the three. It punches through room air that bullies the glider, and down a hallway it is the distance plane.

Trim fixes: dives → bend the back edges of the wings up a hair, both sides the same. Climbs and flops → it is stalling: throw flatter, or add one small paper clip at the nose. Veers → the wings do not match; even them up before touching anything else.

Honest line: a crease one millimetre off-centre is a steering input nobody ordered. Two darts from the same sheet still fly differently — folding accuracy moves a dart's results more than any design tweak, and humid paper folds soft and holds its shape worse.

The Glider

Wide wings, slow, and it floats

  1. Centre crease first: fold the sheet in half lengthwise, crease hard, then unfold.
  2. Fold the two top corners in to the centre crease — a house shape with a pointed roof.
  3. Fold the point straight down, so the tip lands about two finger-widths above the bottom edge. This folded-down nose is the glider's built-in nose weight — remember it when the trim doctor talks about balance.
  4. Fold the plane in half along the centre crease, with the folded-down nose on the outside.
  5. Make wide wings: leave a spine about two fingers wide and fold each side down, taking as much wing as the sheet will give. Broad wings are the whole design.
  6. Bend the last centimetre of each wingtip straight up — winglets. Then launch with a gentle, level push, as if setting a tray down on air. A glider is pushed, not thrown.

What it does well: the floater — slow, stately, the longest time in the air of the three. In dead-calm air it can cross a gym in one long sigh.

Trim fixes: dives → back edges up a hair, both sides. Porpoises — climbs, stalls, dives, repeats → your push is too hard before it is the plane's fault: soften the launch, then flatten half of any up-bend. Sinks fast and straight → the wings may be too narrow or drooping; re-crease the wing folds sharp.

Honest line: a glider this light flies in weather you cannot see — a heating vent, a doorway draft, someone walking past. On a humid day the paper itself goes soft and the wide wings droop mid-flight; the same fold that floated at breakfast can sink by evening.

The Stunt plane

A dart with a trick tail — it loops

  1. Centre crease: fold the sheet in half lengthwise, crease hard, then unfold.
  2. Fold the two top corners in to the centre crease — the pointed nose.
  3. Fold the two new slanted edges in to the centre crease again, as on the dart. The point sharpens.
  4. Fold the plane in half along the centre crease, folds on the outside.
  5. Fold the wings down a little narrower than the dart's, leaving a slightly deeper spine — a stunt plane wants a sturdier body than a racer.
  6. The trick: bend the back edge of each wing UP, about a thumbnail's width, exactly the same on both sides. Those bent-up edges are elevators — the same control surface real planes loop with.
  7. Sight along the nose and check the twins match. Then throw gently, aimed a little above level, indoors or in dead calm.

What it does well: loops — the party trick with real aerodynamics inside it. The bent-up tail trades the plane's speed for climb until it tips over the top and comes down the other side.

Trim fixes: won't loop → deepen both bends a hair and throw a touch harder. Loops too tight and falls out of the top → flatten half the bend and throw softer. Rolls instead of looping → the bends have stopped matching; even them before anything else.

Honest line: the stunt plane is the most fold-sensitive of the three — a thumbnail's width is its whole control range, so a bend that is "about the same" on both sides usually is not. Humid paper will not hold a crisp bend at all; on a damp day, loop a different plane.

Four forces at paper scale

Lift, weight, thrust, drag — one of them quits immediately

Thrust is your arm, and it lasts about a second. After that, nothing pushes the plane at all — the shortest-lived force in the room.

Weight is gravity pulling the plane down for the whole flight — and it doubles as the engine: sliding down a shallow, invisible ramp, the plane's own weight is what keeps the air moving over its wings.

Lift is the wings' answer to that moving air, pushing up about as hard as the weight pulls down whenever the glide runs steady.

Drag is the air pushing back — a tax on every metre, doubled by crumpled noses and flapping edges.

In a steady glide, lift and weight sit nearly tied, and drag is paid out of height: it is gravity's one curve from the workshop below, stretched long and flat. And a wing only earns its lift while it holds its shape — wings are structures, and structures are Everyday Engineering's home ground.

Thrust quits in a second; the other three argue the whole way down

The wing-loading bench

Spread a plane's weight over its wing and one number predicts its character: wing loading, grams per square decimetre (1 dm² = 100 cm²). It does not say how far a plane flies — it says how. The wing areas here are honest estimates for these three folds from one sheet, measured off the finished planes and good to about a fifth either way; the paper weights are the standard sums, stated as about-values.

Balance & trim

The balance point. Rest the plane on a fingertip under its wings: it should balance around the front third of the wing. Nose-heavy planes dive; tail-heavy planes stall. The fix that always works is a small coin — or a paper clip — taped at the nose to bring the balance forward: one coin, three throws, then judge.

Dihedral. Wings tilted up in a shallow V make a plane self-levelling: dip one wing and that wing meets the air more squarely, lifts harder, and rolls the plane back level. Flat wings fly, but they forgive nothing.

Elevator trim. The wings' back edges are the elevators. Bent up a hair, they push the tail down and the nose up; flattened, they let the nose drop. A hair is a real unit here — a thumbnail's width is a big adjustment.

The test-throw ritual. Throw gently. Watch the whole flight. Change ONE thing. Throw again — three throws before you judge anything. It is the troubleshooter's rule: a single flight is an anecdote; the pattern across throws is the knowledge.

Pick a symptom, and the doctor prescribes one change.

The honest summary for the whole section: the design sets the range of what is possible, but fold accuracy and the paper itself — crisp on a dry day, soft and droopy on a humid one — decide where inside that range any given plane lands. Two planes folded from the same instructions are two different aircraft. That is not a flaw in the lab; that is the lab.

The paper plane, with an engine. Everything above glides because a folded plane has no engine. A commercial kit called PaperFlight ↗ clips a small twin-prop motor unit and a real two-stick remote onto the same one sheet of paper — and it steers the way this room would predict: there is no rudder. One prop spins faster than the other and the plane banks, the differential-thrust trick a twin-engine pilot uses. When it crashes, the motor unclips, survives, and you fold a new airframe — their line is "every crash is a reason to fold a new one," which is the test-throw ritual wearing a price tag. A product, not a lesson; listed here because it is this bench, built. (Spotted by Steven, November 2026 — one is on its way to two boys in Defiance.)

Ready to leave the atmosphere? Stage It → — the planes above trade height for speed because they have no engine; a rocket carries its engine and its fuel, and pays for both. Split the fuel between two stages, drop the empty one on time, and make orbit — the rocket equation decides whether any split can.

04 / PENDULUM & SEESAW

Two small benches, two stubborn rules.

The pendulum

A pendulum's swing is timed mostly by the length of its string. Not the bob's weight. For small, honest swings, width hardly matters; widen the swing and the period runs a little long — this bench uses the standard large-angle correction so you can see that. Lengthen the string and the swing slows, by the square root: four times the string, twice the time.

The pendulum is swinging.

The seesaw

A seesaw balances on a multiplication: weight × distance on one side against weight × distance on the other. That is why a small weight far out balances a big weight close in — distance is leverage. Here a 40 kg weight faces a 20 kg weight; slide them and find the balances.

The seesaw is settling.

05 / EXPERIMENTS

Four for the kitchen table.

Nothing here flies at a person, burns, or needs more height than a chair. Each card lists what you need, the steps, what it shows, and its safety line — read the line before starting, not after.

The balloon rocket

Newton's third law, on a string

You need: a long string (3–5 m); a drinking straw; a balloon; two chairs; tape.

  1. Thread the string through the straw, tie it between the two chairs, and pull it tight and level.
  2. Blow up the balloon and pinch the neck shut — do not tie it. Tape the balloon to the straw, neck facing one chair.
  3. Slide the straw to one end, count down, and let go.
  4. Measure how far it travelled. Blow it up more, or less, and run it again — one change at a time.

What it shows: the air rushes out backwards, and the balloon is pushed forwards — every action has an equal and opposite reaction. That push is the whole of rocket science; everything else is engineering.

Safety: balloons pop — keep faces back at the moment of release, and pick up every scrap afterwards, because balloon pieces are a choking hazard for small children and a hazard for pets.

The egg drop

Momentum, stretched over time

You need: raw eggs (expect casualties); a box or container per design; padding candidates — newspaper, sponges, straws, bubble wrap, an old towel; a bin bag for the drop zone; a chair or a doorstep for height.

  1. Agree the rules: the egg rides inside your design, dropped from chair height onto the bin bag. No parachutes of bedsheets — keep designs hand-sized.
  2. Build a cradle whose job is to make the stop take longer: crushable layers, springs of rolled paper, a hammock of tape.
  3. Drop. Open carefully over the bag. Egg intact: raise the height a step and go again. Egg broken: find where the force got through, and change exactly that.
  4. Keep score by height survived, not by prettiness.

What it shows: the egg's momentum at landing is fixed by the drop height — what a good cradle changes is the time the stop takes. Spread the same momentum change over more time and the force shrinks below shell-breaking. It is the pool table's lesson in reverse: hand the momentum over gently.

Safety: drops happen outdoors or over the bag, from a chair at most — nobody climbs for extra height. Raw egg on hands means a wash afterwards, and broken shells go in the bin, not the compost.

The paper airplane lab

One change at a time

You need: several sheets of the same paper; a tape measure or a hallway with counted floor tiles; a notebook; paper clips.

  1. Fold your best plane. Fly it three times from the same spot, same throw, and write down all three distances — the middle one is your plane's honest score.
  2. Change exactly one thing: clip on one paper clip at the nose. Three more flights, same throw. Better or worse?
  3. Take the clip off. Change one other thing — bigger wings, a folded-up tail edge, a blunter nose. Three flights again.
  4. After four or five rounds, your notebook knows things about your plane that guessing never would.

What it shows: this is the scientific method wearing a paper plane: a fair test changes one thing, repeats the measurement, and trusts the middle result over the best one. Weight at the nose moves the balance point; wing shape changes the lift; the notebook separates what works from what merely flew well once.

Safety: planes fly away from faces, and the throwing line is behind the flyer — nobody walks into the flight path to fetch early.

Pendulum painting

The swing draws itself

You need: string (about a metre); a paper cup; washable paint thinned with water; a big sheet of paper or an old sheet; a doorway, broom across two chairs, or a helpful adult's held hand as the pivot; a pin to prick the cup.

  1. Prick a small hole in the bottom of the cup, cover it with a finger, and half-fill the cup with thinned paint.
  2. Hang the cup from the string so it swings just above the paper, centred on the sheet.
  3. Pull the cup to one side — straight, not in a circle — uncover the hole, and let go. Catch the drips' pattern, not the cup.
  4. Now start it with a tiny sideways nudge as you release, and watch the pattern change into loops and ellipses. Shorten the string and paint again: the pattern tightens as the swing quickens.

What it shows: a pendulum left alone draws straight back-and-forth lines, because it keeps its plane of swing; add a sideways push and two swings at once braid into Lissajous patterns. And the string-length rule from the bench above is visible in the paint: shorter string, quicker swing, tighter pattern.

Safety: this is a paint-spill machine by design — it happens over the sheet, outdoors or on a washable floor, in clothes that do not matter. Washable paint only.

07 / HOW PHYSICS WORKS HERE

Five ideas that run the whole room.

Reflection

Angle in = angle out

A ball bouncing off a cushion keeps the part of its motion that runs along the cushion and flips the part that ran into it — so the angle it arrives at is the angle it leaves at, measured from the cushion's face. Light does the same off a mirror; sound does it off a wall. One rule, three worlds.

The cushion flips one part, keeps the other

Momentum hands over

Nothing is lost in a hit

Momentum — mass × velocity — is never created or destroyed in a collision, only passed around. Between equal balls the passing has a beautiful shape: the balls swap the parts of their speed pointing along the line between their centres, and each keeps its sideways part. A head-on hit swaps everything, so the striker stops dead.

Swapped along the line, kept across it

Gravity's one curve

The parabola

A thrown thing moves sideways at a steady pace while gravity pulls its height down faster and faster — two simple motions stacked, and the path they draw is always a parabola. Speed sets the size of the curve, angle sets its shape, and 45° balances height against distance for the longest flight (in a world with no air).

Steady across, falling down

The pendulum's one variable

Length first; width when the swing is wide

For small swings, a pendulum's period is 2π × √(length ÷ gravity). Weight is not in the sum. Swing width almost isn't — until the swing gets wide, when the period runs a little long. This bench shows that correction. The stubbornness that remains is what made pendulum clocks possible: build the length once, and a small swing keeps time whatever the bob is made of.

T = 2π√(L/g), stretched a little when the swing is wide

Torque

Weight × distance

A push far from a pivot counts for more than the same push close in. That product — force × distance — is torque, and it runs the seesaw, the spanner that finally turns the stiff bolt, and every door handle ever placed far from its hinges.

Distance is leverage

The pool table

Ghost lines, arrows, and the readout

Aim with the angle slider or by tapping the table; set the power; press Shoot. While the balls rest, the ghost line draws the shot before you take it — the reflected path and equal angles at a cushion, or the struck ball's direction (gold) and the cue ball's leftover path (faint) at a ball. Step is for watching collisions

The ballistics launcher

Dots, comparisons, and the crates

The dots are drawn from the sums

Flight — paper planes

Folds, loading, and the trim doctor

The pendulum

Length in, period out

The seesaw

Two sliders and a multiplication
