Prism Bench — the lesson

Prism Bench is an optics puzzle box: a laser, a handful of mirrors, prisms, lenses and glass fiber blocks, and sensors that only light up when the right colour of light reaches them — all of them at once. Splitting white light onto a red sensor and a violet sensor simultaneously means there is no paint anywhere in this game: the rainbow has to come out of the physics. This page is the other half of it: the optics the bench runs, the audit that drove its own refraction function against the textbook formulas, and the receipts — including the one place where the game's rainbow is turned up louder than nature's.

Prism Bench was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its optics core headlessly. The game even ships a verification hook — window.__PRISM_BENCH_PHYSICS__ — exposing its own physics functions "for automated physics checks." The audit took it up on the offer.

The physics

Snell's law: light bends because it is in a hurry

The plainest way to say it: light travels at different speeds in different materials, and it always takes the fastest route between two points — Fermat's principle of least time. When the fast route crosses a boundary into a slower material, the cheapest path is no longer the straight one, and the ray bends. The bookkeeping is Snell's law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n is the refractive index (n = c/v — how much slower than vacuum the light runs) and both angles are measured from the normal, the line perpendicular to the surface — never from the surface itself. Entering glass (n ≈ 1.5) from air, the ray bends toward the normal; leaving, it bends away. Every bend on the bench — prism faces, lens surfaces, the fiber block — is this one equation, applied at every surface, in both directions.

Total internal reflection: the angle where the exit closes

Going from dense to rare (glass to air), the exiting ray bends away from the normal — and geometry runs out: at some incidence angle the refracted ray would have to leave at 90°, skimming along the surface. Past that critical angle, total internal reflection, there is no refracted ray at all. All of the light reflects back inside, perfectly. For glass at n = 1.5 the critical angle is asin(1/1.5) = 41.8°. This is not a curiosity — it is the entire working principle of optical fiber: light injected into a glass strand at a shallow enough angle can never leave through the walls, so it zigzags to the far end. The bench's Fiber Trap level is exactly that machine, and the audit ran a ray through it (Receipt 4).

Fresnel: every surface keeps a cut

Refraction is not the whole story at a boundary. Some of the light always reflects, and how much is set by the Fresnel equations. Head-on into glass, the reflected share is ((n−1)/(n+1))² = 4% — that's why a window shows you a faint ghost of yourself. The share climbs as the angle steepens, reaching 100% at grazing incidence, and at one special angle (Brewster's, 56.3° for glass) the reflected light loses one of its two polarizations entirely. The bench models the unpolarized average of the two Fresnel coefficients and — this is the part games usually skip — it traces both children: at every glass surface the ray splits into a reflected ray carrying R of the intensity and a transmitted ray carrying T = 1 − R, and both keep going. The faint ghosts and double images you see around the prisms are not decoration; they're the Fresnel bookkeeping, drawn.

Dispersion: blue bends more because it runs slower

The index n is not one number per material — it depends on wavelength. In glass, shorter wavelengths are slowed slightly more, so violet light (410 nm) sees a higher n than red (650 nm) and bends further at every surface. Two bends through a prism fan white light into a spectrum. The bench models this with a Cauchy equation: n(λ) = nbase + B·(1/λ² − 1/0.589²), with B growing for denser glass — flint spreads a wider rainbow than crown, in the game as in the glass catalogue. The direction and the mechanism are nature's; the volume is turned up, and Receipt 3 measures by how much.

The math the game runs

One function, computeSnellFresnel, is the whole optics department. It takes a ray direction, a surface normal, and the two indices, and returns the reflected direction, the refracted direction, and the Fresnel split — using the vector form of Snell's law:

t = η·d + (η·cos θi − cos θt)·n,   η = n₁/n₂,   sin²θt = η²(1 − cos²θi) If sin²θt ≥ 1 there is no transmitted ray — that's the TIR test, and it falls out of the same arithmetic rather than being a special case bolted on. The Fresnel share is the unpolarized mean R = (Rs + Rp)/2 of the two amplitude coefficients, and T = 1 − R. Rays are traced breadth-first through up to 24 bounces, and a ray dies when its intensity drops below 0.025 — the bench's own honesty card states both cutoffs, and the code matches the card.

Lenses get no shortcut either. A biconvex lens is two circular arcs; the tracer intersects the ray with each arc exactly, takes the surface normal from the arc's own centre, and applies the same Snell–Fresnel function at entry and at exit. There is no focal-point deflection cheat anywhere in the file — the focus is wherever two honest refractions put it, spherical aberration included (Receipt 5).

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's optics core out of the shipped file verbatim — Cauchy function, Snell–Fresnel function, lens geometry, the full scene tracer — and drove it against independent textbook computations. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page.

Violet's index is higher at every wavelength and for every glass, so violet bends more — the rainbow is computed, not painted. Driven through a 60° flint prism at 55° incidence (both surfaces, shipped function), red deviates 52.84° and violet 65.76°, matching angle-form Snell to 4 × 10−14°. But compare the spreads with real glass over the same 650→410 nm band: crown's red-to-violet spread is ≈ 0.018 and dense flint's (SF6) ≈ 0.066–0.068, against the game's 0.055 and 0.099 — so the game's flint runs about 1.5× nature, and its base glass about 3× a real crown. The fan between red and violet leaving that prism is 12.9° — a real flint prism gives a few degrees. The game's dispersion is nature's mechanism, turned up, which is exactly what a rainbow needs to fit on a 1000-pixel bench with sensors a hand's breadth apart. The lesson names it because the game doesn't.

Receipt 4: the fiber really traps light

The audit fired the tracer at a glass slab (n = 1.64, the Fiber Trap block) angled so the ray entered through the end face at 55° and struck the walls at 60° from the normal — safely past the 37.57° critical angle. The ray zigzagged the length of the block, top wall, bottom wall, top, bottom, and left through the far end face still carrying 0.802 of its launch intensity. The losses along the way are all accounted: 9.4% Fresnel reflection at entry (the equations at 55°, not a fudge), then seven total-internal-reflection events recorded by the tracer across the main ray and its own faint Fresnel ghost, which the bench faithfully bounces back down the fiber at 8% strength. Nothing about the trapping is scripted — it's the TIR test of Receipt 2, applied at every wall.

The lensmaker's formula lands between the two measured crossings, and the 15.5-unit gap between them is spherical aberration — the genuine, well-known failure of spherical surfaces to bring marginal and paraxial rays to one point, emerging here from nothing but Snell's law applied twice. Each ray also arrives carrying 0.910 of its launch intensity: two surfaces × ~4.6% Fresnel loss at these angles, compounding exactly as the equations say. The Lens Focus level asks for 3.2 units of power in a 14-unit-radius sensor; five rays converging through a ~15-unit blur circle is precisely the physics that makes the level winnable but not free.

Receipt 6: the game can't be banked

Sensor credit in the code is a wavelength test, sensorMatchesWavelength: a ray adds its intensity to a sensor's received power only if its wavelength falls in the sensor's band — red light on the violet sensor does nothing at all. A sensor locks when its charge reaches full, charge grows at 1.45/second while the sensor is receiving enough power, and decays at 1.9/second the moment it isn't. Decay outruns growth, and the level completes only in a frame where every sensor stands locked — so a sensor fed a second ago has already slipped below full before you can swing the prism to feed the next. "All sensors at once," as pitched, is what the arithmetic enforces.

How it's built — the coding choices

One function as the source of truth

Prisms, slabs, and lenses are three different intersection problems (polygon edges, rectangles, circular arcs) feeding one identical physics function. The geometry code's only job is to hand computeSnellFresnel a hit point and an outward normal; whether the ray entered a prism or a lens is forgotten by the time the physics runs. That's why the audit could verify the physics once and trust it everywhere — and why the lens's spherical aberration is real: nobody told the lens where its focus was.

Breadth-first light

Because every glass surface forks a ray into two, the tracer keeps a queue: initial rays first, then children in the order they were spawned, each carrying its wavelength, its intensity, and its bounce count. The 24-bounce cap and the 0.025 intensity floor keep the fork tree finite, and the white-light emitter simply enqueues nine rays — one per spectrum wavelength — down the same path. White light is not a type of ray in this engine; it's nine monochromatic rays that happen to share a launch, which is why they can be un-shared by a prism.

The medium is a question, asked at every surface

Instead of tracking "inside glass" as a state, the tracer asks, at each hit, what material lies a hair to either side of the surface — sampling the scene geometry on both sides of the boundary. Entering, leaving, and the awkward case of nested or touching elements all reduce to the same two samples. It's the same discipline as the rest of the bench: derive the situation from the world, don't carry a flag that can disagree with it.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate as far as it goes: geometric rays only (no diffraction or interference), a flat 2D bench, a discrete 9-wavelength spectrum instead of a continuous one, unpolarized Fresnel splitting (the two polarizations are averaged, not tracked — so Brewster's polarizing trick shows up in the numbers but can't be played with), no bulk absorption or scattering, and the 24-bounce / intensity cutoffs are stated on the card and match the constants in the code.

Dispersion runs hot: the Cauchy spread is 0.055 for the base-1.50 glass and 0.099 for the base-1.68 flint, against ≈ 0.018 and ≈ 0.066–0.068 in the real materials over the same band — the flint about 1.5× nature, the base glass about 3× — a 12.9° rainbow fan where nature gives a few degrees. It's a bench-visibility choice, but it's the game's biggest silent liberty, and it's measured in Receipt 3.

Sensor "colours" are bands, and the neighbours count: the red filter accepts everything ≥ 605 nm — including the 615 nm ray the palette names Orange; violet accepts ≤ 445 nm, including 435 nm Indigo; green (505–570 nm) takes 555 Lime but refuses 585 Yellow; blue (445–495 nm) takes 495 Cyan. In Levels 2 and 5 this is forgiving in a physically sensible way — real filters have passbands too — but "red sensor" means "red band," not "the red ray."

The critical angle has a wavelength, and the readout shows only one: inside the simulation the TIR boundary is per-wavelength — in base-1.50 glass, red traps at 42.08° but violet already traps at 40.26°, and at 41° the audit watched violet reflect while red escaped. The inspector panel, though, prints a single θc computed from the base index (41.8° at n = 1.50). The physics is righter than the label: real glass does exactly this, but a player trusting the printed number for violet light will be off by a degree and a half.

Two small losses the "lossless glass" card doesn't mention: mirrors return 98.5% per bounce and each total internal reflection keeps 99.2% — quiet anti-loop fudges that stop infinite ray ping-pong from glowing forever. The bulk glass is indeed lossless; the surfaces have these two small prints. (The Fresnel surface losses, by contrast, are the real equations, fully counted.)

There is no water on this bench: the only media are air (n = 1.0) and each glass element's own index. The Snell function itself is general — the audit verified it at n = 1.33 — but no element, level, or pool uses anything but glass and air.
