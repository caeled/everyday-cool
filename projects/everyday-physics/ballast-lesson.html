Ballast: the physics behind the game

The game is the front door — you play it by feel, on the gauges. This page is the back door: the same physics written out in words, sums, and worked numbers, so the feel has something solid underneath it. Everything here is the game's own arithmetic. Nothing on this page describes a different, better simulation than the one you are playing.

Play Ballast → Three dives: hold a depth on neutral buoyancy, set down gently on a wreck, and cross a layer of denser water that changes the rules mid-dive. This lesson also opens inside the game — the 📖 Lesson button pauses the dive while you read.

The game in one paragraph

Ballast puts you at the controls of a small submarine whose only real control is its weight. Flood the ballast tanks and the sub gets heavier and sinks; blow them dry and it gets lighter and rises. A propeller moves you sideways through the water, and dive planes nudge you — but only while water is flowing over them. Across three dives you learn the one skill submariners actually practise: trim — setting the tanks so the sub weighs exactly what the water around it weighs, and hanging motionless in mid-water on nothing but arithmetic.

The physics

Archimedes' principle, in plain words

Push anything under water and the water pushes back. Archimedes' principle says the push is exactly the weight of the water the thing pushes aside — the water that would be sitting where the thing now is. A hull that takes up 10 cubic metres of ocean is held up by the weight of 10 cubic metres of ocean, no more and no less. It does not matter what the hull is made of, what shape it is, or how deep it sits. The water only knows how much of itself is missing.

You don't steer a submarine up and down

An aeroplane climbs because its wings lift it. A submarine has no such trick at rest. It goes down by making itself heavier than the water it displaces, and comes up by making itself lighter. The ballast tanks are the whole mechanism: flood them and seawater pours in, adding its weight to the boat; blow them and air pushes that water out again. The sub is never lifted or pulled — it is forever falling, slowly, in whichever direction its weight tells it to. The water decides; the crew only negotiates.

Neutral buoyancy is a knife-edge, not a plateau

When the sub's mass exactly equals the mass of its displaced water, the two forces cancel and it hangs where it is: neutral buoyancy. Notice there is no restoring force. Nothing pushes a slightly-too-heavy sub back up to the balance point — it just keeps sinking, gently, forever, until something stops it. Neutral is not a valley the sub settles into; it is a knife-edge it balances on, and real submariners re-trim constantly because the balance keeps wandering. The game measures this honestly, and the worked examples below put a number on how sharp the edge is.

Dive planes are little wings — and wings need weather

The planes on a submarine's bow and stern are small wings. Tilted, they deflect the water flowing past and the reaction pushes the boat up or down. But a wing in still water does nothing at all: no flow, no force. In the game the plane force is proportional to forward speed — at 2 m/s ahead, full planes give about 680 N, like instantly adding or removing 69 kg of tank water; at a standstill they give exactly zero. That is why the controls label them need flow, and why planes are a way to hold depth while travelling, never a substitute for trim when hovering.

Pressure grows with depth — and crush depth is where it wins

Water is heavy, and the deeper you go the more of it is stacked on top of you. Pressure grows by roughly one extra atmosphere for every 10 metres down. At the game's crush line of 165 m, the sea is pressing with about sixteen atmospheres of extra pressure — roughly seventeen kilograms of force on every square centimetre of hull. A hull is a pressure vessel run in reverse: the ocean is trying to squash it flat, and it holds only because it was built round and strong. Past its rated depth, it doesn't leak politely. It fails all at once. That is what the red line on the game's depth ruler means.

Density layers: the water itself changes

Seawater is not the same everywhere. Cold water is denser than warm water; salty water is denser than fresher water. Where two water masses meet, the change can be stacked in a layer — a thermocline (temperature) or halocline (salt) — and a submarine crossing it passes from one fluid into a slightly different one. Denser water buoys the same hull harder. So a sub trimmed perfectly neutral above the layer floats up below it, with nobody touching a valve — and has to flood more water just to stay the same. Buoyancy is not a property of the sub. It is a negotiation between the sub and whatever water it happens to be in, and the water can renegotiate without asking.

The math

F = ρ · V · g
and the game's whole vertical story in one line:
net force = (ρ · V − msub) · g

Taken apart symbol by symbol:

  • ρ (rho) — the density of the water, in kilograms per cubic metre. Ordinary seawater in this game: 1,025 kg/m³. Below the layer: 1,050 kg/m³.
  • V — the volume of water the hull displaces: 10 m³, fixed. ρ · V is therefore the mass of the pushed-aside water, in kilograms.
  • g — gravity, 9.81 m/s². Multiplying a mass by g turns it into a weight — a force, in newtons. So F = ρ·V·g is the water's upward push as a force.
  • msub — the sub's own mass: the hull's 9,635 kg plus whatever water is currently in the tanks. The tanks hold 1.2 m³, filled with the water the sub is sitting in, so tank water mass = fill × ρ × 1.2.

If the bracket (ρ·V − msub) is positive, the sub is lighter than its displaced water and rises. Negative, it sinks. Zero, it hangs. Every frame, that is the entire calculation — plus drag, which only ever opposes motion and is covered under How it's built.

The game's own constants, worked through

QuantityValue
Displaced volume V10 m³
Hull mass (tanks empty)9,635 kg
Ballast tank capacity1.2 m³
Ordinary seawater ρ1,025 kg/m³
Layer water ρ1,050 kg/m³
Gravity g9.81 m/s²

In ordinary seawater, the hull pushes aside:

displaced water mass = ρ · V = 1,025 × 10 = 10,250 kg upward force = 10,250 × 9.81 ≈ 100,553 N

For neutral buoyancy the sub must mass exactly 10,250 kg. The hull already accounts for 9,635 kg, so the tanks must carry the difference:

tank water needed = 10,250 − 9,635 = 615 kg a full tank of this water masses = 1,025 × 1.2 = 1,230 kg neutral fill = 615 ÷ 1,230 = 0.500 → exactly 50%

That is why the tank gauge's green marker sits at half in the first two levels, and why the game's win text says "10,250 kg against 10,250 kg." Now the same sum in the layer's denser water:

displaced water mass = 1,050 × 10 = 10,500 kg tank water needed = 10,500 − 9,635 = 865 kg a full tank of layer water masses = 1,050 × 1.2 = 1,260 kg neutral fill = 865 ÷ 1,260 = 0.6865 → about 68.7%

Same sub, same tanks, different water — and neutral has moved by nearly a fifth of the gauge. The marker on the hull gauge is recomputed from the water the sub is actually in, so crossing the layer you can watch the target itself move. More on that under How it's built.

Worked examples — from the game's own proof runs

Ballast ships with a proof test: a scripted pilot that flies all three levels on the same physics the game runs, and a set of measurements taken from the shipped code. These are its numbers, exactly as measured — not illustrations.

1 · The still point: 0.0000 metres of drift

Set the sub at 50 m with the tanks at exactly neutral fill (50.00%) and no input at all. After 8 simulated seconds it had drifted 0.0000 m, with vertical speed 0.0000 m/s. When the two masses are exactly equal there is nothing left to accelerate the sub — the arithmetic doesn't approximate stillness, it produces it.

2 · The knife-edge, measured: +2% of fill

Same setup, but with the tanks at 52% instead of 50%. That extra 2% of the gauge is 0.02 × 1,230 = 24.6 kg of extra water — about one full suitcase — pulling down with roughly 241 N. After 1.5 seconds the sub is already sinking at 0.035 m/s, and the sink keeps growing until drag catches up with it. Two percent of one slider is the whole difference between "parked in mid-water" and "on its way down." That is the knife-edge, with a number on it.

3 · The layer pushes back: +235 kg of lift, valves untouched

Trim the sub neutral above the layer (fill 50%), then let it settle below the layer without touching a valve. Below the layer the same hull now displaces 10,500 kg of water, while the sub masses 9,635 + 0.5 × 1,050 × 1.2 = 10,265 kg. The difference is +235 kg of net lift — about 2,305 N — giving an initial upward acceleration of 0.225 m/s². Two seconds later, valves still untouched, the sub is rising at 0.44 m/s. The proof pilot that beat Level 3 answered the only way there is: it flooded up to the new neutral, 68.7%, and re-trimmed for the new water.

4 · Arriving gently vs. arriving fast: the wreck

Level 2 asks you to set down on a wreck's deck: arrive sinking slower than 0.8 m/s or you crunch. The proof pilot touched at 0.40 m/s down and 0.39 m/s across — about half the allowed speed. Why the fuss over fractions of a metre per second? Because kinetic energy grows with the square of speed. A 10,265 kg sub carries roughly 820 joules of vertical energy at 0.40 m/s — and roughly 20,500 joules at 2 m/s: five times the speed, twenty-five times the energy, all of it to be absorbed by the hull in the first instant of contact. The proof suite also flew the 2 m/s arrival, once, to check the other branch: the dive ended on the spot. Momentum does not care that the water felt soft on the way down.

5 · Level 1 walked through — how a submariner thinks

  1. Read the water first. Level 1 is ordinary seawater all the way down (ρ = 1,025), so neutral is one fixed number, not a moving target. The band is 38–46 m; the hold counts only while you are inside it and slower than 0.15 m/s.
  2. Do the sum before you move. Displaced 10,250 kg, hull 9,635 kg, so neutral is 615 kg of tank water — the gauge at exactly half. Check your arithmetic against the instrument: the green marker on the hull's tank gauge sits at 50%.
  3. Get heavy to go down. Flood past neutral — toward 60–65% — and let gravity do the work. No propeller needed for the descent itself; this is a weight change, not a swim.
  4. Start lightening early. The tanks chase your order at a game pace, and a ten-tonne sub carries momentum. Begin blowing back toward 50% well above the band, aiming to arrive near the middle of the band — about 42 m — with the gauge already near neutral, rather than fixing it after you get there.
  5. Arrive slow, then settle exactly. Feather the last of the sink out with the fill a touch under neutral, then set it dead on the marker. At exactly neutral the proof sub drifted 0.0000 m in 8 seconds; at +2% it was already sinking. Small corrections, made early.
  6. Sit on the knife-edge for six seconds. Not touching the bottom, not bobbing — hanging in mid-water on arithmetic. That is the whole dive, and it is the real skill: the proof pilot flew exactly this plan and settled at 42.1 m, holding the full six seconds.

How it's built — the coding choices

Ballast is one self-contained page, but its insides are split on purpose, and the split is the lesson for builders.

One pure physics core, shared by the game and its proof

Every rule — Archimedes, drag, tank behaviour, crush depth, the win and lose conditions — lives in a single block of code called BallastCore that knows nothing about drawing, buttons, or the screen. The game wraps it in canvas and gauges. The proof test loads the very same block out of the shipped page and flies it headlessly. So when the worked examples above quote a number, it came from the code you are playing — not from a copy, a cousin, or a developer's intention. The tested physics is the shipped physics.

The proof pilot flies on the player's instruments

The scripted pilot that proves every level can be won is not allowed to cheat. Its tank orders are built from the same number the HUD shows a human: the core's neutralFill — the fill that would be neutral here, in this water — plus a correction proportional to the gap between the vertical speed it wants and the speed it has. Builders call that pattern feedforward plus feedback: the physics tells you where the answer should be, the instruments tell you how you're drifting from it. Because the pilot wins using only what the player can see, its success is evidence a human can win the same way. A proof that peeked at hidden state would prove nothing about the game.

The sim advances in fixed small steps

The simulation always moves in slices of 1/240 of a second, however fast the screen happens to redraw. On a quick machine and a slow one, a dive unfolds identically — the physics cannot depend on your frame rate. It costs a little bookkeeping (an accumulator that carries leftover time between frames) and buys the one property a physics lesson must have: same inputs, same ocean, every time.

Drag in two terms — and what each is for

Vertical drag in the core is 50·v + 260·v·|v| newtons. The two terms have different jobs. The linear term (50·v) rules at creep speed, where all the delicate work happens: it gives motion near neutral a soft, damped feel and stops a correction from ringing back and forth. The quadratic term (260·v·|v|) is negligible at 0.1 m/s (about 2.6 N) but reaches roughly 1,040 N at 2 m/s — it is the deep-water brake that stops a flooded sub from accelerating all the way down and makes a gentle arrival at the wreck possible. Real water drag is mostly quadratic; the linear term is a modelling choice for low-speed handling, made openly so the hovering game feels like hovering instead of ice-skating.

The gauge's green marker — an honesty feature disguised as an instrument

Drawn on the hull is a small tank gauge: blue for the water actually in the tanks, and a green tick for where neutral sits right now. That tick is recomputed every frame from the density at the sub's current depth. Cross the layer and the tick slides from 50% to 68.7% on its own — the instrument admits, in public, that the target moves. Games usually hide their moving targets. This one prints it on the side of the boat, because the moving target is the physics being taught.

Simplifications, named

Named plainly, same as in the game: the hull displaces a fixed 10 m³ — a real hull compresses slightly as pressure grows (and the water compresses too), which is one reason real trim is a constant chore. The tanks answer at a game pace, not a real boat's. There are no currents and no surface weather — the density layer's effect on buoyancy is the only surprise in the water, and the layer itself is drawn as a smooth ramp of density between 55 and 75 m rather than a sharp sheet. The ocean is a 2D side view: no turning, no fore-and-aft trim. And one thing that is not simplified: the arithmetic. Net force really is (displaced water mass − sub mass) × g at every step, and the HUD prints the same numbers the physics is using.
