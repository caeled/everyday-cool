## Docking — an original game (added 20 November 2026)

`docking.html` is an original Everyday Cool game built by Muse (AI assistant) on 20 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction: the orbital-rendezvous lab on the Physics Game Table, and this room's own counterweight to the supplied orbital games — where Orbital Pinball and Gravity Golf play with gravity, Docking flies it. Both craft move under point-mass Newtonian gravity integrated in the page, and the ship's thrust points in its own local frame (prograde, retrograde, radial out, radial in), so the counter-intuitive heart of rendezvous is the physics itself rather than a script: aim straight at the station and burn, and the orbit bends away underneath you.

Its simplifications, named plainly: the planet is a point mass; the world is a flat 2D plane; there is no drag, no perturbation, and no third-body effect; the station flies a fixed, unpowered circular orbit; thrust is a constant acceleration with fuel metered as Δv — there is no mass model and no rocket equation, so a burn costs the same Δv early or late; the ship has no attitude or rotation model, so thrust is available instantly in any of the four frame directions; and hitting the planet is a simple radius check.

It was not shipped on a promise. Before integration, the game's own physics core was run headlessly: the station's orbit drifted by 2.16e-11 units over five periods — a circle, held; from the second level's start, a burn straight at the station ended the orbit at range 114 from a 13-unit start, farther away than it began, while a retrograde phasing burn arrived and docked; and a scripted feedback controller docked all three levels inside their fuel budgets. The levels are hard the way the real manoeuvre is hard, and the proof says they are fair.

## Stage It — an original game (added 20 November 2026)

`stage-it.html` is an original Everyday Cool game built by Muse (AI assistant) on 20 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction: the Flight section's rocket game. Where the paper planes are gliders trading height for speed, a rocket carries its engine and its fuel — and the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = vₑ · ln(m_full / m_empty) applied stage by stage with the upper stage riding as the lower stage's payload, decides what that costs. The player splits a fixed fuel load between two stages on a slider and sees each configuration's total Δv computed live from the real equation, then flies the ascent and chooses the moment the empty first stage drops away.

Its simplifications, named plainly: the ascent is one-dimensional and vertical — there is no steering and no gravity turn, and orbital speed is taught as the speed ingredient of orbit, separate from the direction problem; gravity is a constant g = 9.8 m/s² at every altitude; the exhaust velocity is a constant 3,000 m/s; drag is a light quadratic term, not an atmosphere model; each stage's structure and engine mass split in proportion to its fuel share; and stage separation is instant, with no coast or ignition delay.

It was not shipped on a promise. Before integration, the game's own core was run headlessly: its Δv figures match independent hand arithmetic to 1e-9; every level has a winning split on the player's own slider grid — 95/5 on the first level, 80/20 on the second, 75/25 on the third; the second level's single-stage configuration (the whole fuel load in one stage) totals 4,828 m/s of Δv against a 5,600 m/s mission budget, and flown perfectly by the script it still dies at 4,418 of the required 4,800 m/s; and delaying separation ten seconds past first-stage dry costs 119 m/s of final margin. The rocket equation is the game's villain, and the proof says it plays fair.

## Ballast — an original game (added 20 November 2026)

`ballast.html` is an original Everyday Cool game built by Muse (AI assistant) on 20 November 2026 at Steven Powell's direction: a submarine buoyancy game that leaves Archimedes unhidden. The boat displaces a fixed volume of water; flooding or blowing the ballast tanks changes the boat's mass; and the net force is the difference between the displaced water's mass and the boat's, shown on the HUD in kilograms as the player flies. Three levels teach the three lessons in turn: finding and holding neutral buoyancy, diving to a wreck above the crush-depth line and arriving gently, and crossing a density layer — a band of denser water that changes the boat's buoyancy without the player touching a valve.

Its simplifications, named plainly: the displaced volume is fixed — a real hull compresses slightly with depth, which is part of why real submarines grow heavier as they descend, and the game leaves that out; the tanks answer at a game pace rather than a real boat's slower flood and blow; there are no currents, so the density layer is the only surprise the water holds; the world is a 2D side view; and the dive planes matter only while the boat is moving, as the page states. The arithmetic itself is not simplified: the displaced mass, the boat's mass, and their difference are the real numbers the simulation flies on.

It was not shipped on a promise. Before integration, the game's own physics core was run headlessly through eleven checks, all passed: the neutral fill the game computes — 0.5000 at ρ = 1025 — holds 0.0000 m of drift over eight seconds, while a +2% fill sinks at 0.035 m/s; a scripted pilot flying on the HUD's own instruments (depth, vertical speed, net lift) completes all three levels; crossing the density layer with the tanks untouched yields +235 kg of net lift and 0.225 m/s² of upward acceleration, measured, not scripted; and every lose condition — crush depth, a hard arrival at the wreck, a forbidden broach — actually fires in simulation when driven into.

## Data sources (facts, not files)

- **Reflection** — a bounce off a flat surface keeps the velocity component parallel to the surface and reverses the perpendicular component, so the angle to the surface is the same in and out. The standard treatment in every introductory mechanics text; the pool table computes it, and `test.cjs` re-checks the equality on both axes.
- **Equal-mass elastic collisions** — for two equal masses meeting elastically, the velocity components along the line of centres are exchanged and the perpendicular components are unchanged; total momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. Standard mechanics; asserted in `test.cjs` for head-on and glancing cases.
- **Projectile motion** — range R = v²·sin(2θ)/g, apex h = (v·sinθ)²/(2g), flight time t = 2·v·sinθ/g, with g = 9.8 m/s². These assume launch and landing at the same height and no air resistance — the page says so wherever the sums appear.
- **The pendulum** — T = 2π√(L/g) for a small swing, the approximation taught everywhere. A wider swing's period is lengthened by the standard factor T/T₀ = (2/π) K(sin(θ/2)), the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, implemented as its usual power series in `phys.js` and checked in `test.cjs` (a 60° swing's factor is about 1.0732). The animation still draws a smooth cosine swing; only the period is corrected, and the page says so.
- **Torque and balance** — a beam balances when force × distance matches on both sides of the pivot. The seesaw's weights are masses used as weights in the everyday sense (40 kg against 20 kg under the same gravity), which the card wording keeps plain.
- **Paper-plane flight** (added 20 November 2026) — wing loading is weight ÷ wing area, the standard aeromodelling measure of how hard a wing is working. The bands the bench uses — under 2.5 g/dm² slow and floaty, 2.5–4 the steady middle, over 4 fast and demanding — are this room's rules of thumb for paper planes, not an industry standard, and the page presents the number as character, never as distance. Wing areas (the Dart ≈ 1.05 dm², the Glider ≈ 2.7 dm², the Stunt plane ≈ 1.1 dm²) are planform estimates for the three folds as built from one Letter/A4 sheet, good to about a fifth either way. Paper weights come from basis weights: US Letter copy paper at 20 lb bond ≈ 75 g/m², and a Letter sheet is 0.0603 m², so ≈ 4.5 g a sheet; A4 at 80 g/m² × 0.0624 m² ≈ 5.0 g; cardstock at 65 lb cover ≈ 176 g/m² ≈ 10.6 g a Letter sheet, stated on the bench as about 10 g. The sums are asserted in `test.cjs` (Glider on Letter = 1.67 g/dm²). The four forces and the glider's bargain — a glider with no engine descends to keep its speed, paying height for distance — are introductory aerodynamics as taught in every beginner text; the trim practice (nose weight moves the balance point forward, up-bent trailing edges act as elevators, a shallow V of dihedral self-levels) is standard paper-plane practice, and the fold sequences are the classic schoolroom dart, wide-wing glider, and elevator-trim stunt folds, written out fresh for this room.

## Limitations

The pool table is a teaching model, not a pool simulation: no spin or throw, friction as a steady share of speed lost per frame rather than cloth physics, all balls equal in mass, and speeds in the table's own units (pixels per frame), which the page states rather than converting. The launcher's world has no air, so its ranges are the longest a real throw at those numbers could never quite reach; the linked PhET Projectile Motion simulation offers the with-air comparison. The pendulum animation is timed by the same (angle-corrected) formula it teaches, so it cannot disagree with the readout — a real string, a real stopwatch, and the experiment cards are the honest check. The pool table and launcher advance on a fixed 1/60 s timestep taken from elapsed time, with a large-dt clamp when a tab resumes, so their speed does not depend on the display's refresh rate. The seesaw treats its two weights as fixed so the distance lesson stays in front. The flight section's wing areas are estimates and its loading bands are rules of thumb: the bench predicts a plane's character, never its distance, and the page says so. Descriptions were written on 18 November 2026 (the flight section on 20 November 2026); each linked site's own pages are the authority.
