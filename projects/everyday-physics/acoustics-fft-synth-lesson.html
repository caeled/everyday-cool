Harmonics — the lesson

Pluck a string, blow a pipe, twist a synthesizer drawbar — and watch a live spectrum sort the sound into its harmonics, one peak per partial, in real time. Underneath the waterfall sits a piece of machinery with a famous name: the Fast Fourier Transform, the algorithm that made spectra cheap enough to draw sixty times a second. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the physics of strings and pipes, the mathematics of the transform itself, and the audit that ran the shipped FFT against an independent transform written only to check it — and found them agreeing to the fourteenth decimal place.

Verdict: Core PASS — a genuine radix-2 FFT matching an independent DFT to 1.5×10⁻¹⁴, with exact string and pipe series and Bessel-true FM sidebands — but the 'live FFT' analyses a parameter-matched twin of the sound rather than the audio itself, and at slider extremes the top harmonics alias into phantom inharmonic peaks.

The physics

A string sings in whole numbers

A string fixed at both ends can only sustain standing waves that fit: a whole number of half-wavelengths in its length. So its allowed pitches are not a smear but a ladder — fn = n·v/2L — the fundamental and its integer multiples, the harmonic series. Where you pluck decides which rungs sound. Pluck off-centre and every mode that moves at the pluck point contributes, weighted by sin(nπx₀/L)/n²: strong low down, falling as the square of the mode number. Pluck at the exact centre and something clean happens — every even mode has a node at the centre, so it cannot be excited at all, and the even harmonics vanish. That is not a display trick. It is geometry, and the audit measured it below.

A pipe is a string made of air — with one end's worth of difference

An open pipe fits whole half-wavelengths too, fn = nc/2L, the full series. Close one end and the rules change: the closed end is a node, the open end an antinode, so only odd multiples fit — fn = nc/4L, odd n only. The fundamental drops by exactly an octave and every even harmonic is erased. That is why a clarinet, a closed pipe, sounds hollow where a flute, an open pipe, sounds full — and why a closed pipe overblows at the twelfth, landing on its third harmonic, not the octave.

Timbre is a recipe, and FM cooks with Bessel functions

The synthesizer presets are the same ladder read as recipes: a sawtooth carries every harmonic at 1/n, a square wave the odd ones at 1/n, a triangle the odd ones at 1/n² with alternating signs. Frequency modulation is richer cooking: wiggle a carrier's phase with another sine and sidebands sprout at fc ± n·fm, their strengths set not by a recipe table but by Bessel functions Jn(β) of the modulation index. Choose an inharmonic ratio — the Bell preset runs β = 2.65 at ratio 2.76 — and the sidebands land between the harmonics, which is exactly why FM bells clang instead of hum.

Two close pitches beat

Sound two tones a few hertz apart and they take turns agreeing and cancelling: the loudness pulses at |f₂ − f₁|, the beat frequency. At Δf = 3.5 Hz the envelope completes one swell-and-null every 0.286 seconds. Beats are the oldest tuning instrument there is — a piano tuner listens for the pulse slowing to zero — and here you can watch the envelope do it.

The math the game runs

The transform: Cooley–Tukey, radix-2

Xk = Σn xn · e−2πikn/N,   N = 1024 (or 512) The shipped radix2FFT is a genuine Cooley–Tukey radix-2 implementation, run at the model's own sample rate of 16384 Hz — bin widths of 16 Hz at N = 1024 and 32 Hz at N = 512. Before the transform, the frame is multiplied by a window — Hann, Hamming, Blackman, or Rectangular, all the textbook formulas, all coherent-gain normalised (Σw/N = 1.000000) so a bin-centred tone reads its true amplitude. The window is the classic trade: the Rectangular window leaks a 440 Hz tone's energy 10 bins away at 0.0316; Hann holds that same leakage to 0.0003. A parabolic interpolation across the peak bin then refines the pitch readout.

The string and the pluck

fn = n·v/2L,   an ∝ sin(nπx₀/L)/n² At the defaults — v = 340.1 m/s, L = 65 cm — the code's fundamental is 261.615 Hz, C4. The audit compared pluckString(0.24) against the analytic triangular-pluck series mode by mode: 0.76052, 0.27720, 0.09511, 0.00870, −0.02612, −0.03031, −0.01914, −0.00432 for n = 1…8 — a match to all printed digits.

The pipes

open: fn = nc/2L  ·  closed: fn = nc/4L, odd n only,   c = 331.3 + 0.606·T At 20 °C the code's speed of sound is 343.42 m/s (the standard linear law; the true value is 343.2). Open fundamental 261.75 Hz; closed 130.88 Hz — the exact octave the physics promises.

FM sidebands

y = sin(2πfct + β·sin 2πfmt),   sideband n ∝ Jn(β) Genuine phase modulation, in both the display model and the audio engine, where the deviation is correctly Δf = β·fm. The sidebands' strengths are not chosen — they fall out of the Bessel functions, and the audit checked that they do.

Receipt 3: FM sidebands track Bessel

At the Bell preset's β = 2.65, sideband ratios to the carrier measured [1, 3.54, 3.81, 2.12] against Bessel theory [1, 3.80, 3.87, 2.04] — within about 4–7%, the gap accounted for by negative-frequency folding and Hann leakage. In a clean no-fold configuration (f₁ = 440, fm = 220, β = 1): J₁/J₀ measured 0.62/0.65 vs 0.575, J₂/J₀ 0.177 vs 0.150, J₃/J₀ 0.029 vs 0.026. The distribution is Bessel's, not a look-alike.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The "live FFT" analyses a twin, not the audio: there is no AnalyserNode anywhere in the file. The spectrum, scope, and waterfall analyse a second, deterministic synthesis built from the same parameters — not the sound actually produced. For Synth and Pipe modes the twin and the audio bank share parameters, so what you see matches what you hear in content. For String mode they diverge in detail: you hear a Karplus–Strong pluck — a genuine KS loop, comb-shaped by pluck position — while you see the exact modal Fourier series. The honest label would be "model FFT."

Partials shown vs heard: the display synthesis uses 12 modes for string and pipe; the audible pipe and synth banks have only 8 oscillators — partials 9–12 are silent in the audio and present on screen. Small at their weights, but the same twin-model gap.

The display silently stops at 4400 Hz: the analysis runs to the 8192 Hz Nyquist, but the spectrum and waterfall axes end at 4400 Hz. Harmonics above 4.4 kHz are computed and never drawn — at C4 all 12 modes fit; at C5 only 8 of 12 are visible. The truncation is disclosed nowhere.

"Pure 12th" overstates: the 1/3 node gives a 12th-dominant spectrum — n = 6 at 32% of n = 3 — and the n = 3 boost (×2.35) is a deliberate design choice, disclosed in a code comment but not on the page.

Smaller print: the pipe's "Natural Rich" amplitudes are invented rolloffs (open: n^−1.12 with a 0.92 dip at n = 2; closed: k^−0.95 on odd k) — plausible organ and clarinet shapes, presented as natural without saying they are a chosen model, not a measured pipe spectrum. The clarinet preset carries small even partials (0.05, 0.06, 0.04, 0.03) under an "Odd reed" label — realistic for a real clarinet, worth this footnote. The analyser's 16384 Hz sample rate is the model's, not the device's WebAudio rate. And one FFT frame is 62.5 ms — shorter than a beat period — so beats live on the scope and across frames, never inside a single spectrum; that is inherent to the tool, not a defect.
