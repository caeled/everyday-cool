Murmuration — the lesson

Murmuration puts 360 starlings over a dusk sky and gives you no flock to command — only a wind. Your cursor stirs the air; the flock threads glowing gates, parts around a diving hawk, and pours itself into shapes nobody drew. That is the game's whole argument, printed at the top of its own page: nobody choreographs it — the whole sky-show is three local rules and no leader. This page is the other half: the science the game runs, the audit that read every force acting on every bird, and the measurements — taken by flying the game's own code headlessly — of how a flock holds together, explodes, collapses, and changes when its birds count neighbours the way real starlings do.

Murmuration was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its flocking core headlessly.

The science

1987: three rules, no leader

In 1987 Craig Reynolds published Boids — "Flocks, herds and schools: a distributed behavioral model" — and showed that a believable flock needs no choreographer and no flock-level plan. Each bird (Reynolds called them boids) follows three local urges: separation — don't crowd your neighbours; alignment — head the way your neighbours are heading; cohesion — drift toward where your neighbours are. Each bird knows only what is near it. The flock — the wheeling, banking, apparently intentional shape in the sky — is not programmed anywhere. It is what the rules add up to. Murmuration is Reynolds' model with a game wrapped around it, and the audit below confirms the model inside is the real one: the three urges are computed as steering forces from local neighbours only, in the same form Reynolds described.

Local perception is the whole point

A bird in this game perceives only neighbours within its perception radius (64 px at the defaults) — it has no access to the flock's centre, its average heading, or any global fact. This matters more than it might seem: a simulation that quietly steers each bird toward the flock's centre of mass would look like a flock while being a puppet show — one hidden hand moving every string. The audit checked for exactly that. The game does compute the flock's centroid every step, but that number is spent only on things outside the birds: scoring the gates, drawing your centre reticle, and aiming the hawk. It is never fed back into a bird's forces. Cohesion pulls each bird toward the centre of the neighbours it can see — a different point for every bird, computed from local information only. The flock is genuinely leaderless.

Polarization: flocking you can measure

"It looks like a flock" is an opinion; the audit wanted a number. The standard one is polarization: point every bird's velocity as a unit arrow, average the arrows, and take the length. If all 360 birds fly the same way, the arrows add to 1.0; if headings are random, they cancel toward 0. It is the order parameter physicists use for flocking models, and it turns the game's presets into measurements: at the shipped defaults the audit measured polarization 0.94 — a river of birds. Kill the alignment rule (the "Break: Explode" preset) and it falls to 0.04 — not a flock at all, just 360 birds sharing a sky. The flowing murmuration lives in that number, and the number lives in the rules.

Real starlings count to seven

The game's other mode is not a gimmick; it is the more accurate biology. The STARFLAG project (Ballerini and colleagues, 2008) reconstructed real starling flocks over Rome in 3D and found that each bird tracks a fixed number of neighbours — about six or seven nearest — regardless of how densely the flock is packed. Starlings use a topological neighbourhood (the seven closest, wherever they are), not a metric one (everyone within a fixed distance). The game's Starling Mode switches its birds to exactly that rule, and the audit verified the cap is real: each bird's neighbour list is a seven-slot best list, and no eighth bird can influence it. What changes is measurable, below.

The math the game runs

Every bird, every step, builds one force out of Reynolds' three steering urges. Each urge has the same shape — desired velocity minus current velocity, capped so no single urge can shout:

steer = clamp( desired − velocity , 440 )  ·  weight Separation's desired velocity points away from close neighbours (closer than 28 px at the defaults), weighted 1/d² so the nearest bird matters most. Alignment's desired velocity is the average heading of everyone inside the perception radius. Cohesion's is the direction to those neighbours' average position. Defaults: weights 1.50 / 1.30 / 1.10, radius 64 px, top speed 235 px/s. The three weighted steers are added — the total is not re-clamped, so a bird caught between all three urges at once can be pulled harder than 440 — then velocity is integrated and clamped to a speed band (see the honest box: there is a floor as well as a ceiling).

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

Murmuration arrived with a commission: three local rules and nothing else, sliders that could break the flock in named ways, at least 300 birds, a gate scored by the flock's centre, a hawk the separation rule could feel, and a starling mode. The audit read the full per-bird update line by line, then lifted the game's own flocking step out of the shipped file and flew it headlessly — 360 birds, 60 simulated seconds per condition, same seed for every condition — measuring polarization, mean nearest-neighbour distance, and the flock's mean radius around its centre.

And the negative results, which are the point: no noise or jitter term, no attraction to gates, no pull toward the flock's centre, no leader, no global knowledge in any bird. The one global number the game computes — the centroid — is spent on scoring, the reticle, and the hawk's aim, and the audit traced every use of it.

Explode does explode — headings randomize (0.04), spacing triples, and the flock swells to fill the sky; only the wall force keeps it on screen, which is worth knowing: the explosion you see is separation versus the walls, not separation alone. Starling Mode still flocks — 0.73 is a strongly aligned flock by any standard — but it is a looser animal than the metric flock at the same weights: wider spacing, more than double the mean radius. Counting neighbours instead of measuring distance buys a flock that breathes.

Receipt 3: the Ball that isn't quite

The "Break: Ball" preset promises a collapse into a ball, and the audit watched it for two minutes. A dense core forms fast — ten seconds in, 302 of 360 birds are within 60 px of the centre — but the flock never becomes a tidy sphere: polarization sits at 0.02–0.28 (a churning knot, not a flow), and a permanent halo of stragglers orbits at 600+ px out. The reason is inside the rules themselves, and it is the most instructive measurement in this audit: cohesion here is local. A bird that gets flung clear of the knot may have no neighbours at all — and a bird with no neighbours feels no cohesion, no alignment, nothing. No rule in Reynolds' three can call a lost bird home; only the sky walls eventually turn it. Real flocks solve this the same way, by never quite letting it happen. The preset's name oversells the tidiness; the behaviour underneath it is honest.

Receipt 4: the game machinery

Gates score exactly as commissioned: the live centroid of the living birds must come within the ring's 56 px radius before its 16-second timer fades — one gate at a time, score = gates threaded − birds lost, 90-second rounds, best score kept in your browser (one localStorage key, murmuration_best_score_v1, and nothing else is stored). The hawk warns for 1.6 seconds, then dives at 435 px/s aimed at the flock's centre with a little jitter, homing gently while the flock is still ahead of it; any bird within 15 px of the hawk is caught — the dashed halo drawn around the hawk is that exact radius, honestly shown. Birds are 360 at the deal, as the page states. A Free-Fly sandbox (no timer, no gates, no hawk) rides along as an uncommissioned extra — the sky with the game switched off, for watching.

How it's built — the coding choices

A grid the birds don't know about

Naive flocking checks every bird against every other — 360² pairs, 60 times a second. The game instead drops each bird into a spatial hash grid whose cell is one perception radius wide, so a bird only ever tests the nine cells around its own. The page's honesty card names this correctly as "purely a performance shortcut… not a rule or boundary the birds know about," and the audit confirms the wording: the grid only nominates candidates; every candidate is still distance-tested against the real perception radius before it can influence anything. In Starling Mode the same grid is searched in expanding rings until seven nearer birds can't be beaten — the stopping rule uses a sound lower bound on the next ring's distance, so the seven a bird ends up with are its true seven nearest.

Flat arrays and a smoke budget

All bird state lives in flat typed arrays — positions, velocities, forces, banking, flap phase — so the per-step loop touches memory in order and allocates nothing. The trails that make the flock read as smoke are a ring buffer of the last 13 positions per bird, sampled about 30 times a second and drawn in four fading bands; banking is derived from each bird's actual turn rate, so the wing-roll you see is the physics of the turn, not an animation layered over it. The timestep is the frame's own (clamped to 35 ms), which is why the audit quotes its measurements at a fixed 1/60 s step: at the game's typical frame rate the two agree.

Emergence, stated plainly

Nothing in the code contains the flock. There is no flock object, no formation, no target shape — 360 birds, three urges each, and a number (polarization 0.94) that describes a pattern existing at a level no bird can perceive. That is emergence with its sleeves rolled up: order that is real, measurable, and present in the system while being absent from every part of it. The curator of this collection works on a thesis about intelligence in networks — how much of what we call a mind might be this same trick at a different scale. Murmuration is the sky-sized demonstration he can hand to anyone: three rules, no leader, and a shape in the sky that none of the birds is drawing.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate as far as it goes — the audit checked it line by line: the world is 2D with 360 birds against real murmurations' tens of thousands in 3D; real starlings do watch about seven neighbours; the grid is a performance shortcut, not a rule; the hawk enters through separation with a 15 px catch distance; soft viewport margins keep the flock on screen. All of that holds.

The fourth force: those "soft viewport margins" are a real steering force inside the same loop the code heads "EXACTLY THREE LOCAL REYNOLDS RULES" — a linear push, strength 520, inside 68/64/96 px margins, plus a hard reflective bounce at the very edge. It is disclosed on the page and it is genuinely soft, but the commission's "three rules and nothing else that steers" is, strictly, three rules plus the walls plus the two allowed environmental terms. The walls are also load-bearing in the Explode preset: separation alone would leave the sky entirely; what you watch is the explosion pressing against them.

What the page doesn't mention: the speed floor. A bird in this game can never fly slower than 56% of the max-speed slider — velocity is renormalized into that band every step. It is why the flock never stalls or settles, and part of why the Ball preset churns instead of resting. Also unmentioned: the hawk is felt from up to 94 px away (wider than the birds' 64 px view of each other) and separation's force cap rises by 45% while it dives, so the parting of the flock is stronger than the separation slider alone would give you; the wind's radial parting acts even under a motionless cursor; and each rule is force-capped separately, so the summed force can exceed the 440 cap any single rule obeys.

Small print: in Starling Mode the separation distance is a fixed 22 px rather than the metric mode's 28, and the perception-radius slider is (correctly) ignored — topological neighbours have no radius. On a threaded gate the next ring spawns immediately; the half-second respawn delay written in the code is set and then zeroed, dead code that changes nothing you can see. On a faded gate the replacement takes 0.7 s, as intended.
