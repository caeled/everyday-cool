Siege Demolition — the lesson

Siege Demolition is a castle-wrecking game: a slingshot, four kinds of shot, and five fortresses built from glass, timber, stone, and TNT, each guarding a set of green target cores. You don't win by knocking the castle down — you win by destroying every core, and the castle is what's standing in the way. Underneath the cartoon is a real rigid-body simulation: blocks with mass and hit points, impulses that shove and spin, towers that lose their support and fall. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples computed from the game's own numbers, and how it is built.

Siege Demolition was built with Google Antigravity and supplied by Steven. This lesson page was written for Everyday Cool, explaining the game exactly as it ships — including the places where the game's label promises more physics than the code delivers.

The physics

Rigid bodies: blocks that answer at their edges

A rigid body is an object that keeps its shape while the world pushes on it. Every block in the fortress is one: it has a position, a velocity, an angle, a spin, and a mass computed from its size and material. The important consequence is that a block doesn't just respond to how hard it's hit — it responds to where. A shove through the centre slides the block. The same shove near a corner also twists it, because the force arrives with leverage. That twist is torque, and it is why a hit at the top of a tall post can start a tumble that a hit at the base never would.

Toppling: the centre of mass walks off the base

A standing tower is stable while its centre of mass — the average position of all its weight — sits over its base. Gravity pulls that centre straight down; if the line of the pull lands inside the base, the ground can push back and the tower stands. Lean the tower (or slide a block out from under one side) until the centre of mass passes the base's edge, and gravity stops being the thing that holds the tower up and becomes the thing that pulls it over. No extra push is needed past that point — the fall feeds itself, because the further it tips, the longer gravity's lever arm gets. Every demolition trick in the game is a way of arranging this: remove a support, shove a base sideways, or drop something heavy on a roof so the whole stack's centre of mass ends up somewhere its base isn't.

The shot flies a parabola (plus a push from the wind)

Once a shot leaves the slingshot, exactly two things act on it: gravity pulling down at 610 px/s², and the level's wind pushing sideways at a constant rate (up to 75 px/s² in the Windy Bunker). Constant downward acceleration plus constant sideways acceleration traces the same parabola family as every other projectile in this room — the dotted aim arc the game draws is that parabola, computed ahead of the shot. The full story of projectile motion — why the arc is a parabola, and how launch angle trades height for range — is told in Ragdoll Archery's lesson; here the new wrinkle is that the arc bends sideways when the wind blows, and the game's preview arc honestly includes the bend.

Energy vs momentum: two different wrecking stories

A moving shot carries two quantities that both get called "power" in everyday talk, and they are not the same. Momentum (mass × speed) is what a collision hands over — it's what shoves a block backward. Kinetic energy (½ × mass × speed²) grows much faster with speed, and it's the number the game's HUD displays as "Impact KE." A heavy, slow boulder and a light, fast lance can carry identical momentum while the lance holds far more energy — and this game, unusually, keeps the two stories separate: the shove follows the collision impulse, but the damage formula reads momentum, not energy. Worked Example 2 below shows the two stories disagreeing about what "stronger" means — and the momentum story wins.

Fracture and collapse: death by a thousand cuts, then one fall

Blocks don't break because they were touched; they break because damage accumulates past a material limit. Glass shatters at 38 damage, timber at 82, stone at 175 — and damage arrives from four directions: direct shot impacts, block-on-block collisions, hard landings, and explosions. Collapse is what happens next: when a block is destroyed, everything near it wakes up, and anything it was holding up suddenly has gravity and no support. A fortress rarely dies from the hole your shot punched in it. It dies from the chain — the beam that falls 200 px and breaks on landing, the post it lands on taking collision damage, the TNT crate that goes off in the middle of the rubble.

The math the game runs

τ = F · d Torque: a force F applied at distance d from the centre of mass twists with strength F·d. Double the lever arm, double the twist — which is why where a shot lands on a tall block matters as much as how fast it arrives. The game's version of this lives in its collision code, and Worked Example 3 runs its actual numbers.
toppling starts when the centre of mass passes the base edge For a block of width w and height h tipping on its own corner, that's a lean of atan(w/h)... precisely, atan((w/2)/(h/2)). The Outpost's timber posts are 22 px wide and 90 px tall: atan(11/45) ≈ 13.7°. Tip a post past about 14° and gravity finishes the job for free. Equivalently, a resting block can slide until just under half its length hangs past its support — the 120 px cap beam could overhang nearly 60 px before its centre crosses the edge.
block mass = (width × height) / 1400 × material density Density: glass 0.65, timber 1.0, TNT 0.9, stone 2.1 (with a floor of 0.6 so nothing is weightless). An Outpost post (22×90 timber) masses 1.41; a Grand Keep stone pillar (24×90) masses 3.24. Mass decides both how hard a block is to shove and how hard it falls on whatever is underneath it.
shot damage = impact speed × 0.22 × shot mass × material multiplier The multiplier is the ammunition's personality: the Iron Boulder gets ×1.25 against everything; the Drill Lance gets ×1.8 against wood and glass but only ×0.9 against stone; everything else gets ×0.9. Read the formula and you can see it is speed × mass — momentum — wearing a coefficient. Damage under 5 points is ignored entirely: a gentle tap never chips a wall.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Example 1: the full-pull boulder

The slingshot pull is capped at 128 px, and launch speed is pull × 6.5 — so the fastest Iron Boulder leaves at 832 px/s, carrying momentum 2.8 × 832 ≈ 2,330 and kinetic energy ½ × 2.8 × 832² ≈ 969,000 (the HUD's Impact KE will read about 969, since it displays the same number divided by 1000). Its damage on a clean hit: 832 × 0.22 × 2.8 × 1.25 ≈ 641 — more than three times stone's 175 hit points. In fact the boulder one-shots stone at any impact speed above 175 / (0.22 × 2.8 × 1.25) ≈ 227 px/s, barely a quarter of full pull. Below that, the materials sort themselves: at 150 px/s the boulder deals 116 — through glass (38) and timber (82), but a stone block survives with 59 hit points left. That is the whole ammunition economy in one line: against stone, either arrive fast or arrive twice.

Example 2: same momentum, different energy — and momentum wins

Take two shots with identical momentum, 840 units. The Iron Boulder (mass 2.8) at 300 px/s: kinetic energy 126,000. The Drill Lance (mass 1.6) at 525 px/s: kinetic energy 220,500 — 75% more energy on the HUD's terms. Now fire both at a stone block. The boulder deals 840 × 0.22 × 1.25 = 231 — stone destroyed. The lance, its multiplier against stone only ×0.9, deals 840 × 0.22 × 0.9 = 166 — the stone survives with about 9 hit points. Aim the same lance at timber instead and its ×1.8 cutting multiplier deals 333, nearly double the boulder's damage. The lesson the numbers teach: in this game "how much wreck" is momentum times match-up, and the energy readout — the number that looks like power — is mostly a spectator. (In the real world the split is subtler: energy governs penetration and fracture, momentum governs shove. The game assigns both jobs to momentum and says so in its formula.)

Example 3: torque, run through the game's own collision

A boulder arrives horizontally at 400 px/s and strikes an Outpost post (mass 1.41). The collision impulse the code computes is J = (1 + 0.35) × 400 / (1/2.8 + 1/1.41) ≈ 507. What that impulse does to the post's spin depends entirely on the lever arm — the vertical distance from the post's centre to the hit:

Hit locationLever armSpin kick (code's arithmetic)
Dead centre0 px0 — pure shove, no rotation at all
Halfway up22.5 px≈ 6.5 rad/s
Top edge45 px≈ 12.9 rad/s

That is τ = F·d wearing the game's clothes: the spin is strictly proportional to the lever arm, and a centre hit cannot rotate the block no matter how hard it arrives. (The game then bleeds spin away fast — rotational damping of ×0.95 per frame — so the kick reads as a sharp tumble that settles, not a pinwheel.) The playing lesson: shoot the top of the tall thing and the base of the wide thing. Top hits buy rotation; base hits buy translation; a fortress falls when you purchase the right one.

Example 4: how far a block can fall before the fall itself breaks things

Falling damage starts at an impact speed of 185 px/s, which under the game's gravity (610 px/s²) means a drop of just 185² / (2 × 610) ≈ 28 px — less than the height of one timber post. A block that drops 100 px lands at √(2 × 610 × 100) ≈ 349 px/s and takes (349 − 150) × 0.25 ≈ 50 damage on landing: enough to finish a glass pane (38) by falling alone, not enough to break timber (82). A block that drops 200 px lands at 494 px/s and takes ≈ 86 — timber breaks from the fall itself, and it will also deal collision damage to whatever it lands on. This is why killing a low support is worth more than sniping a high block: gravity bills every block in the stack for the full height of the drop, and it collects from all of them at once.

How it's built — the coding choices

Siege Demolition is one HTML file, and its physics engine is custom code, no library — no Matter.js, no Box2D. That choice shapes everything, so the engine's design is worth reading as a builder's lesson.

Small steps, six at a time

The frame time is clamped to at most 0.03 s and then split into 6 substeps, each running the full simulation: projectiles, awake blocks, block collisions, targets. Stacked rigid bodies are where cheap simulations fall apart — one big step lets blocks sink into each other, and the correction then explodes them apart. Six small steps keep overlaps shallow and corrections gentle, at the cost of doing the work six times. For a scene of a few dozen blocks, that trade is cheap and it is the right one.

Sleeping bodies: the fortress holds still until it matters

Every block starts the level asleep (awake: false), and asleep blocks are skipped entirely — no gravity, no motion, no cost. A block wakes when something happens near it: a shot impact wakes everything within 260 px, a block's destruction wakes 190 px, explosions wake over twice their blast radius. This is the classic physics-engine trick for stacked scenes: a tower of sleeping blocks is perfectly stable because nothing is integrating it, and the simulation only spends effort where the action is. It is also the collapse mechanism — destroying a support is really just waking the neighbourhood and letting gravity find out the support is gone.

Two collision tests, each honest about its job

Shot-vs-block uses the proper test: the shot's centre is rotated into the block's own coordinate frame, clamped to the rectangle, and the distance decides the hit — so shots respect a block's true rotated shape, corners and all. Block-vs-block uses a cheaper approximation: each rotated block is replaced by the axis-aligned box that bounds it, and overlaps are pushed apart along whichever axis penetrates least, with an impulse whose restitution is 0.18 (nearly no bounce — rubble shouldn't bounce). The shot test needs to be exact because aim depends on it; the stacking test needs to be stable because forty blocks depend on it. Different jobs, different honesty budgets.

Impulses, and a tuned dial where inertia should be

Collisions resolve as impulses: a single instant change of velocity computed from the masses and a restitution coefficient (0.35 for shot-vs-block — a little bounce — 0.18 block-vs-block). The spin a shot imparts uses the real lever-arm cross product, r × n, multiplied by a hand-tuned constant, 0.0008, and the block's inverse mass. That constant is doing the job a real engine gives to the moment of inertia — how much a body's shape resists spinning — which this code never computes. The dial is set so hits feel right; the shape of the physics (spin ∝ lever arm, centre hits don't spin) is preserved even though the magnitude is tuned rather than derived.

The launch is arithmetic, and the preview is the same arithmetic

There is no simulated catapult arm. Dragging the slingshot sets a pull vector (capped at 128 px), and release velocity is simply pull × 6.5 — ×7.8 for the lance, which is why it flies flatter and faster. The dotted aim arc is drawn by stepping that same launch velocity through the same gravity and wind the shot will actually feel, so the preview cannot lie about the trajectory (it can only omit the collisions). Explosions are likewise one honest mechanism: everything within the radius gets a radial impulse that fades linearly with distance, damage on the same falloff, and TNT crates respond by running the same routine again from their own position — chain reactions are recursion, not a special effect.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: there is no moment of inertia anywhere in the code — spin from a shot is the lever-arm cross product scaled by a tuned 0.0008 constant, and spin from an explosion is frankly random (±9 × falloff rad/s, rolled per block). Gravity never applies torque about a pivot: support is decided by bounding-box overlap, so a block keeps full support until its box nearly clears the block beneath it — the code's towers collapse mainly when supports are destroyed outright, and the true 13.7° centre-of-mass tipping point taught above is the real world's rule, not the game's trigger. Relatedly, a block that lands tilted is gently eased toward the nearest flat 90° orientation rather than resting at an odd angle. Blocks never bend or deform — damage is a hit-point counter with a cosmetic crack drawn below 72% health, and a block at 1 HP is exactly as strong a support as a fresh one. Damage from shots reads momentum (speed × mass), not kinetic energy, whatever the HUD's KE readout suggests. Wind is a constant sideways acceleration with no gusts and no drag — the lance's "aerodynamic" quality is a flat 0.35 wind factor. Friction exists only as velocity multipliers on contact (×0.82 and friends), not as a friction force that can hold a steep stack together. The targets are circles with 32 hit points and painted eyes; the eyes are not simulated.
