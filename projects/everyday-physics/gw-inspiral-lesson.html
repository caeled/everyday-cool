Chirp — the lesson

Chirp is the loudest room in Everyday Physics: two black holes (or neutron stars) spiralling into each other, radiating gravitational waves, merging, and ringing down into a single Kerr black hole — with a LIGO oscilloscope, a draggable test-mass ring, and a synthesized chirp you can hear. It is also the most two-faced exhibit in the collection, in a way the audit can prove. The numbers on its astrophysics panel are real published formulas, evaluated with real SI constants, and several are exact to the printed digit. But the exhibit runs on two clocks: a physical clock for the readouts, and a theatrical clock for the motion — and they disagree, by design, by a factor that changes from preset to preset. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that separated the two clocks and priced each one.

Chirp was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

Play Chirp →

How to play

There is no score and no win screen. Chirp is a laboratory whose outcome is a waveform: an inspiral, a merger flash, a ringdown, and a settled remnant. The phase pill in the top-left tells you where you are — Inspiral, Merger Peak!, QNM Ringdown, or Kerr Remnant — and the telemetry under it reads out the wave frequency, the strain, the separation, the orbital speed, the energy radiated so far, and a countdown to coalescence.

  • Choose an event. Six presets: GW150914 (36 + 29 M☉, the first detection, at 410 Mpc), GW170817 (1.45 + 1.35 M☉ neutron stars at 40 Mpc, with kilonova ejecta), Extreme Ratio (60 + 5 M☉), Equal Heavy Pair (50 + 50 M☉), Fast Endless Loop (32 + 28 M☉ on a quick replay), and GW200105 NSBH (a black hole with a neutron star). Or drag the mass sliders yourself — anything at or below 2.5 M☉ is treated as a neutron star.
  • Set the stage. Initial separation from 6.5 to 17.5 rg (gravitational radii, GM/c²), and a Wave Radiation Rate dial from 0.25× to 4× that speeds or slows the decay. ⚡ Final Chirp restarts you deep in the late inspiral (6.23 rg on the default event); ↻ Restart Merger starts over.
  • Watch the wave, three ways. The luminous field and the warped grid show the strain spreading outward; the floating LIGO scope draws h+(t), h×(t) and the frequency track; and the test-mass ring can be dragged anywhere on the canvas to feel the quadrupole stretch and squeeze at that distance. The Waves tab isolates h+ or h×, rescales the strain amplification, and toggles each layer.
  • Listen. Hear LIGO Chirp (or M) turns on a synthesizer that tracks the wave frequency into the audible band, with a noise thump at the merger. It starts muted — browsers insist, and so does good manners.
  • Read the astrophysics tab. Total and chirp mass, symmetric mass ratio η, ISCO radius in kilometres, predicted radiated energy, remnant mass and spin, the ringdown (QNM) frequency, and the recoil kick — all recomputed live from the two masses. The audit's first receipt is about this panel.
  • Keys. Space pause, R restart, M audio, H Zen (hide the HUD), D controls, C recentre. Drag space to pan, scroll to zoom. Speeds 0.25×–2× are on the bottom bar.

With auto-replay on (the default), the remnant settles for about 3.4 seconds and the event starts again. Turn it off and the remnant sits there, kicked gently sideways if the masses were unequal — the quietest physics in the room, and some of the strangest: the merged black hole leaves the scene of the collision at highway speed, forever.

The physics

Why a binary chirps

A single mass sitting still does not radiate. Two masses orbiting each other do, because their quadrupole moment — the lopsidedness of their mass distribution — changes twice per orbit. General relativity says that changing quadrupole radiates gravitational waves, and the waves carry energy away. Energy lost means the orbit shrinks; a smaller orbit is a faster orbit; a faster orbit radiates harder. The loop runs away: frequency and amplitude climb together in the rising wail that gives this exhibit its name. The wave frequency is exactly twice the orbital frequency, because the quadrupole pattern repeats every half-turn:

fGW = 2 forb,   da/dt = −(64/5) · G³M₁M₂(M₁+M₂) / (c⁵a³) The second equation is the Peters formula (1964): how fast quadrupole radiation drains the separation a. It is printed in the game's own Astrophysics tab, and — in geometric dress — it is the law the code integrates. Everything about the chirp's shape follows from the a−3 on the right: the decay is gentle for years and violent in the last second.

Integrate that law and the frequency's rise is fixed by a single combination of the masses, the chirp mass ℳ = (M₁M₂)3/5/(M₁+M₂)1/5. That is why LIGO's first measurement of any event is its chirp mass: the pitch rise of the chirp is the mass, heard directly. For GW150914 the sweep says ℳ ≈ 28 M☉ before any other analysis is done. Keep this in mind — the audit will fit the game's own sweep and read off the mass it implies, and the answer is the single most interesting number in this lesson.

Strain: how big is a ripple from a billion light-years

The wave's amplitude at Earth, the strain h, is the fractional stretching of space itself — for GW150914 it peaked near 10−21, moving LIGO's mirrors by about a thousandth of a proton's width. For a circular binary the quadrupole formula gives:

h = 4η · (GM/c²) / (DL · a) With a in units of GM/c² and η the symmetric mass ratio M₁M₂/(M₁+M₂)². This is the formula the game evaluates for its strain readout, with real G, real c, and the preset's real distance — so during the inspiral its strain numbers are genuine face-on quadrupole amplitudes, not set dressing. The audit checks them at three separations and two distances.

Merger and ringdown: the bell, not the spiral

When the two horizons touch, the spiral description ends. The merged object is a single distorted black hole, and it sheds its distortion the way a struck bell sheds sound: in quasi-normal modes (QNMs), each a pure tone with a frequency and a damping time set only by the final mass and spin. For the dominant l = 2, m = 2 mode, Berti, Cardoso & Will fitted the tone to numerical relativity:

MfωQNM = 1.5251 − 1.1568(1 − χf)0.1292,   Q = 0.7000 + 1.4187(1 − χf)−0.4990 ω is the ringdown angular frequency, Q its quality factor, χf the remnant's spin. The damping time is τ = Q/(πf). The game implements these fits verbatim — the audit verifies the frequency to the digit, and then prices what the game does with τ.

The remnant itself — its mass, spin, and recoil — cannot be derived from the quadrupole formulas; it comes from supercomputer simulations of full general relativity, distilled into fitting formulas. The game uses the standard ones: a remnant-spin fit in η (2√3 η − 3.871η² + 4.028η³), and Fitchett's recoil law, in which the kick vanishes for equal masses (there is no preferred direction to kick toward) and peaks near mass ratio q ≈ 0.36 at about 175 km/s. The audit checks all of them against the published values.

The math the game runs

The game keeps two unit systems. The readouts are computed in honest SI: the file opens with G = 6.67430 × 10−11, c = 2.99792458 × 108, M☉ = 1.98847 × 1030 kg, from which it derives rg = 1.476625 km per solar mass and the orbital-frequency unit c³/(2πGM☉) = 32,309.5 Hz·M☉. Separations live in geometric units (a in GM/c²), and the displayed frequency is the physical one for the separation on screen:

fGW = 2 · (32,309.5 Hz / M) · a−3/2,   v/c = a−1/2 At the default start (a = 11.5, M = 65) that is 25.49 Hz and 0.295c; at the ISCO (a = 6) it is 67.5 Hz and 0.408c — the real GW150914 passed its ISCO frequency near 68 Hz. The speed is capped at 0.68c, which binds only inside a = 2.16 rg.

The motion, though, runs on a calibrated constant. The separation is stepped by genuine RK4 on the Peters form, but with a coefficient chosen for the screen:

da/dt = −KPM · η / a³,   KPM = 1660 × (radiation-rate dial) The code's own comment says why: with a₀ = 11.5 and amerge = 2.65, the integral (a₀⁴ − am⁴)/4 ≈ 4,360, and K ≈ 1660 makes the inspiral last ~10.5 s at η = 0.25. The true Peters coefficient, converted for a 65 M☉ binary, would be 64/(5 · GM/c³) ≈ 39,980 — the game's decay is the same law with the clock slowed. Receipt 3 prices the difference, preset by preset.

And the bodies you see orbit at a third rate, neither of the two above:

ωvisual = 16.5 · a−3/2 · (1 + 1.15/a) · (radiation rate)0.12 Rad/s, and the code's comment is candid: "tuned for dramatic, legible real-time motion." At the default start it is 0.465 rad/s where the physical orbital angular frequency is 80.1 rad/s. The orbital phase φ that this advances is what the wave field, the scope trace, and the Orbits counter all read — which is why Receipt 4 finds the eye and the panel keeping different time.

The wave field is the most honest piece of engineering in the file. Every 8 ms of simulation time, the source's phase, amplitude, and frequency are pushed into a 2,048-sample ring buffer — about 16.4 s of history. Every pixel of the field and every vertex of the grid then samples that buffer at the retarded time tret = t − r/cgw, with cgw = 14 world units/s, and applies the quadrupole pattern h+ ∝ A cos(2(φret − θ)), h× ∝ A sin(2(φret − θ)) with a 2D falloff A ∝ 3.2/√(r + 4.5). The grid's vertices are displaced by that strain tensor acting on the radial direction (× 1.65, plus a static dent toward the binary), and the test-mass ring is deformed by the actual geodesic-deviation matrix δr′ = δr + ½h·δr, clamped to ±0.48 and gained by 0.52 so the stretch is visible. The merger is a fixed 0.22 s bridge that lerps the frequency from its value at amerge to the QNM tone; the ringdown decays as exp(−t/τ) with τ = 0.42 + (Q − 2) × 0.08 s. The file is fully self-contained: no external references, no localStorage, no network — everything you see is in the one file.

The radiated-energy fit — 0.0485(4η) + 0.008(4η)² — overshoots the numerical-relativity values by about a fifth at every mass ratio the audit tried, and the code's comment on that very line claims "~4.65% for GW150914 (~3.02 M☉)": the comment quotes reality, the formula doesn't deliver it. Receipt 8: the ringdown tone is exact; its decay is a screen mapping

The ringdown holds f = 277.3 Hz — the Berti value for the game's remnant, to the digit — for its whole 2.30 s. But the damping time is τ = 0.42 + (Q − 2) × 0.08 = 0.5165 s, a mapping chosen for the screen, where the physical value from the game's own Q and f is τ = Q/(πf) = 3.68 ms. The on-screen ringdown therefore lasts ~140× longer than the same tone would at the event — in line with the exhibit's general slow motion, but it is not the Q = 3.21 the panel quotes; no measurement of the drawn decay would recover it. The remnant phase also keeps the phase spinning at 1.48 × the merger's visual rate, a wobble for the eye, long after the strain has been zeroed.

How it's built — the coding choices

One state machine, four phases

Everything flows through a single stepPhysics: inspiral (RK4 on a, phase from ωvisual, telemetry from calcOrbitalProps), merger (a fixed 0.22 s bridge), ringdown (exponential decay at the QNM tone), remnant (kick drift, then auto-replay). Because the scope, the field, the audio, and the HUD all read the same state, the audit could lift the whole engine out and drive it from node — and because they read different parts of that state (the scope reads φ, the audio reads f, the HUD reads both), the two-clocks split was discoverable at all. The presets are just launch conditions — masses, a starting separation, a distance, a radiation-rate — dropped into the same machine; nothing about any event is scripted after the start.

The history buffer is the field's memory

The elegant choice is the 2,048-sample ring buffer of source history. A cheaper game would paint expanding sine rings from the current phase; this one records what the source actually did and lets every point of space ask what the source was doing r/cgw ago. That is why the merger flash arrives at the test-mass ring late, at the coded wave speed, and why the falloff measurement in Receipt 6 could be made at a single emission event. One recorded channel is never read back: the buffer stores the frequency too, but the field renders only phase, amplitude, and flash.

The audio is a synthesizer, honestly labelled as one

The sound is two oscillators — a sine at the mapped frequency and a triangle an octave up at low gain — through a low-pass filter, plus filtered noise for the merger thump, with the gain envelope following the strain. The mapping is 1:1 only when the event's QNM tone falls between 170 and 520 Hz; below that it stretches by 230/fQNM, above that (neutron-star events) it compresses by 460/fQNM — GW170817 is heard at 0.0735× its physical frequencies — and the result is clamped to 38–620 Hz, which is why the default event's first seconds sit on the 38 Hz floor while its panel reads 25.5 Hz. The drawer calls it a "Chirp Synth," which is exactly what it is; the note's "40 Hz → 480 Hz" band is approximate. What it is not is a sonification of strain data — real LIGO audio is the measured h(t) itself, played back.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The inspiral clock is calibrated, not physical: da/dt is the true Peters form with KPM = 1660 chosen (says the code's comment) for a ~10.5 s show. Against real Peters time over the same separations, the default event runs 24.1× slow — and because the constant ignores mass, the stretch runs from 10.3× (Extreme Ratio) to 486× (GW170817). The tc countdown is exact within the sim, but no duration on screen is comparable to a real event's, and a LIGO-style fit of the sweep returns a chirp mass of 4.2 M☉ for an event labelled 28.1.

The eye's clock is a third rate: the bodies orbit at ωvisual, "tuned for dramatic, legible real-time motion" — 0.465 rad/s at the start against a physical 80.1. The Orbits counter counts those visual turns (1.29 by merger, where the physical binary completes 8.80), and the scope's h(t) trace oscillates at the visual rate — about 0.2 Hz early on — beside its own frequency line reading 25–230 Hz. Only the audio tracks the physical frequency.

The ISCO is displayed but never happens: the panel computes a true ISCO radius (575.9 km for the default), but the dynamics ignore it — the Peters spiral rides its rails from 11.5 through 6 and down to amerge = 2.65 rg, spending 0.72 s of the default inspiral inside the ISCO still labelled "Inspiral." There is no plunge. And amerge itself is a visual-radius heuristic — max(2.65, 1.05 × (rVis₁ + rVis₂)) for black holes, 2.9 for NSBH, 3.1 for BNS — not an ISCO, a horizon contact, or (for neutron stars) a surface contact condition.

The peak is an extrapolation with a garnish: strain at merger is the inspiral quadrupole formula evaluated at 2.65 rg, multiplied by 1.42 and a sinusoidal bump up to 1.32, peaking at 5.30 × 10−21 — about five times GW150914's recorded peak — at a frequency of 231 Hz against the real merger's ≈ 150 Hz. The strain everywhere assumes a face-on, optimally oriented source; inclination and antenna-pattern dimming are not modelled.

The energy fit runs hot and its comment disagrees: the radiated-energy formula gives 5.58% for the GW150914 masses and 5.65% at equal masses, against numerical relativity's ≈ 4.65% and ≈ 4.8% — while the comment on the formula claims the NR values. The remnant mass inherits the overshoot (61.4 vs 62.0 M☉). The running counter's 42/48/10 split across inspiral/merger/ringdown is invented, and at merger it trails the orbit's own binding-energy release (1.52 shown vs 2.33 M☉ actually given up).

The ringdown's decay is not its Q: τ = 0.42 + (Q − 2) × 0.08 s is a screen mapping — 0.52 s where Q/(πf) gives 3.68 ms — so the drawn ringdown (2.30 s) is ~140× longer than the tone it displays would really ring, and the quoted Q cannot be recovered from it.

Two distance laws in one exhibit: the field and ring use the 2D cylindrical falloff 1/√r (the page's equation box prints it, and the audit verified it exactly), while the strain telemetry uses the physical 3D 1/DL. Both are labelled where they live, but a reader who drags the ring outward expecting the telemetry's law will measure the other one. The grid's static inward dent is not strain at all, and the ring's deformation — though computed with the true geodesic-deviation matrix — is fed the normalized amplitude and clamped at ±0.48, so its ΔL readouts are percent-level where the real figure is 10−21.

Preset liberties: GW200105's primary is 12 M☉ in the game against ≈ 8.9 measured (its 1.9 M☉ companion is right). "Extreme Ratio" (60 + 5, q = 1/12) is an intermediate-mass-ratio inspiral, not an EMRI. The GW170817 remnant is assigned a Kerr spin of 0.686 from the black-hole fit, though that event's remnant may not be a black hole at all. The BNS radiated fraction uses its own branch (2.4% × 4η scaling) with no published fit behind it.

Small print: dragging the mass sliders never rescales the distance — a comment in computeAstro says the fiducial distance scales with mass; the code just reads the preset's. The merger lasts exactly 0.22 s at every mass. The kick points at a fixed φmerger + 0.65π and drifts at (vkick/175) × 1.55 world units/s. The radiation-rate dial also slightly speeds the visual orbit (rate0.12). The history buffer's frequency channel is recorded every 8 ms and never read. And this game, unlike its sibling Event Horizon, carries no in-page honesty note — the equation box and tips are accurate as far as they go, but every divergence above is one the page itself never mentions.
