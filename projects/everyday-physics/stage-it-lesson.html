The Stage It Lesson — the physics behind the game

The back door to Stage It, the two-stage rocket game in Everyday Physics · Flight. The game is the front door — you learn by flying. This page is the same lesson with the sums showing: the physics, the maths, the answers the game's own proof runs produced, and how the game is built. If you opened this from the pop-up mid-flight, your rocket is paused and waiting.

The game in one paragraph

You are given one rocket — a payload, some structure, some fuel — and one decision before launch: how to split it into two stages. Then you fly it straight up, and at the moment the lower stage runs dry you choose when to throw it away. Reach the orbit line at the mission's orbital speed and you win; your score is the Δv still in the tanks. Every number the game shows you — every stage's Δv, the budget bar, the verdict on your design — comes out of one equation, computed live. This page takes that equation apart, then shows you the flights the game's proof pilot actually flew.

The physics

The tyranny: to lift fuel, you must burn fuel

A rocket does not carry its fuel the way a car does. A car's tank is a small fraction of the car. A rocket is mostly tank: in this game, 24 tonnes of a 27-tonne stack is fuel and structure wrapped around fuel. And the fuel is not just along for the ride — every kilogram of it has to be accelerated by burning other kilograms of it. The deeper you dig, the worse it gets: the fuel that lifts the fuel itself needs lifting. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky wrote down the exact shape of this trap in 1903, and it has not loosened since: the velocity a rocket can gain grows only with the logarithm of how much heavier it starts than it finishes. Double the fuel and you do not double the speed — you add a fixed, grudging increment. That is the tyranny.

Staging: the escape

There is one cheat, and every real rocket uses it. The moment a tank runs empty, throw the tank away. Structure you no longer carry stops taxing every burn that follows: the remaining fuel no longer pays to accelerate dead metal, so the mass ratio — full versus empty — resets in your favour for the next stage. A two-stage rocket is not one rocket with a seam in it; it is two rockets, the second one launched from the top of the first, already moving, already above the thickest air. The game makes you feel the size of the prize: on Level 2, staging is worth the whole mission.

Δv: the budget everything is priced in

Rocket people don't budget in fuel or in speed; they budget in Δv (delta-v) — the total change in velocity a rocket can still produce. It is the one currency that gravity, drag, and the mission all accept. The design screen prices your split in Δv, the mission states its cost in Δv, and your score at cutoff is the Δv you didn't spend. Learn to think in Δv and the whole game — and a fair slice of real spaceflight — turns into bookkeeping.

Gravity collects rent

While you climb, gravity is pulling you back at 9.8 m/s², every second, whether your engine is burning or not. Climb for 75 seconds and gravity has taken roughly 735 m/s of your Δv — not spent on speed, just rent for being in the sky that long. That is why the mission budget sits above the orbital speed (Level 2 wants 4,800 m/s of speed but budgets 5,600 m/s of Δv: the extra 800 is gravity and drag's expected take). And it is why hauling an empty stage hurts twice: the dead structure dilutes your mass ratio and every extra second aloft is another 9.8 m/s of rent. When the game flashes "STAGE 1 IS DRY," it is not being dramatic. It is reading your meter.

The math

The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, one stage at a time:

Δv = vₑ · ln(m_full / m_empty)
SymbolWhat it isIn this game
ΔvThe velocity change this stage can deliverwhat you're budgeting
vₑExhaust velocity — how fast the engine throws mass out the back. Faster exhaust, more push per kilogram.3,000 m/s, constant
m_fullMass at ignition — everything this stage has to move, including everything stacked on top of itfrom the design table
m_emptyMass when this stage's fuel is gone — its structure, plus everything above itfrom the design table
lnThe natural logarithm — where the tyranny lives. Δv grows with the log of the ratio, so ratios multiply but Δv only adds.—

How two stages add

Price the top stage first: its m_full is its fuel + its structure + the payload; its m_empty is just its structure + the payload. Then price the lower stage: its m_full is the entire stack, and its m_empty is its own structure plus the whole upper stage, still full — because when stage 1 burns out, everything above it is untouched. Add the two Δv figures. That sum, and nothing fancier, is the design screen's total.

The game's own worked example — Level 1 at a 50/50 split

Level 1: payload 1,000 kg, structure 2,000 kg, fuel 24,000 kg. A 50/50 split gives each stage 12,000 kg of fuel and 1,000 kg of structure.

Stage 2: Δv = 3,000 · ln(14,000 / 2,000) = 3,000 · ln(7) = 3,000 · 1.9459 = 5,837.73 m/s
Stage 1: Δv = 3,000 · ln(27,000 / 15,000) = 3,000 · ln(1.8) = 3,000 · 0.5878 = 1,763.36 m/s
Total: 5,837.73 + 1,763.36 = 7,601.09 m/s — against a mission budget of 5,400 m/s

Read the two lines against each other and you can see the whole game. Stage 1's ratio is poor — 27,000 down to 15,000 — because it spends its burn lifting the entire stack, most of which is the upper stage's fuel. Stage 2 flies light, with a ratio of 7, and the logarithm pays it nearly three times the Δv for the same fuel. The lower stage's job is not to be efficient; it is to get the upper stage up and moving, then leave.

Worked examples — from the game's own proof runs

Before this game shipped, a scripted pilot flew every level at full throttle across the whole range of splits, staging exactly at dry. These are its verified results — the same numbers the game's proof test still checks.

The winning splits

1 · First Orbit95 / 52,712 m/s to spare

The pattern is real rocket practice: a big first stage to do the heavy lifting, a small upper stage to finish the job light. But note where the winners stop — none of them is 100/0. Take the split all the way and there is no second stage at all, which is the next exhibit.

The tyranny exhibit — Level 2 flown as a single stage

Put all of Level 2's fuel in one stage and the equation gives:

Δv = 3,000 · ln(20,000 / 4,000) = 3,000 · ln(5) = 4,828 m/s — against a budget of 5,600 m/s

Already short on paper. And the flight agrees: the scripted pilot — full throttle, flown perfectly — dies at 4,418 m/s of the 4,800 m/s needed, tanks dry, at 64 km. No skill fixes that flight, because the arithmetic said no before the engine lit. Staging the same rocket at 80/20 reaches orbit with 703 m/s to spare. Same fuel, same structure, same payload — the only difference is throwing the empty part away.

The named trap — Level 1 at 5/95

Starve the first stage and a different law bites. Engines in this game are sized from structure: 400 N of thrust per kilogram. A 5% share gives stage 1 just 100 kg of structure — a 40 kN engine — under a 27,000 kg stack that weighs 265 kN. Thrust below weight: the rocket sits on the pad and burns. The proof test asserts this exact outcome: status no-liftoff, every time.

Late separation, measured

Fly Level 2's winning 80/20 split but wait 10 seconds past dry before dropping stage 1, and the margin falls from 703 m/s to 584 m/s — 119 m/s gone. Gravity's rent accounts for most of it: 9.8 m/s for every one of those ten seconds, charged on a stack that is coasting on an empty tank instead of burning its second stage. Ten seconds of tidiness, 119 m/s of mission. The drop button is a Δv decision, not housekeeping.

How it's built — the coding choices

One core, three customers

Every scrap of physics lives in a single block of code (called STAGEIT) that knows nothing about the page: no buttons, no canvas, no screen. Three different customers use it. The design screen calls its Δv function every time you move the slider, to fill the table and the budget bar. The flight calls its step function fifty times a second to move the rocket. And the proof test lifts the exact same block out of the shipped page and runs it headlessly, with no browser at all. Because all three run the same code, the Δv the design screen promises is the Δv the flight delivers — there is no second, friendlier set of numbers for the brochure. No bait and switch.

The slider is the lesson

The most important control in the game is not the throttle; it's the split slider, and it sits before the flight on purpose. Moving it, you watch stage 1's Δv, stage 2's Δv, and the budget verdict change under your own hand — the staging trade-off is something you do, not something you're told. The flight then grades the decision you already understand. Games usually teach by letting you fail; this one first lets you calculate, then lets you fail with your eyes open.

Engines sized by a stated rule

One line of the design does a lot of teaching: thrust = 400 N per kilogram of stage structure. It's what makes the 5/95 trap possible (a starved stage gets a starved engine), what makes a bigger first stage climb out more briskly, and it's printed on the design screen rather than buried — a rule you can plan around beats a surprise you can't.

A clock that can't cheat

The flight steps the physics at a fixed 0.02 s per step, fed by an accumulator: the display can stutter, the speed button can triple the rate, and the physics still advances in identical steps. (That is also what makes the in-game pop-up fair — opening this lesson freezes the step loop, and closing it resumes the same flight, untouched.) Integration is semi-implicit Euler: simple, stable, and honest at this scale — the worked examples above were produced by this very integrator, not by the ideal equation alone, which is why the flown numbers sit a little below the paper ones.

The proof pilot: turning "unwinnable" into a proof

The proof test flies a scripted pilot — full throttle, stage exactly at dry — across every split on the 5% grid, for every level. That script is a perfect-pilot bound: it never hesitates, never mistimes, never wastes a burn. So when it cannot reach orbit as a single stage on Level 2, that is not an opinion about difficulty — it is a proof that no player can, flown any way at all. And when it does reach orbit, the level is certified winnable and its margin is a real number, not a vibe. Every figure in the worked examples came out of that bound.

Simplifications, named

The world is simplified; the arithmetic is not. The Δv figures are the real Tsiolkovsky equation, computed live from the masses on the table. What follows is everything the world leaves out.

  • 1-D vertical ascent. No steering and no gravity turn. Real orbit is mostly sideways speed; this game teaches the speed ingredient of orbit on its own, and says so on the pad.
  • Constant gravity, 9.8 m/s². Real gravity thins with altitude — about 3% less at 100 km — so the game's rent is very slightly overcharged near the top.
  • Constant exhaust velocity, 3,000 m/s — a fair chemical-rocket figure, held steady so the equation stays readable.
  • Light quadratic drag with a single coefficient for the whole rocket — real drag depends on shape, speed regime, and thinning air.
  • Structure splits in the same proportion as fuel, tanks and engine together. Real stages are engineered one by one; the proportional rule is the game's stated simplification, and it's what makes the slider a single honest dial.
  • Instant separation and ignition. Real staging involves ullage, settling, and a held breath. Here, the drop is a clean cut — the cost of lateness is kept, the mechanics of the moment are not.
