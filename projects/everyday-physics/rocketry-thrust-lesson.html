The Rocketry & Thrust Lesson — the physics behind the exhibit

The back door to Rocketry & Thrust — the Physics of Rockets, in Everyday Physics. The exhibit is the front door — you learn by throwing bricks, opening nozzles and launching. This page is the same lesson with the sums showing: Newton's third law as momentum bookkeeping, the thrust equation term by term, specific impulse, the rocket equation and why staging works, gravity and drag losses, nozzles, the Δv budget, model-rocket motor codes, and the numbers the exhibit's own proof runs produced.

The exhibit in one paragraph

Nine benches, one idea: a rocket gains speed by throwing mass backwards. A cart on frictionless rails throws bricks and conserves momentum to fourteen decimal places. The thrust equation splits into its momentum and pressure terms as you change chamber pressure, throat size and altitude. A nozzle bench shows the gas reaching Mach 1 at the throat and then accelerating in the widening bell — and what happens when the exit pressure is too low or too high for the air outside. A table of real engines, every number sourced, compares specific impulse. The rocket equation and staging are a calculator you can push against the 9.4 km/s it takes to reach low Earth orbit. A vertical liftoff on a fixed 1/60 s timestep keeps the books on gravity and drag losses. A Δv budget totals the trips to the Moon and Mars. A model-rocket bench decodes motor codes like C6-5 and flies them, alongside the NAR Safety Code. And an orbit bench lets you throw sideways from 400 km and watch the ball miss the Earth.

The physics

Newton's third law is momentum bookkeeping

When the person on the cart throws a brick of mass m backwards at speed u (measured from the cart), the brick and the cart push on each other with equal and opposite forces for the same instant, so their momentum changes are equal and opposite. Total momentum — zero before the throw — stays zero. A rocket is the same cart throwing a continuous stream of very small, very fast bricks. Nothing outside is needed: no ground, no air. That is why rockets work best in a vacuum, and why the New York Times was wrong in 1920 (see the stories below).

Thrust: a momentum term and a pressure term

F = ṁ·ve + (pe − pa)·Ae

ṁ is the propellant mass flow (kg/s), ve the exhaust speed at the nozzle exit (m/s), pe the exhaust pressure at the exit plane (Pa), pa the outside air pressure (Pa) and Ae the exit area (m²). The first term is the brick-throwing. The second is a correction for the fact that the exhaust still pushes outward on the open end of the nozzle with pe while the air pushes back with pa. At sea level an engine built for vacuum has pe far below pa, so the second term is negative. In vacuum, pa = 0 and the same engine gains exactly p₀·Ae of thrust compared with sea level (p₀ = 101 325 Pa) — one of the exhibit's proofs.

Specific impulse: the exhaust speed in disguise

Isp = F / (ṁ·g₀)   ⇔   ve,eff = Isp·g₀,    g₀ = 9.80665 m/s² (exactly)

Specific impulse is thrust per unit weight-flow of propellant, measured in seconds. g₀ is the defined standard gravity, not local gravity: it is only a unit conversion, and the exhibit uses 9.80665 m/s² everywhere. A Merlin 1D at sea level (282 s) has an effective exhaust speed of 282 × 9.80665 = 2 765 m/s; an RS-25 in vacuum (452.3 s) reaches 4 436 m/s. Higher Isp means each kilogram of propellant buys more momentum.

The rocket equation and the tyranny of the logarithm

Δv = Isp·g₀·ln(m₀ / mf)

m₀ is the mass at ignition and mf the mass when the propellant is gone. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published it in 1903. Because of the logarithm, doubling the mass ratio adds only a fixed amount of speed, Isp·g₀·ln 2. To reach 9.4 km/s with Isp 350 s in one stage you need a mass ratio of e9400/(350 × 9.80665) ≈ 15.5: 93.5% of the rocket must be propellant, and the tanks, engines and payload must all fit into the other 6.5%. At 282 s (kerosene at sea level) the ratio would be about 30. That is why orbital rockets come in stages: dropping empty tanks means the next stage's mass ratio starts fresh.

Staging, in numbers

The staging bench splits the same 500 t rocket, carrying 10 t of payload with structure at 8% of each stage's mass, into 1–4 equal-ratio stages at Isp 330 s. One stage gives 7.50 km/s — not enough for orbit. Two give 10.10 km/s, three 10.77, four 11.05. The first split matters most; after that the gains shrink while the cost and failure points grow. That is why real orbital rockets mostly have two or three stages.

Thrust-to-weight and the losses

A rocket on the pad lifts off only when thrust exceeds weight (TWR > 1). With TWR 0.9 the liftoff bench shows the rocket sitting on the pad burning propellant until it is light enough — pure waste. Once airborne, two losses take a bite from the ideal Δv: gravity loss (∫g·sin γ dt; flying straight up, every second of burn costs about 9.8 m/s) and drag loss (∫D/m dt). The exhibit adds both up step by step and checks that burnout speed = ideal − gravity loss − drag loss. Real launches fly a gravity turn to keep the gravity loss down to roughly 1–1.5 km/s; the straight-up bench is deliberately the worst case.

Nozzles: why the bell is shaped like that

In a converging nozzle, subsonic gas speeds up as the area shrinks. If the pressure drop is large enough, the gas reaches exactly Mach 1 at the narrowest point, the throat. Past the throat something counterintuitive happens: supersonic gas speeds up as the area grows. The bell turns pressure and heat into exhaust speed, and the area ratio Ae/At sets the exit Mach number through the isentropic area–Mach relation. A large bell gives a fast, low-pressure exhaust — ideal in vacuum. At sea level that same exhaust is over-expanded (pe < pa): the air squeezes the plume, shock diamonds form, and if pe drops below roughly 0.4·pa (the Summerfield criterion) the flow can separate from the wall and damage the nozzle. A short bell is under-expanded at altitude (pe > pa): the plume balloons out and some energy is left unused. That is why a Merlin 1D Vacuum has a huge nozzle and cannot be fired at sea level, and why first-stage nozzles are a compromise.

The Δv budget

Mission planners add up Δv rather than distance. The exhibit's table (after the Wikipedia Delta-v budget figures) is: Earth's surface → low Earth orbit 9.4 km/s, including roughly 1.5–2 km/s of gravity and drag losses; LEO → trans-lunar injection 3.2; → low lunar orbit 0.9; → Moon's surface 1.87; LEO → Mars transfer 3.8; → Mars capture 0.9; → low Mars orbit 1.4; → Mars's surface 4.1 if flown entirely on rockets. Real Mars landers use aerobraking for most of the descent, and the table says so. Treat the figures as typical values: real missions vary with trajectory, launch window and how much aerobraking they use.

Model rocket motor codes

A code like C6-5 packs three facts. The letter is the total-impulse class, and each letter doubles the one before: A is 1.26–2.50 N·s, B 2.51–5.00, C 5.01–10.0, D 10.01–20.0, and so on. The number is the average thrust in newtons. The last number is the delay in seconds between burnout and the ejection charge that deploys the parachute (0 means a booster stage, P plugged). An Estes C6-5 delivers 8.8 N·s at an average of 4.7 N for about 1.9 s. The NAR Model Rocket Safety Code sets the rules: no more than 1 500 g at liftoff, no more than 125 g of propellant and 320 N·s of total impulse; a minimum launch-site size that grows with the motor class; 15 ft (4.6 m) between spectators and D-or-smaller motors, 30 ft (9.1 m) for larger; no launches in winds over 20 mph. Our motor flights use average thrust rather than the full thrust curve, a Cd of 0.75 and no wind. Use them to understand the physics, not to plan a real launch.

Orbit: falling and missing

Throw sideways fast enough and the ground curves away beneath you as fast as you fall. At 400 km altitude that speed is vc = √(μ/r) = 7.67 km/s (μ = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s², r = 6 771 km), and one lap takes 92.4 minutes — the International Space Station's orbit. Slower and the orbit becomes an ellipse that dips into the atmosphere. At √2 times circular speed (10.85 km/s at 400 km) the path no longer closes: escape. Most of the 9.4 km/s to orbit is spent on going sideways, not up. Satellite Watch shows the real satellites doing this right now.

The ideal nozzle runs a few percent high, and that is honest. The bench assumes frozen, one-dimensional, isentropic flow with one γ per propellant: no friction, no heat loss to the walls, no unburned or dissociated gas. Real engines lose a few percent to all of those, so the Merlin-like model (294 s) runs about 4% above the published 282 s. The RS-25 model (449 s vs 452.3 s) happens to land very close because hydrogen engines suit the ideal model well. Both stay within the stated 5%.

Two stories

The New York Times and Robert Goddard

On 13 January 1920 a New York Times editorial mocked Robert Goddard, who had just proposed that a rocket could reach the Moon. It said he lacked "the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools" because a rocket would have nothing to push against in a vacuum. Bench 1 shows why that is wrong: the rocket pushes against its own exhaust. On 17 July 1969, the day after Apollo 11 launched, the Times printed a correction: "it is now definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum as well as in an atmosphere. The Times regrets the error."

The tyranny of the rocket equation

Astronaut Don Pettit wrote an essay with that title for NASA in 2012, from the International Space Station. A soda can, he pointed out, is about 94% drink and 6% can; the Space Shuttle's external tank was about 96% propellant and 4% tank. Rockets have to be built thinner, relative to what they hold, than a soda can. That is the logarithm in the rocket equation made physical.

How the exhibit is built

One core, between the ROCKET-CORE markers in the page source, serves all nine benches and the proofs. Its parts are:

  • the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere up to 86 km, with an exponential tail above;
  • the rocket equation and equal-ratio staging;
  • an isentropic nozzle that solves the area–Mach relation by bisection;
  • a vertical-flight RK4 integrator with thrust varying with altitude as F(h) = Fvac − (Fvac − Fsl)·p(h)/p₀, quadratic drag and inverse-square gravity, that books every loss;
  • a two-body RK4 orbit propagator;
  • the motor and engine tables, with sources.

Animation uses the Physics room's clock: fixed 1/60 s steps, at most 100 ms of real time and six steps per frame, with leftover time dropped after a stall so a background tab never comes back to a teleported rocket. With reduced motion turned on, every bench computes its final result at once instead of animating. No external scripts, fonts or images are loaded. The page works offline.

Deliberate simplifications: the liftoff bench flies straight up (no gravity turn), which pushes the acceleration near burnout towards 10 g on a single stage; the model-rocket flights use average thrust; the orbit bench ignores the atmosphere and the Earth's rotation.
