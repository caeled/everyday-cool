Everyday Physics · The back door to Lattice

Lattice — the lesson

Lattice is a working crystal on a screen: 182 atoms holding each other in a hexagonal sheet, every one of them pulling on every neighbour within reach through the Lennard-Jones force — the same toy force that real molecular-dynamics codes are built from. You can pluck it and watch phonons run, heat it until it melts, shear it until dislocations glide, and boil it into a gas and anneal it back. This page is the other half of the lab: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that checked it — including the proof that its phonon frequencies match its own force law, the measurement of where its crystal actually melts, and the discovery that its defect counter cannot see a vacancy at all.

Lattice was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

How to play

There is no score and no win screen — Lattice is a bench, and the experiments are the game. Six curated scenarios set the bench up; everything after that is yours.

  • Hexagonal Phonons — a cold hexagonal crystal with a compression pulse already running through it. Drag any atom (or flick across a row) with the Pluck tool to launch your own waves.
  • Diatomic Waves — a checkerboard of light and heavy atoms (mass ratio 3.5×) with the optical mode excited: the two sublattices swinging against each other. Receipt 7 measures its frequency.
  • Melt & Anneal — the thermostat drags the crystal to 165 K. Watch the order parameter fall apart, then try the Anneal 15 K button and watch it find its way back.
  • Hypervelocity Impact — a projectile at 11.2 σ/τ slams the sheet. The Impact Hammer tool lets you aim your own.
  • Defect Scattering — three vacancies and four heavy impurities (mass 5) carved into the sheet, with a wavefront arriving to diffract off them.
  • Shear Dislocation — a steady shear stress with a notch cut in one edge to seed a gliding 5–7 dislocation. Receipt 6 reports what the stress actually does to a free crystal.

The tools are on keys 1–4: Pluck, Impact Hammer, Dope/Vacancy (delete an atom, plant a heavy impurity, or insert one), and Shear (drag the plates). The drawer holds the rest: wave triggers (Longitudinal Pulse, Transverse Wave, Optical Mode, Fire Projectile), the shear slider, the symmetry switch (Hexagonal / Square / Diatomic), the mass-ratio slider, the thermostat with its Anneal / Melt / Boil presets, damping, and the colour overlays — bond strain, atom temperature, order, species, displacement. The telemetry strip reads temperature, order Ψ, defect count, kinetic and potential energy, mean coordination ⟨Z⟩, and the clock in picoseconds. Read those numbers the way this lesson does: as claims, each one checked below.

The physics

Why atoms sit where they sit — the Lennard-Jones bargain

Two neutral atoms negotiate. Push them together and their electron clouds repel, fiercely and at short range. Pull them apart and a gentler attraction — the van der Waals force — tugs them back. The Lennard-Jones potential is the standard cartoon of that bargain:

V(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶] The r⁻¹² term is the repulsion (a convenient stand-in for the real, steeper physics); the r⁻⁶ term is the genuine van der Waals attraction. The two balance at r₀ = 21/6σ ≈ 1.122σ, where the well bottoms out at depth −ε. σ sets the size of an atom, ε the strength of the bond. Everything in the lab is measured in these units.

A crystal is what happens when hundreds of atoms strike that bargain simultaneously. In two dimensions the winning arrangement is the triangular (hexagonal) lattice: six neighbours each, every bond near the bottom of its well. The game builds its sheet at a spacing of 1.114σ and lets the force law do the rest — no springs are drawn in; the bonds you see are just pairs close enough to matter (within 1.46σ, the game's bond cutoff). Because the force reaches out to 2.5σ, each atom in the sheet actually feels 18 neighbours — three hexagonal shells at σ·1.114, σ·1.93, and σ·2.23 — which is part of why the sheet behaves like a material and not a trampoline.

Phonons: the crystal's notes

Disturb one atom and the disturbance does not stay put. It propagates as a wave in which the atoms oscillate in organised patterns called normal modes; a quantum of such a wave is a phonon — sound and heat, carried as a particle-like packet. Each wavevector k (a wavelength and direction) has its own frequency ω(k), and the map of those frequencies is the dispersion relation — the crystal's sheet music. For the textbook one-dimensional chain of masses and springs it is exact:

ω(k) = 2√(κ/m) · |sin(ka/2)| κ is the spring stiffness, a the spacing. Two lessons live in this curve: at long wavelength ω ≈ √(κ/m)·ka — frequency proportional to k, which is just sound, travelling at c = a√(κ/m) no matter the pitch — and at the shortest possible wavelength (neighbouring atoms in antiphase, ka = π) the frequency tops out and stops growing. A crystal has a highest note. The 2D sheet in the game has a richer score — longitudinal and transverse branches, different in different directions — but the same two lessons. Receipt 4 reads the game's actual sheet music off its own force law and then plays the notes to check.

A diatomic crystal — two masses alternating — splits the music into two bands. The acoustic branch is sound: neighbours swinging together. The optical branch is the sublattices swinging against each other, at high frequency even for the longest wavelength, so named because in real ionic crystals those modes couple to light. The game's Diatomic Waves scenario excites exactly that antiphase mode.

Temperature is motion you can count

In this lab, temperature is not a substance poured in; it is the average kinetic energy of the atoms, full stop. Equipartition says each quadratic degree of freedom carries ½kBT of energy; an atom moving in two dimensions has two, so:

T* = ⟨KE⟩ per atom   (in units where ε = kB = 1) The HUD's kelvin are this number × 300 — a chosen scale, examined in the honest box. Heating the crystal means making its atoms move faster; a phonon is heat, organised. When the crystal melts, the same energy stops sloshing around fixed sites and starts carrying the atoms themselves away — which is why the audit detects melting by watching whether atoms still live where they started.

Melting, defects, and why crystals are weak

A solid melts when its atoms' thermal excursions reach a fair fraction of the spacing — the Lindemann criterion puts the RMS excursion at roughly a tenth of the bond length — and the lattice loses its grip. In a small free cluster like this one there is no single melting point: the surface, where atoms have fewer neighbours, lets go first, and the disorder eats inward over a range of temperatures. Receipt 5 maps that range in the game.

Real crystals are also full of defects, and the defects run the mechanical show. A vacancy is a missing atom. An impurity is a wrong atom. A dislocation is a half-row of atoms inserted or removed, visible in a hexagonal sheet as a bound pair of 5-coordinated and 7-coordinated atoms — a 5–7 pair. Dislocations are why real metals yield at a hundredth of their ideal strength: instead of shearing a whole plane at once, the crystal moves the dislocation line one step, the way you move a rug by walking a wrinkle across it. Each pass of a dislocation shifts the crystal by one Burgers vector — one lattice spacing — and that shift is permanent. That permanence is the difference between elastic and plastic, and Receipt 6 measures it.

One more quiet phenomenon the lab gets right for free: thermal expansion. The Lennard-Jones well is lopsided — steep on the repulsive side, gentle on the attractive side — so as atoms vibrate harder they spend more time at larger separations, and the mean bond length grows. No expansion coefficient is coded anywhere. It falls out of the asymmetry of the well, and Receipt 5 weighs it.

The math the game runs

The simulation works in reduced Lennard-Jones units: σ = ε = light-atom mass = 1, pair force evaluated for every pair within a 2.5σ cutoff found by a spatial hash grid. Two adjustments are made to the textbook potential, both standard practice in real MD codes and both measured in Receipt 1. The force is shifted so it reaches exactly zero at the cutoff instead of being chopped:

F(r) = 24[2(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶]/r − F(rcut),   rcut = 2.5σ F(rcut) = −0.0390 ε/σ. The shift is small everywhere and decisive at the cutoff, where an unshifted force would kick every atom that strays across the boundary.

Positions advance by velocity Verlet — half-kick, drift, recompute forces, half-kick — at a fixed dt = 0.0048 time units, seven substeps per animation frame at 1× speed, so the crystal lives about 2.0 time units per real second. The clock on the HUD converts at 1 unit ≈ 2.15 ps, which is argon's natural time unit σ√(m/ε) — the lab's internal calendar is argon-flavoured even where its thermometer is not (see the honest box).

Square and diatomic crystals get one extra ingredient, because Lennard-Jones alone will not hold a square — see Receipt 7. It is a three-body angle energy in the Stillinger–Weber spirit, applied to every pair of bonds at an atom:

Uangle = λ₃ · w(r₁)w(r₂) · cos²θ sin²θ,   λ₃ = 6.2ε θ is the angle between two bonds; the w factors fade the term smoothly to zero as a bond stretches toward 1.46σ, so it lets go cleanly when the crystal melts. The cos²θ sin²θ shape is zero at θ = 0°, 90°, and 180° — the angles a square lattice is made of — and maximal at 45°, the angle a square must not have. Receipt 7 removes it in a control copy and watches the square fall.

The order parameter behind the HUD's Ψ is the standard bond-orientational order of condensed-matter physics, ψm(i) = (1/Z)Σ exp(imθij) summed over an atom's bonded neighbours — m = 6 for the hexagonal sheet, m = 4 for the square ones. How the HUD blends that quantity into its single number is its own story, told in Receipt 8. The thermostat is a Berendsen weak-coupling rescale: every step, all velocities are multiplied by a factor λ = √(1 + rate·(Ttarget/T − 1)) with rate = coupling × dt × 18 and λ clamped to [0.85, 1.15] — a gentle exponential drag of the kinetic temperature toward the target, examined in Receipt 3.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit loaded the shipped file's own script headlessly — same constants, same force routine, same Verlet step, same telemetry code — with the random generator seeded so every run is reproducible, and drove it through the game's own presets and controls. Every number below is measured from that code, not quoted from the page.

The bends are in the fine print, and they are the professional ones: the coded pair well bottoms at 1.124σ with depth −0.930ε, not the textbook 1.122σ and −1.000ε (the force shift flattens the well by 7%), and below 0.76σ the 1/r¹² wall is quietly replaced by a soft linear core — at 0.5σ the game pushes with 1,771 ε/σ where the true potential pushes with 390,144, a factor of 220 softer, joined seamlessly at 0.76σ (1,537.0 from both formulas). That safeguard is what lets the hypervelocity impactor exist without exploding the integrator. The chamber walls are likewise harmonic springs (k = 55) with a hard, slightly lossy clamp behind them.

Receipt 2: the integrator does not leak

The default crystal (182 atoms, thermostat off, no damping) was run for 30,000 Verlet steps — 144 time units. Total energy moved by +0.0004% end to end, with the whole excursion band inside 0.0005%. Velocity Verlet doing exactly what it promises. One safeguard does bite in normal play: any atom faster than 20 σ/τ is silently scaled back to 20 — an atom set to 30 measured exactly 20.0 after one step. The impact preset's 11.2 σ/τ projectile flies under the cap; a harder throw of your own may not.

Receipt 3: temperature is honest — and the build temperature halves itself

The HUD temperature is exactly 300 × (kinetic energy per atom), verified against an independent sum over the velocity arrays — the correct 2D equipartition definition, no fudge factor. But watch what a fresh crystal does with it. assignThermalVelocities loads all the thermal energy into motion and none into stretched bonds; a solid at equilibrium splits its vibrational energy half kinetic, half potential, so the crystal's temperature falls by half as it settles:

Built atEquilibrated temperature (thermostat off)
6 K (Hexagonal Phonons preset family)3.1 K

The thermostat repairs this wherever it is switched on — driven from 5.3 K to a 165 K target at the default coupling, the kinetic temperature covered 63% of the climb in 400 steps (≈1.9 time units) and 90% in 770, landing on 165.6 K. The approach is about 2.8× slower than the raw rescaling rate suggests, precisely because the solid keeps parking half of every injection in potential energy. The thermostat itself is Berendsen rescaling and nothing else — no random force, so it is not the "Langevin" partner the code's own header comment advertises — and it does not sample a true canonical ensemble: at a 120 K hold, the instantaneous temperature fluctuated with a standard deviation of 5.0%, against the 7.4% a canonical ensemble of 182 atoms requires (1/√N) and the 6.4% the same crystal showed with the bath unplugged. Close, calm, and measurably too calm — the known Berendsen signature.

Now you know.

The sheet is a true solid at 90 K and a true liquid by 125 K, with the crossover — surface first, as a free cluster should — running roughly 100–125 K (T* ≈ 0.33–0.42). The code's own header comment says the crystal "melts ~120–155 K": by the bottom of that range the game's crystal is already most of the way gone, and the 165 K Melt preset overshoots into a runny, evaporating droplet (⟨Z⟩ 2.74 is a droplet coming apart, not a calm liquid). Nothing here is scripted — no atom is ever told to melt; the disorder is the force law's own verdict — but it is a cluster's melting, broad and surface-led, not a bulk first-order jump. The same runs weigh thermal expansion, which is coded nowhere and happens anyway: mean bond length 1.1155σ cold, 1.169σ at 90 K (+4.8%), 1.203σ at 165 K (+7.9%) — the lopsided well doing its quiet work. Cooling tells the hysteresis half of the story: melted at 165 K and cooled to 110 K, the sheet stays liquid-like (order 0.48, atoms still wandering 2.6σ) — supercooling, unscripted — and only at 70 K does it recrystallize (order 0.89, RMS back to 0.15σ), keeping 8 counted defects as souvenirs. The Boil preset is real too: at 430 K the cluster disperses to ⟨Z⟩ = 1.99 with only 44 of 182 atoms still within 6σ of the centre, the phase label reads gas, and the Anneal button's promise checks out — cooled back to 15 K, the vapour recondenses and recrystallizes to order 0.94.

Receipt 6: the defect counter cannot see a vacancy — and shear is a somersault that yields

One atom was deleted from the centre of a perfect cold crystal, exactly what the Defect Scattering preset does three times. The HUD's defect count read zero. The mechanism is beautiful: the counter flags an interior atom only if its coordination is wrong and its ψ₆ falls below 0.78 — but for a hexagonal site, every neighbour contributes the same phasor to ψ₆ (6θ is a whole number of turns for all six bond directions), so the five neighbours of a hole keep ψ ≈ 0.996 and the vacancy is invisible until the lattice around it badly distorts. The counter is a disorder meter, not a defect census: it counts heavy impurities one-for-one (one impurity → one defect, verified) and it catches dislocation cores, whose angles genuinely scramble. It will not count your vacancies, and it undercounts gentle grain boundaries for the same reason.

The shear preset, run as shipped (τ = 1.45, notch cut, 28 K bath), shows what the counter can see: bursts of up to 16 counted defects with order dipping to 0.77, healing back, bursting again — dislocations nucleating, gliding, and annihilating in cycles. At a third of the stress (τ = 0.45) the same crystal ran 144 time units with zero defects and order never below 0.98: there is a real yield threshold between the two. After the preset's stress was released, a rotation-corrected residual slip of ≈1.2σ remained — about one Burgers vector of permanent, plastic offset, against +0.12σ for the elastic control. But hold the picture loosely: the "shear" is applied as a force couple to the top and bottom layers of a free cluster, with nothing gripping it, so the cluster tumbles through full revolutions while it yields — the Kabsch rotation angle wrapped past ±180° repeatedly in both runs. The glide is real; the geometry is a somersault, not a vise.

Receipt 7: the square lattice is held up by one term — remove it and it falls

The page says square and diatomic crystals carry "a smooth 90° bond-angle term." The audit ran the control the claim invites: a square crystal at 5 K in the shipped code, and the same crystal in a copy with only the angle term switched off (the shipped file itself untouched).

Exactly as the physics says it must be: a Lennard-Jones square is mechanically unstable — its atoms slide into the triangular arrangement at the first opportunity — and the λ₃ = 6.2ε angle term, six times the pair well depth, is the entire reason the Square and Diatomic benches exist. The diatomic bench repays its rent immediately. Its optical mode, excited with the game's own pulse, oscillated at ω = 14.57; the Rayleigh quotient of the game's own force routine under the same antiphase pattern predicts 14.05 (whole cluster) to 14.79 (bulk atoms only). The antiphase swing is a real normal mode at the frequency the force law sets — and the pulse that excites it is almost momentum-balanced: it leaves a net drift momentum of (0.122, 0.026), about 0.2% of what it injects, because the Gaussian envelope does not weight the two sublattices quite equally.

Receipt 8: two HUD numbers are not what their labels say

"Order Ψ₆" is a blend: 0.65 × the mean local |ψm| (summed over bonded atoms but divided by all atoms, so unbonded atoms drag it down) plus 0.35 × the global orientational coherence, then smoothed by an exponential average over frames. It is a good disorder gauge — the melt scan above leans on it — but it is not the bare Ψ₆ of the textbooks, and on the square and diatomic benches the underlying quantity is ψ₄ anyway, whatever the label says. "Phonon RMS" is the RMS displacement of atoms from their build positions, and that reference frame is the problem: thermal draws give the free cluster a net angular momentum that nothing ever removes, so the whole crystal slowly rotates like a merry-go-round. In one audited run a perfect crystal at 3 K, order 0.997, rotated −19.9° over 144 time units, and its "Phonon RMS" read 2.12σ — while the true vibrational amplitude, with rigid rotation and translation fitted out, was 0.018σ. The readout overstated the phonons by a factor of 117. After a melt-and-recrystallize cycle it read 7.68σ in a crystal whose true RMS was 0.15σ. As a "how much history has this crystal lived" meter it is evocative; as a phonon amplitude it is measuring the merry-go-round.

How it's built — the coding choices

One force routine, preallocated everything

All physics flows through a single computeForcesAndOrder: a spatial hash grid (cell = the 2.5σ cutoff) finds the pairs, the shifted-force Lennard-Jones is applied with Newton's third law, the three-body angle term follows for square lattices, then chamber walls, the shear bands, and the pluck spring are added into the same force arrays — and the same pass accumulates the bond list, coordination numbers, and ψ phasors that the telemetry and the renderer both read. State lives in preallocated typed arrays (Float64 positions and velocities, 360 atoms maximum), so the hot loop allocates nothing. That discipline is why the audit could lift the routine out and interrogate it: two atoms in, a force out, matching the analytic formula to the last digit.

The scenarios are initial conditions, not scripts

Each preset is a build, a temperature, a thermostat setting, and at most one kick: the impact is a 2.2-mass atom placed at (−11.8, 0.2) with velocity 11.2; the defect scene is three atoms removed and four re-massed to 5.0; the shear scene is one notch atom deleted and a constant band force. Nothing is animated by hand afterward — the dislocation cascade, the diffraction off the vacancies, the recrystallization with its leftover defects all emerge from the force law, which is why the audit's job was measurement rather than fact-checking a script. The file is fully self-contained — no external references, no storage, no network — and its one flourish is honest about being a flourish: plucks, dopings, and impacts are sonified as pentatonic marimba-bell tones, pitch mapped from the interaction, music standing in for the resonance you cannot hear a crystal make.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The crystal is a nanoparticle, not a bulk material: 182 atoms, free surfaces on every side, in a chamber far bigger than itself. Melting is surface-led and spread over ~25 K, boiling is evaporation into the box, and there are no periodic boundaries — every number in Receipt 5 is a finite-cluster number. Bulk 2D Lennard-Jones melts higher and sharper (and theorists still argue about its two-step KTHNY character); the lab shows the cluster version, which is the honest version of what fits on a screen.

The kelvin are a chosen scale, and they fight the picoseconds: the thermometer maps T* = 1 to 300 K, but the clock uses argon's time unit (2.15 ps), and for real argon ε/kB ≈ 120 K — on argon's own scale the "165 K" melt preset would read 66 K and the crystal's T* ≈ 0.42 melting range would sit near 50 K. The lab's K are its own unit choice, internally consistent, externally arbitrary. Treat them as the lab's currency, not argon's.

A fresh crystal runs at half its labelled temperature: velocities are initialised with all the thermal energy in kinetic form, and equipartition reclaims half for the bonds within a few time units — 6 K builds settle at 3.1 K, 10 K at 5.3 K. Only the thermostat presets quietly correct this; the cold NVE scenarios are colder than they say.

The thermostat is Berendsen only: velocity rescaling toward the target, with its fluctuation suppression measured in Receipt 3. The code's header comment says "Berendsen/Langevin," but no Langevin random force exists in the loop — the only noise is a small seed kick used to warm up from exactly 0 K. Temperature averages are right; temperature fluctuations are damped by design.

The defect counter counts disorder, not defects: a lone vacancy reads zero (Receipt 6 — ψ₆ is structurally blind to a missing neighbour at perfect angles), gentle grain boundaries undercount for the same reason, and what the counter does catch is impurities (one-for-one) and cores disordered enough to push ψ below 0.78. "Defects / N" is a turbulence gauge wearing a census label.

"Phonon RMS" measures history, not phonons: displacement from build positions, rigid rotation included — a factor of 117 overstatement in a perfect cold crystal, and it never forgives a recrystallized lattice its past life (Receipt 8). The displayed order parameter is likewise a labelled blend (0.65 local + 0.35 global, frame-smoothed, ψ₄ on the square benches), and the phase badge — solid / melting / liquid / gas — is a threshold classifier over that blend, the mean coordination, and the temperature: a verdict computed from the gauges, not a measured transition.

The square crystal is a held structure: without the 6.2ε three-body angle term it collapses to triangular in under 96 time units (Receipt 7). The term is disclosed on the page — but its size is not: at six times the pair well depth it is not a correction to the Lennard-Jones physics, it is a second, stronger physics that the square benches run on. Its cos²θ sin²θ shape also favours 0° and 180° as well as 90°; "90° term" names the intent, not the whole function.

Shear is a couple on a free cluster: the stress is applied as opposite forces on whichever atoms currently sit in the top and bottom bands (with an extra vertical velocity damping on those atoms hidden inside the force routine), so the crystal tumbles while it yields. Dislocation nucleation and glide are real; the fixed-grip simple shear the slider's label evokes is not the geometry. The pluck tool is likewise a spring (k = 26, capped pull) with its own damping, not a finger.

Safeguards wear no labels: the soft core below 0.76σ (220× softer than the true potential at 0.5σ), the silent velocity cap at 20 σ/τ, and the lossy hard wall clamp behind the harmonic walls are all invisible from the page. Each one exists so an enthusiastic throw cannot tear the integrator apart; each one is a place where the physics quietly stops being Lennard-Jones.

Small print: the pair well the atoms actually feel is 7% shallower than the ε on the label (−0.930ε at 1.124σ) because of the force shift; the lattice is built at 1.114σ, slightly inside even that minimum, and the strain legend's r₀ = 1.12σ is the textbook value, not the coded one. The diatomic "heavy impurity" of the Dope tool is mass 5.0 — heavier than the diatomic bench's own heavy atoms at the default 3.5× ratio. And the optical pulse's momentum balance is approximate (0.2% residue), so an optically excited crystal very slowly drifts. None of these is hidden in the code; all of them are named here because the page doesn't.
