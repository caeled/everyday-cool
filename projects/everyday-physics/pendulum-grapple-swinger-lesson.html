Pendulum Grapple Swinger — the lesson

You are a glowing orb with a grappling hook, a long drop below you, and a finish line somewhere off to the right. Latch onto an anchor, swing, and let go at the right instant to fling yourself onward. Underneath the fun — and Steven's verdict on this one was "just plain fun" — sits the oldest machine in physics: the pendulum, trading height for speed and speed for height, plus one beautiful cheat: reel the rope in mid-swing and angular momentum whips you faster. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, the math underneath, worked examples computed from the game's own numbers, and how it is built.

The physics

The pendulum trade: height for speed, speed for height

A swing is a barter that never stops. At the top of your arc you are high and slow — nearly stopped at the turnaround. Gravity pulls you down the arc, and every pixel of height you give up comes back as speed. At the bottom you are as low as the rope allows and as fast as you will ever be on that swing. Then the trade reverses: speed is spent climbing the far side, until you are high and slow again. Nothing is created and nothing is lost in the exchange — the sidebar's energy bars are literally watching the barter happen, the blue kinetic bar filling as the green potential bar drains, and back again. That is conservation of energy with a user interface.

The bottom of the arc is the launch pad

Because speed peaks at the bottom, the bottom is where letting go buys you the most flight. But speed is only half of a good release — direction is the other half. While you swing, your velocity always points along the tangent to the rope's circle: at the bottom, that tangent is dead horizontal, so releasing there sends you flat and fast. Release later, partway up the far side, and the tangent points up-and-forward — you trade some of that peak speed for altitude. The dotted arc the game draws ahead of you is your velocity's tangent line bent by gravity into a parabola: it is showing you, honestly, where this release, right now, would actually go. Choosing the instant is the whole skill of the game, and it is a physics decision every time.

Tension: the rope pays two bills at the bottom

The sidebar's Tension readout is the force the rope is exerting, and it is not steady — it peaks hard at the bottom of the arc. Up there at the turnaround, the rope mostly just holds your weight. At the bottom it pays two bills at once, and both pull the same way:

  • Your weight — gravity pulling you down, which the rope must cancel just to keep you from falling.
  • The centripetal bill — your path is bending, and bending a path takes force aimed at the centre of the circle. Moving in a circle at speed v on a rope of length r costs v²/r of inward acceleration, every instant, and the rope pays it.

At the top of the arc the two bills partly cancel — gravity is pulling you toward the anchor, helping bend the path, so the rope can slack off. At the bottom they add. That is why playground chains feel like they might snap at the bottom of a big swing and go almost slack at the top, and it is why the game's tension number — and the pink tension arrow — flare at the bottom of every arc.

Reeling in: the figure-skater effect

Now the cheat, which is not a cheat at all. Press W mid-swing and the rope shortens — and you speed up, noticeably, without anything pushing you. This is the same physics as a figure skater pulling in her arms to spin faster, and it runs on angular momentum: the quantity L = m·v·r — mass times speed times distance from the centre. Nothing in a swing twists you from outside, so L stays put. Shrink r, and v has nowhere to go but up: halve the rope and the tangential speed doubles. The energy to go faster comes from the reeling itself — you are hauling yourself inward against a tension that peaks at the bottom, doing real work, and the work lands in your speed. That is also why the game's own pro tip says to reel through the bottom: the multiplier applies to whatever speed you have, and the bottom is where you have the most. Reeling at the turnaround doubles a small number; reeling at the bottom doubles a big one.

Pumping: spending your own energy

The A/D keys lean you sideways — a gentle sideways push (stronger while attached than in free flight). Think of it as pumping a playground swing: timed pushes feed energy into the arc a little at a time. It is the one place the game lets you add energy from outside the height-for-speed barter, and it is honestly your own muscles doing it — the game just doesn't model the muscles.

The math the game runs

The game's world runs on pixels and seconds, with one gravity for everything: g = 680 px/s². Your orb's mass is quietly 1, which is why the formulas below look clean — and why the sidebar's "newtons" are really force-per-unit-mass (see Simplifications). Four formulas run the whole game.

E = mgh + ½mv² Total mechanical energy is potential plus kinetic — and that is exactly what the three sidebar bars are summing. The game computes kinetic as ½·speed² ÷ 1000 and potential as g × height ÷ 1000, where height is measured up from the bottom of the screen. Watch the Total bar during a clean swing: it should sit almost still while the other two seesaw. Almost still — the game has a whisper of air drag, so the Total sags slowly. The bar is honest enough to tell on it.
v = √(2gh) The speed at the bottom of a swing that started from rest having dropped a height h — it falls straight out of the energy formula: mgh = ½mv², the mass cancels, and what is left says speed at the bottom depends only on how far you fell, not on the rope's length or your mass. Fall 250 px in this game and you arrive at √(2 × 680 × 250) ≈ 583 px/s, whatever rope you fell on.
L = m · v · r Angular momentum of the swing. With nothing twisting you from outside, L is conserved, so v scales inversely with r: reel from 300 px to 150 px and your tangential speed doubles. The code applies this as a per-step multiplier — new speed = old speed × (old length ÷ new length) — every simulation substep while you reel.
T = mg + mv²/r Rope tension at the bottom of the arc: weight plus the centripetal bill, added. The game's readout computes exactly this shape — v²/r along the rope plus the component of gravity along the rope — which is why it peaks at the bottom, fades at the top of the arc, and reads zero whenever the rope is slack.

Worked examples — from the game's own numbers

Both examples use the game's real constants: g = 680 px/s², rope lengths inside the game's 60–430 px reel range, and anchors taken from the actual first course, First Arc.

Example 1: one clean swing, audited by the energy bars

First Arc has an anchor at (650, 110). Imagine you latch on with a 250 px rope and start level with the anchor, momentarily at rest — the pure pendulum case. You fall 250 px to the bottom of the arc. The books:

MomentPotential (game units)Kinetic (game units)Total
At the start, level with the anchor680 × 470 ÷ 1000 ≈ 3200≈ 320
At the bottom, 250 px lower680 × 220 ÷ 1000 ≈ 150½ × 583² ÷ 1000 ≈ 170≈ 320

The 170 units of potential you spent are the 170 units of kinetic you own — the bars trade, the total holds (minus drag's small cut on the way down). And the tension at that bottom instant: T = g + v²/r = 680 + 340,000/250 = 680 + 1360 = 2040 — exactly three times the 680 the rope would feel if you simply hung still, because a swing started level with its anchor always arrives with v²/r = 2g. Watch the sidebar on a big swing: the tension readout flaring to roughly triple the hanging value at the bottom is this arithmetic, live.

Example 2: the reel-in boost, priced

You are swinging through the bottom at a tangential speed of 400 px/s on a 300 px rope, and you hold W to reel in to 150 px. Angular momentum per unit mass is r × v = 300 × 400 = 120,000, and it does not change while you reel:

RopeTangential speedKinetic (game units)
300 px400 px/s½ × 400² ÷ 1000 = 80
150 px400 × (300 ÷ 150) = 800 px/s½ × 800² ÷ 1000 = 320

Speed doubled — and kinetic energy quadrupled, from 80 to 320. That extra energy is not a gift from the conservation law; it is the work your reel did hauling you in against the rope's tension. Conservation of angular momentum sets the speed; the work you do pays the energy bill. Both laws get satisfied, and you get flung. Course 3, Reel-In Boost, is built around exactly this exchange.

How it's built — the coding choices

The game is one HTML file, and its physics is neither an analytic pendulum formula nor a spring simulation. It is a constraint solver — a choice worth understanding, because it is how most real game physics engines treat ropes, chains, and joints.

A rope is a rule, enforced by projection

Each simulation step first lets you fly freely — gravity is added to your velocity — and then enforces one rule: you may not be farther from the anchor than the rope is long. If you are, the code moves you back onto the rope's circle (that move is the "projection") and then fixes your velocity: it splits the velocity into a radial part (along the rope) and a tangential part (along the swing), and deletes the outward radial part. What survives is pure tangential motion — a swing. There is no pendulum equation anywhere in the file; the pendulum emerges from gravity plus the rule, the same way it emerges in the world. And the rule only pulls: if you are closer than the rope's length, the rope is slack, the code does nothing at all, and you are simply a falling object until the rope comes taut. A rope that could push would be a stick, and the game would feel entirely different.

Small steps, many of them

The game integrates with the velocity-first method (update velocity, then position — the semi-implicit Euler that game physics favours because it doesn't pump energy into oscillations the way naive Euler does), and it runs eight substeps per frame, with the frame time itself clamped so a hiccup can't teleport you. Small steps are what keep the constraint honest: project a wildly overstretched rope in one big step and the correction itself adds energy and jitter. Eight quiet corrections per frame keep the circle a circle — cheap insurance, paid in arithmetic nobody sees.

Reeling is a multiplier, applied gently

While you reel, the rope's target length changes at 210 px/s, and at every substep where the rope has shortened, the tangential speed is multiplied by old length ÷ new length — the conservation law, applied in slices. Two guards keep it physical: the multiplier is capped at ×1.025 per substep, so no single step can whip you absurdly, and it only applies when you are actually swinging (tangential speed above a small threshold), so reeling while hanging still doesn't manufacture motion from nothing. The tension readout is computed in the same block of code — v²/r plus gravity's component along the rope — so the number on the sidebar is the simulation's own estimate, not a decoration.

The readouts are computed, not choreographed

The energy bars are recalculated from your live speed and height every frame, in a separate display function that reads the physics but never writes it — which is why they can be trusted as instruments, including when they misbehave: if the Total bar sags, that is the drag term, visible. The dotted release arc is the same honesty in the other direction: pure ballistic kinematics from your current velocity, 22 dots of x = x₀ + v·t, y = y₀ + v·t + ½g·t², redrawn every frame. It is not a hint system guessing where you should go; it is the actual parabola you are already committed to if you let go now. Even choosing your anchor is a small piece of judgement in code: candidates must be within 460 px and not closer than 35 px, scored with a bias toward your pointer and against anchors behind you — the game picks the anchor you probably meant, and shows it highlighted before you commit.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: you are a point mass on a massless, perfectly inextensible rope — the rope has no weight, no stretch, and no sag; when taut it is a rigid rule, when slack it is nothing. Your mass is 1 throughout, so the tension readout is really acceleration (force per unit mass) wearing a "newtons" label, and the energies are per-unit-mass figures scaled by 1000 for display. Gravity is a constant 680 px/s² everywhere, at every height. There is a small air drag — velocity is multiplied by 0.9985 per 1/60-second frame's worth of time, roughly a 9% speed loss per second of flight — so total energy is almost conserved, not exactly, and the Total bar sags honestly. The reel-in multiplier is capped per substep and switches off at very low swing speeds, so angular momentum conservation is very slightly approximate by design. Pumping (A/D) adds energy with no modelled muscles behind it. The world is a flat 2D plane, anchors are fixed points that never move or break, and the rope never wraps around a pillar — it passes through the world, only ever measuring distance to its one anchor.
