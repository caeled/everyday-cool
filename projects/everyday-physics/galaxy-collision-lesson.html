Everyday Physics · The back door to Tidal Arms

Tidal Arms — the lesson

Tidal Arms is the collection's galaxy-collision laboratory: two spiral galaxies on a collision course, thousands of stars, and a dark-matter halo you can dial up or delete. It belongs to an honourable lineage — in 1972 Alar and Juri Toomre showed that the great tidal tails of galaxies like the Antennae could be reproduced by nothing more than gravity acting on stars that never touch each other, and this game is that experiment rebuilt for a browser. This page is the other half of the game: the physics it runs, how to play it, and the audit that ran its code headlessly — which found tails that genuinely emerge from the integration, a disk built exactly on the rotation curve it advertises, a spin experiment whose own counters contradict its button label, and a merger ending that, as shipped, can never happen.

Tidal Arms was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

How to play

There is no score and no win screen. You set up an encounter between galaxies, press play, and read what gravity does — the telemetry strip is the scoreboard: elapsed time, core separation, relative speed, the percentage of stars counted as escaped tidal material, the number of stars currently flagged as starbursts, and how many cores are still active.

  • Choose a scenario. Six presets: MW + Andromeda (a prograde, inclined grand-spiral collision), The Antennae (an equal-mass face-on encounter modelled on NGC 4038/4039), Cartwheel Ring (a compact intruder plunging through a disk along the line of sight), Retrograde Flyby (counter-rotating disks), Triple Merger (three cores), and Cannibalism (a giant spiral and a dwarf satellite).
  • Tune the encounter. Star count (1,500–6,000), mass ratio M₂/M₁ (0.10–2.00), spin alignment (prograde / mixed / retrograde), and impact parameter (0–55 kpc). Relaunch applies them. The defaults rebuild the scenario; the sliders rebuild the physics.
  • Tune the physics. Dark Matter Halo Strength (0–2×), Dynamical Friction (0–2.5×), simulation speed, and the Starburst Ignition Rate, plus toggles for SMBH Core Coalescence and Tidal Starburst Physics. The Physics tab carries a live rotation-curve plot — total curve in cyan, baryons-only in dashed amber — that redraws as you move the halo slider.
  • Throw a dwarf at it. Click and drag anywhere in space to slingshot a Dwarf Satellite Galaxy (0.16× the primary's mass, up to 340 stars) onto any trajectory, or press Drop Dwarf for a random grazing one. A dotted preview sketches its path before you let go.
  • Look properly. Three star-colour mappings (spectral type, speed, home galaxy), overlays for halo contours, core orbit trails, nebula glow and tidal-stream highlighting, a camera that can ride the centre of mass or either core, and a Cinema mode that hides the HUD.

Stars are never destroyed. Cores can, in principle, coalesce into a single remnant — the audit has something to say about that "in principle" (Receipt 4).

The physics

A collision with no collisions

Two galaxies can pass through each other and almost no two stars ever meet. A galaxy is mostly empty space: the Sun's nearest stellar neighbour is some 30 million solar diameters away, and when the Milky Way eventually merges with Andromeda, direct star-on-star collisions are expected to be essentially zero. What collides is not the stars but the gravity — each galaxy's stars fall in the combined, changing gravitational field of both. That is why a faithful simulation does not need star-star forces at all: it needs each star integrated honestly in the field of the two galactic potentials. It is also why this game's architecture — thousands of test-particle stars, a handful of massive cores — is not a shortcut around the physics. To first order, it is the physics, and it is exactly the approximation Toomre & Toomre used in 1972 to explain the Antennae.

Tides again — this time the difference is a galaxy wide

Event Horizon, elsewhere in this room, shreds single stars with tides: the near side pulled harder than the far side. A galaxy encounter is the same effect scaled up. As two disks pass, the near side of each disk feels the intruder's pull more strongly than the far side, and the difference stretches material out along the line of approach into a bridge toward the companion and a tail flung away from it. The famous asymmetry — one bridge, one long sweeping tail per galaxy — comes from rotation. On the side where the disk's spin moves with the passing companion, stars linger near it, pulled steadily for a long time: the resonance is strong, the tail is long and thin. On the counter-rotating side the encounter is a quick swipe and the response is feeble. That is why prograde encounters (spin aligned with the orbit) draw the textbook tails and retrograde ones mostly shrug — the comparison the game's spin control exists to make, and the one the audit measures in Receipt 3.

The flat rotation curve — the halo's fingerprint

Count a spiral galaxy's visible mass and Newton predicts the outer disk should crawl: circular speed falling as 1/√r beyond the bright disk, the Keplerian falloff. Measure real galaxies and the speed stays flat — roughly constant out to the edge of observation and beyond. The standard reading is that each disk sits inside a much larger, invisible halo of dark matter whose mass keeps growing with radius, holding the curve up. The game's Physics tab makes the same argument with its two plotted curves, and its halo slider is the argument as a control: at 0× the disk is baryons alone and the curve falls; at 1× it stays up. The audit reads the game's own numbers off its own formula (Receipt 1) — and checks what the halo is actually made of, mathematically, in the honest box.

Dynamical friction — why real mergers finish

Left to pure two-body gravity, two galaxy cores on a bound orbit would swing past each other forever. Real mergers end because of dynamical friction: a massive body ploughing through a sea of stars and dark matter gravitationally focuses a wake behind itself, and the wake's pull drags on the body, bleeding its orbital energy into the sea. Chandrasekhar derived the drag in 1943; it is stronger for heavier bodies, denser seas, and slower passages, and it is the reason the Antennae will eventually coalesce and the Milky Way–Andromeda pair will not simply oscillate. Notice what the mechanism needs: a sea that can respond. Keep that in mind for Receipt 4, because in this game the sea — the stars — is massless, and a massless sea makes no wake.

Starbursts — the part that needs gas

Real colliding galaxies light up. The Antennae are forming stars at a furious rate in the overlap region and along the tails, because the encounter compresses gas — and gas, unlike stars, can collide, shock, cool, and collapse into new stars. The blue knots and magenta H-II regions in Hubble's portrait of the Antennae are stellar nurseries a few million years old. A test-particle simulation has no gas and forms no stars, so any "starburst" it shows is necessarily a flag planted on existing stars, not a birth. The honest question for the audit is not whether the game's bursts are real star formation (they cannot be) but whether the flag is planted by a rule with physics in it, and what its counter actually counts (Receipt 6).

The math the game runs

The game works in units tuned so the arithmetic stays near 1: 1 length unit = 0.25 kpc, 1 time unit = 10 Myr, so 1 velocity unit = 24.45 km/s, with G = 1 and a primary galaxy mass M₀ = 2200. Those are not arbitrary game numbers: run M₀ through the real gravitational constant and it is ≈ 7.6 × 1010 solar masses of baryons — a Milky Way-scale galaxy, which is why the speeds that fall out of it (Receipt 1) are real galaxy speeds. Each galaxy is a softened point mass wearing a halo. A star at displacement r from a core feels:

a = Σcores [ GM/(r² + a²)3/2 + h·Vh²/(r² + rh²) · Rcut²/(r² + Rcut²) ] · r The first term is a Plummer sphere: a point mass with its singularity sanded down over a softening length a = 11.5 units (2.9 kpc) for M₀, scaling as M0.34. The second is the halo: Vh² = 54·(M/M₀)0.72, core radius rh = 22 units (5.5 kpc) scaling as M0.30, truncated at Rcut = 220 units (55 kpc) by the last factor. h is the Halo Strength slider. At large r inside the truncation the halo term gives a ≈ Vh²/r — the 1/r pull of an isothermal halo, which is exactly the pull that keeps a rotation curve flat.

Disks are born honest: every star's speed is set from the exact circular speed of that same force law around its own core,

vc²(r) = GM·r²/(r² + a²)3/2 + h·Vh²·r²/(r² + rh²) · Rcut²/(r² + Rcut²) The same expression the Physics tab plots as the rotation curve — one function serves as the disk-builder, the plot, and the vflat telemetry (which reads it at r = 44 units, 11 kpc). A small random dispersion is added: ±1.75% tangential in the disk, warmer in the bulge. The companion's pull is ignored at birth, which is standard practice and is named in the honest box.

The cores are integrated as bodies in their own right — pairwise, momentum-conserving, with a Plummer pair term using the combined softening √(r² + a₁² + a₂²), a second, different halo attraction symmetrised between the pair, and the friction drag:

drag ∝ f · 58 · (m₁m₂/M₀) / (r² + 28²)1.35 / (vrel² + 34)1.15 , applied along −vrel Labelled "Dynamical Friction (Chandrasekhar)" on the Physics tab. It has the right instincts — stronger when the cores are close and slow — but it is an inspired-by term, not Chandrasekhar's formula (which carries a Coulomb logarithm and the sea's velocity distribution), and the audit prices what it actually does in Receipt 4.

Everything is stepped with a genuine velocity-Verlet integrator (half-kick, drift, recompute forces, half-kick) — the same symplectic family as Star Forge's engine room. The step is wall-clock driven: each frame advances frame-time × 4.4 × speed, split into 2 substeps (3 when the frame is long), so at 60 fps and 1× speed the step is dt = 0.0367 time units ≈ 0.37 Myr, and the simulation runs at ≈ 44 Myr per real second. Cores coalesce — if they ever do — under a coded rule: separation below 0.52(a₁ + a₂) and relative speed below 0.72 × the pair's escape speed at that separation and t > 0.04 Gyr, whereupon the heavier core absorbs the other with mass, position, and momentum conserved exactly.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own script out of the shipped file — same constants, same force functions, same Verlet step, same coalescence rule — and ran it headlessly with a seeded random generator, at the game's own 60 fps step. Every number below is measured, not quoted from the page. Star counts were reduced only for the core-dynamics runs, which cannot change them: the cores do not feel the stars at all (that fact is itself Receipt 4's foundation).

Receipt 1: the rotation curve is real, and the disk is built on it

The file's own header comment promises "v_circ(10 kpc) ≈ 225 km/s" for M₀; the code delivers 231 — within 2.5%. With the halo at 1× the curve declines gently (239 → 196 km/s from 5 to 25 kpc, about −18%) rather than falling off a cliff; without it, the falloff is unmistakable (207 → 114). And the disk honours the curve it was born from: in a fresh isolated galaxy, the mean tangential speed of stars in the 9–11 kpc annulus measures 230.6 km/s against the formula's 230.6 km/s at 10 kpc — agreement to the printed digit. Left alone for 2 Gyr, that disk does nothing at all: median stellar radius 31.7 → 31.6 units, the game's escaped counter at 0.00% throughout, and the orbital energy of tracer stars at 7.5 kpc drifting by at most 0.18% — the integrator and the initial conditions are both honest. Even at six times the normal step (dt = 0.22, the worst case at 3× speed on a slow machine) the drift is 0.19% per Gyr: the Plummer softening makes this the most step-forgiving gravity in the room.

Receipt 2: the tails are integrated, not drawn

Nothing in the code knows what a tidal tail looks like. There is no tail shape, no spline, no script: stars are placed in two disks, the cores are given velocities, and the Verlet step runs. In the Antennae preset at 2.5 Gyr, across two seeds:

Stars beyond 1.38 disk radii of every core ("Escaped Tidal")29–30%36%

Some 15% of all stars end up more than 46 kpc from either core — debris at genuinely Antennae-like distances, produced by the force law alone. The bridge is real too: a fifth of the prograde disks' stars finish the run closer to the other galaxy than to their own. Whatever else the honest box has to qualify, the central Toomre claim of the piece — give me two disks and honest gravity and I will give you tails — is true in the shipped code.

Receipt 3: the spin button's label fails its own counters

The Retrograde Flyby button promises "Counter-spin suppresses tails," and real physics backs the slogan: prograde resonance is what draws the long tails. The audit ran the Antennae encounter with only the spin flipped — same geometry, same masses, two seeds each — and read the game's own counters at 2.5 Gyr (the table above). The result is more interesting than the slogan. Retrograde leaves more material at large radius by every positional measure — 36% escaped against 29–30%, 18% beyond three disk radii against 15% — while prograde transfers more: 21% of stars captured into the companion's neighbourhood against 16–17%. What counter-spin suppresses in this simulation is not the amount of debris but its organisation: the prograde response is coherent — a bridge that delivers stars to the companion and tails that stay attached — while the retrograde response is a broader, less directed spray that the positional counter scores as more escaped. The eye may still prefer the prograde picture (coherent tails are brighter per star, and brightness is not in these counts), but as a claim about the game's own numbers, "suppresses tails" is not what the counters say — "suppresses capture" is.

Receipt 4: the merger never happens — the friction is a dial on an empty room

The game's finale is core coalescence: the "Merged Remnant" status, the merge banner, the remnant naming, the central starburst flash. The audit ran every preset's cores at their shipped settings for 8 Gyr, and the flagship for 20 Gyr at maximum friction:

The distance half of the merge rule is met constantly — MW + Andromeda plunges to 1.6 kpc, well inside its 3.1 kpc merge distance, on every passage. The speed half never is: the cores whip through pericentre at ≈ 770 km/s against a gate of 0.72 × escape speed ≈ 405 km/s. And nothing slows them down. The friction term, evaluated with its own constants at pericentre, is an acceleration of order 10−4 in a field where gravity is of order 3 — across the full slider range from 0× to 2.5×, six billion years of pericentre times shift by at most 0.02 Gyr. The deeper reason is structural: dynamical friction is a wake effect, and this game's sea is massless test particles that cannot form a wake — the cores feel only each other, through forces that are exactly antisymmetric (total core momentum is conserved to 3 × 10−10 over 6 Gyr, which the audit also measured). So the slider adjusts a hand-written drag that is four orders of magnitude too weak, in a universe where the real mechanism has been approximated away. The galaxies pass through each other, raise their tails, and swing back — forever. It is a beautiful eternal flyby machine wearing a merger's label. (A merger can be forced by hand: drop a dwarf almost at rest within ~3 kpc of a core and the gate accepts it. No preset does this.)

One honourable exception in the same receipt: the Cartwheel's guard works exactly as its code comment claims. The bullet core passes 1.7 kpc from the disk's centre — inside the 2.7 kpc merge distance — and the speed gate is precisely what stops the bullseye fly-through from "merging." The gate is a good gate. It just also stops everything else.

Receipt 5: the halo is the real binding agent between the cores

Turn the halo off and the flagship encounter changes species: at halo 0× (friction 1×) the MW + Andromeda pair makes exactly one pericentre passage in 6 Gyr, at 2.2 kpc, and does not return within the run — against a passage every 1.12 Gyr with the halo at 1×. The cores' mutual attraction at their 49 kpc starting separation is dominated by the halo term, not the baryonic one, which is physically the right way round — real pairs are bound by their halos long before their disks meet — even though the core-core halo force is a separate symmetrised formula rather than the potential the stars feel (honest box). The same slider, moved mid-run, is a different beast: build a settled disk at halo 1×, then set the slider to 0 without touching anything else, and within 0.3 Gyr the escaped counter reads 51.6% — the potential was edited out from under stars whose speeds were tuned for the deeper well. Relaunching with the slider at 0 rebuilds a consistent, slower disk that stays home (Receipt 1's galaxy is exactly that, at 1×). The slider is a potential editor, not a physical process, and the game is honest about it only if you relaunch.

Receipt 6: the starburst counter saturates — and the bursts push nothing

The ignition rule has real physics in its ancestry: a star's glow is rolled for when the intruder's pull on it exceeds about a quarter of its host's pull (otherPull / (hostPull + 0.12) > 0.24), only for young spectral types (O, B, A, G — type ≤ 3), with a probability per step proportional to the excess. In both Antennae runs the counter peaked at ≈ 3,160 of 4,500 stars — 70% of everything on screen — flagged as bursting at once, which is to say nearly every eligible star in both galaxies simultaneously. As a picture of an ignition front it is generous; as a count of starburst knots it has saturated. And the glow is all it is: the audit verified by reading every use of the burst variable that it feeds the renderer and the telemetry and no force anywhere — bursts neither push stars nor compress anything, and the variable decays exponentially with a ~40 Myr e-folding once the tide relaxes. Real Antennae star formation is gas physics; this is a well-chosen light switch labelled in its honour.

How it's built — the coding choices

Test particles and potentials — the Toomre architecture

The simulation is a restricted N-body problem, the same design as the 1972 paper it channels: up to 8,500 stars in flat struct-of-arrays storage (positions, velocities, accelerations, spectral type, home galaxy, burst glow — pre-allocated, zero garbage at 60 fps), each feeling only the analytic potentials of the live cores, and up to a handful of cores feeling each other exactly. Star-star forces are absent by design, which is why the whole thing runs in a browser tab — and why the honest box's items about wakes, bars, and violent relaxation are not bugs but the price of the architecture, payable knowingly.

One force law, three jobs

The Plummer-plus-halo acceleration is written once and used for the live stars, the rotation-curve plot, the disk builder (through its circular-speed form), and the vflat readout — the same one-source-of-truth discipline as Event Horizon's single acceleration function, and the reason Receipt 1's agreement to the printed digit is possible. The dwarf preview is the exception: a cheaper 2D Euler sketch at dt = 0.24 with the cores held fixed, which is why the dotted line is a promise of shape, not of arrival time.

Disks are painted, then flown

Each galaxy is generated as art first — a bulge (19% of stars, 38% for compact dwarfs) plus a two-armed logarithmic spiral with stars concentrated on the arm ridges and hot blue types assigned there, warm types between the arms — and then given the exact circular speeds of the force law, with dispersion. The spiral you admire at t = 0 is the generator's; the spiral at 1 Gyr is gravity's commentary on it. Nothing in the file is loaded from outside: no images, no data, no storage — the sprites are pre-rendered radial gradients and the deep-field background is 320 seeded dots and 14 smudges.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The merger ending is unreachable in every shipped preset: coalescence requires a pericentre slower than 0.72 × escape speed (≈ 405 km/s for the flagship pair), and every preset arrives at 470–770 km/s, while the friction term — the only decay channel, since massless stars make no wake — is ~10−4 of gravity at pericentre and shifts pericentre times by ≤ 0.02 Gyr over 6 Gyr across its whole slider range. The Merged Remnant status, merge banner, remnant naming and merger starburst flash are, in the shipped scenarios, dead code; the Triple Merger's "3 cores form giant elliptical" subtitle describes an ending the code cannot reach (and collisionless test particles could not violently relax into a true elliptical even if the cores merged). Only a hand-dropped, nearly stationary dwarf can trigger the rule.

The halo is not NFW: the Physics tab says "NFW/isothermal"; the coded profile is a cored pseudo-isothermal halo — Vh²·r²/(r² + rh²), flat-cored where NFW is cusped — with a hard truncation factor beyond 55 kpc that neither profile has. There are also two halo mass laws in the file: stars feel Vh² ∝ M0.72, while the core-core attraction uses a separate symmetrised formula with its own (mean mass ratio)−0.28 scaling — the cores are not bound by the same halo the stars orbit in, though the qualitative conclusion (halos bind the pair) survives, per Receipt 5.

"Baryons only" is a Plummer sphere, not a Keplerian point: at halo 0× the curve the tab calls Keplerian rises ∝ r through the inner disk (the softening length is 2.9 kpc) and only falls Kepler-style beyond ~10 kpc — 207 → 114 km/s from 5 to 25 kpc, where a true point mass normalised at 5 kpc would read 207 → 93. The falloff story the plot tells is right at disk radii and wrong in the bulge.

"Escaped Tidal %" is a position, not an energy: a star counts when it is more than 1.38 disk radii from every live core, recomputed on a timer. Bound stars on wide orbits count; tail stars that swing back inside un-count; the measure is taken against each core's birth disk radius, which never grows — and Receipt 5 shows half a settled disk "escaping" with no encounter at all when the potential is edited mid-run. Read it as "currently outside the disks," which is what it computes.

Starbursts are a glow with a good pedigree, not star formation: no gas, no shocks, no new stars — a per-step probability keyed to the intruder-to-host pull ratio, restricted to spectral types O–G, decaying over ~40 Myr, feeding no force, and saturating at ~70% of all stars at peak in the Antennae runs. The telemetry's "Starbursts" is a count of flagged stars, not of clusters formed.

Cores cannot be disrupted: a core is an indestructible analytic potential. In Cannibalism the dwarf's stars strip away on passage, but its core never gets closer than 10.8 kpc in 8 Gyr and cannot be torn apart at any distance — real satellites dissolve; these can only be orphaned or (per the first item) almost never absorbed.

No self-gravity anywhere: stars do not attract each other, so there are no bars, no spiral density waves, no violent relaxation. The grand-design arms are the generator's logarithmic ridges, wound by differential rotation as the run proceeds; their persistence is the flat curve's slow shear, not a wave phenomenon.

The first passage is partly choreographed: core starting velocities are hand-set numbers (approach speed 7.4 velocity units for the flagship), not derived orbital elements, and each disk is built in equilibrium with its own core alone — at first approach the companion already supplies about a quarter of the rim pull the disk was not balanced for. This is the Toomres' own 1972 practice, and it is why the encounter looks right immediately; it is still a choice, not an initial condition nature supplied.

The view is flat: positions are fully three-dimensional, but rendering projects onto the x–y plane; z survives only as a sprite-size cue (×0.75–1.28). The Cartwheel bullet dives almost exactly down the z axis, so on screen it hangs over the disk's centre while the ring expands — the true face-on view of that collision, and the only view there is.

Small print: the toggle labelled "SMBH Core Coalescence" merges whole-galaxy potentials, not black holes — no SMBH binary physics is modelled. The vflat telemetry always reads the primary M₀ curve at 11 kpc, whatever galaxies are actually on screen. Simulation time is wall-clock locked (frame time × 4.4), so on a slow machine the step coarsens rather than the clock slowing — accuracy holds anyway (Receipt 1), but two machines are not running identical universes. A dead core's stars keep its colour in Galaxy ID mode forever. And the halo slider at exactly 0× also removes the halo term from the core-core force — the pair's binding changes with the disks', which is at least consistent, if rarely noticed.
