Clockwork — the lesson

Five mechanism benches in one lab: a compound spur train with genuine involute teeth, a planetary gearbox with clutches and brakes, a four-bar linkage that grows a Jansen Strandbeest leg, a Geneva indexing drive, and an anchor escapement keeping time with a pendulum. Four of the five are as exact as the formulas printed beside them — one of them mathematically perfect. The fifth keeps different time than its own panel claims. This page is the other half of the lab: the kinematics, the audit that drove every button, slider, and preset and measured what came back, and the receipts for both halves of that sentence.

Verdict: Spur train, planetary, four-bar and Geneva are exact — Geneva mathematically perfect, Grashof 8/8 — but the escapement keeps different time than its own panel claims (−4.3% at defaults, −27.2% at double weight), and the efficiency readouts are the η slider multiplied by arbitrary constants.

The physics

Teeth are ratios you can count

Meshing gears trade speed for torque in the ratio of their tooth counts, and flip direction at every external mesh. A compound train — two meshes sharing an axle — multiplies the two ratios. Nothing here needs to be approximated: if the code turns the output shaft by exactly (Z1Z3)/(Z2Z4) of the input, with the right sign, the bench is right, and the audit's live drives below show that it does. The tooth shape matters too. These gears are drawn as true involutes — pitch radius Rp = mZ/2, base radius Rb = Rp·cos α, addendum +1.0 m, dedendum −1.25 m — which is why the contact ratio, the average number of tooth pairs sharing the load, comes out as a real geometric quantity instead of a decoration.

A planetary set is an equation with three shafts

Sun, planets on a carrier, and a ring gear with internal teeth (ZR = ZS + 2ZP) obey the Willis equation: the three shaft speeds are locked together by one relation, so holding any one member still turns the set into a gearbox with a definite ratio — reduction, overdrive, or reverse. Brake nothing and it would be a differential; brake one member, as every mode here does, and it is a transmission. The lab's four clutch modes are the four textbook rows of that table, and each one was driven live.

Four bars, one law

A four-bar linkage's character is decided before it moves, by Grashof's law: if the shortest plus the longest link is no more than the other two together (S + L ≤ P + Q), the shortest link can rotate fully, and which link is shortest names the type — crank-rocker, double-crank, double-rocker. The coupler point riding the middle link traces the famous coupler curves: Watt's near-straight line, and, with Theo Jansen's eleven “holy numbers,” the flat-bottomed stride of the Strandbeest leg.

Indexing and timekeeping

The Geneva drive turns continuous rotation into steps: a pin on the driver engages a slot, advances the star wheel by exactly 360/N, then leaves it locked in dwell — and, by the geometry of the entry, arrives with zero impact velocity. The escapement is the reverse bargain: a pendulum's swing meters out a gear train, half a tooth pitch per beat. Its period, on the panel, is the small-angle formula T = 2π√(L/g). That formula assumes the swing is small. Keep that assumption in mind — it is where this lab's one false number lives.

The math the game runs

The spur train

GR = (Z2/Z1)(Z4/Z3)  ;  torque multiplier = GR × η²  ;  mesh frequency = |RPMin|·Z1/60 η is the per-mesh efficiency, applied once per mesh, and the torque figure is power-consistent with the displayed efficiency. The tooth-phase equations differentiate to exactly dθ2/dθ1 = −Z1/Z2 and dθ4/dθ1 = +(Z1Z3)/(Z2Z4), and the centre distances are the exact pitch-radius sums.

Contact ratio, from the drawn teeth

εα from the true path-of-contact formula, on the drawn involute geometry At the defaults the page reads 1.55; an independent computation from the same geometry gives mesh averages of 1.547. The pressure-angle buttons move it the right way by the right amount: α = 14.5° → live 1.78 (independent 1.779); α = 25° → live 1.40.

The planetary set

(ωS − ωC)/(ωR − ωC) = −ZR/ZS,   ZR = ZS + 2ZP Assembly rule enforced live: (ZS + ZR)/Np must be integral for the planets to fit. Switching to 4 planets at ZS = 21, ZP = 12 auto-bumped ZP to 13 (ZR 45 → 47) and the note updated to “(21 + 47)/4 = 17 (exact)”.

Grashof and Geneva

Grashof: S + L ≤ P + Q  ;  Geneva: index = 360/N, motion fraction = ½ − 1/N, peak velocity ratio = sin β/(1 − sin β) Both are checked as exact statements below — the classifier against an eight-case test battery, the Geneva against three full drive revolutions per slot count.

Receipt 5 — the FALSE one: the escapement's clock disagrees with itself

The wheel's bookkeeping is right: it advances exactly half a tooth pitch per beat (230 beats → 115.0 teeth in simulation), and the displayed wheel RPM = 30·beatHz/Z checks out at Z = 15 (2.00 RPM) and Z = 24 (2.49 RPM), with vph = beatHz × 3600. What is wrong is the beatHz those formulas consume. Beat Freq, Period, vph, and wheel RPM are all computed from the small-angle formula T = 2π√(L/g) — but the page's own simulated pendulum, integrated with the shipped code verbatim (the result is identical at dt = 1/60 and dt = 1/120, so it is not a timestep artifact), beats measurably slower:

The cause is in the impulse code. The kick that sustains the swing is sized by targetAmp − |pendAngle| sampled at the release instant, where |pendAngle| is always ≈ 0.075 rad — so the energy added per beat is essentially constant no matter how wide the pendulum is already swinging, and the amplitude runs away from the 0.27 rad (15.5°) design target to ≈ 49° at the defaults and ≈ 132° at 2.0× weight, the bob swung past horizontal. A real pendulum at 49° amplitude runs slow by circular error — its period lengthens by ≈ +4.8% — which matches the measured −4.3% rate. The formulas on the panel describe a clock the simulation is not keeping: it is the timebase, not the counting, that is wrong.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The escapement, restated plainly: the displayed Beat Freq, Period, vph, and wheel RPM come from the small-angle formula while the simulated pendulum — amplitude run away to ≈49° at defaults, ≈132° at double weight — beats −4.3% to −27.2% slower. The geometry and the per-beat bookkeeping are right; the regulator is not.

The efficiency readouts on four benches are decorative. Four-bar 95.1%, Jansen 94.1%, Geneva 92.1%, escapement 90.2%, planetary 96.5%/95.5% are just the per-mesh η slider multiplied by an arbitrary constant (×0.97, ×96, ×0.94, ×0.92, ×0.985) — no loss model stands behind them, yet they sit in the same telemetry grid as the measured quantities. (The spur train's 96.0% is at least power-consistent with its torque.)

The status pill mangles the non-Grashof label. The pill is produced by type.replace('Grashof ', ''), which turns “Non-Grashof Double-Rocker” into “Non-Double-Rocker” — on the double-rocker and Watt presets the pill reads as the opposite of the truth. The Grashof box next to it states the type correctly.

Small print: the seconds-pendulum button sets L = 0.994 m internally — the correct seconds-pendulum length, giving T = 2.0003 s and 3599 vph — while the slider and label read “0.99 m”. The exhibit's title says “Planetary Differentials”, but with one member always braked the planetary set operates as a gearbox in all four modes, never as an open differential. And the Watt coupler curve is a sextic with a genuinely straight middle section (measured in Receipt 3), not a lemniscate, despite the page's “Watt … Lemniscate” wording.
