Everyday Physics · The back door to Lorentz

Lorentz — the lesson

Lorentz puts you in a cockpit and lets you throttle up from a standstill to 99.8% of light speed, through a corridor of distance gates and synchronized beacon clocks, past calibration cubes and ringed planets, under a sky of 3,000 stars — with every special-relativity effect live and separately switchable: aberration, Doppler shift, beaming, length contraction, and Terrell–Penrose rotation. It is the exhibit in this collection most exposed to fakery, because relativistic pictures are easy to paint and hard to check: tint the front blue, squash the world, call it Einstein. So this audit did the one thing that settles it — it took the game's own code apart and checked whether every effect is computed from the same β and the same γ, and whether the effects agree with each other, not just with their own labels. The verdict, up front: this one is real. One β, one γ, one rapidity, feeding the starfield, the gates, the cubes, the clocks, the telemetry, and the Twin Paradox autopilot — and the cross-checks close to machine precision. What is dressed up is dressed at the edges — the brightness curve, the beacon clocks' semantics, a title that undersells the throttle — and every piece of it is named in the honest box.

Lorentz was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

How to play

There is no score. Lorentz is a flight laboratory: the experiment is what the universe looks like, and how its clocks behave, at each speed you dial in. The HUD keeps the books in front of you — velocity β, Lorentz factor γ, forward and rear Doppler factors, contracted length, your proper time τ against Earth time t, and the growing gap between them.

  • Pick a preset. Six scenarios: Sub-Relativistic (0.10c), Half Light Speed (0.50c), Relativistic Tunnel (0.90c), Ultra-Relativistic (0.99c), Twin Paradox (a 4 ly round-trip autopilot), and the Light-Cone Demo (β = 0.866c, split-view Minkowski diagram).
  • Fly. The velocity slider runs the whole range; W/S burn and brake with the proper-acceleration throttle; drag to look around the full 360° cockpit, or use the Fwd 0° / Side 90° / Rear 180° look buttons — the side view at a passing cube is where Terrell rotation shows itself, and the rear view is a wall of crimson redshift.
  • Isolate the effects. Five toggles — Aberration, Doppler, Beaming, Contraction, Terrell–Penrose — each switch one piece of physics off while the rest keep running, plus All SR ON and Classical OFF buttons. Turning effects off one at a time is the fastest way to learn which piece of the picture each formula owns. (Read the honest box before trusting Classical mode's clocks.)
  • Run the Twin Paradox. Choose a target star (2, 4, or 8 ly) and a cruise speed (0.80c, 0.92c, 0.98c) and launch the autopilot: burn, cruise, brake, turn around, come home. Watch the Minkowski inset — the dashed line of simultaneity sweeping across Earth's worldline at the turnaround is the whole resolution of the paradox, drawn live.
  • Split the view. M or the Split button opens the external lab: Earth-frame and ship-frame length strips side by side, and the full Minkowski diagram — light cone at an honest 45°, your worldline, and the ship's "now" line with its slope of β.

The corridor is a lattice of gates one light-second apart (a quarter light-year apart in Twin mode), each topped with a beacon clock. The clocks are synchronized in the Earth frame — what their labels actually show you is subtler, and the audit section takes it apart.

The physics

One number runs everything: γ

Special relativity starts with a single quantity built from your speed. Write the speed as a fraction of light speed, β = v/c. Then:

γ = 1 / √(1 − β²) At β = 0.5, γ = 1.155. At 0.9, γ = 2.294. At 0.99, γ = 7.089. At the game's throttle limit of 0.998, γ = 15.82. Every effect in this room — contraction, clock rates, Doppler, aberration — is this one number, or its companion the rapidity η = atanh β, wearing different clothes. That is not a simplification; it is the structure of the theory, and it is exactly what makes the audit possible: if the game computed each effect from its own private formula, the effects would drift apart at high β. They don't, because there is only one γ in the file.

Aberration: the sky crowds forward

Run through rain and it slants into your face; drive at half light speed and the same thing happens to starlight, exactly. A star at angle θ from your direction of motion (measured in the rest frame) appears at angle θ′:

cos θ′ = (cos θ + β) / (1 + β cos θ) A star exactly abeam of you at rest (θ = 90°) appears at cos θ′ = β — at 0.9c that is 25.8° off your nose, at 0.995c just 5.7°. The whole celestial sphere is squeezed toward the apex: at high β, nearly everything you can see is in front of you, including stars that are, in the rest frame, behind you. The game applies this to all 3,000 stars every frame — and, through the transform below, to every gate, cube, and planet as well.

Doppler: one factor, three faces

Light from a source at rest-frame angle θ arrives frequency-shifted by the relativistic Doppler factor:

D = γ (1 + β cos θ) Dead ahead (θ = 0) this is γ(1 + β) = √((1+β)/(1−β)) — 4.36 at 0.9c, 19.98 at 0.995c. Dead astern it is the reciprocal. At the angle where D = 1 — the zero-Doppler ring the cockpit draws as a dashed circle — the transverse Doppler effect and the longitudinal shift exactly cancel: cos θ′ = (1 − 1/γ)/β, which is 51.2° at 0.9c. Inside the ring, blueshift; outside, redshift. The same D shifts wavelengths (λ′ = λ/D), shifts blackbody temperatures (T′ = D·T — a star's colour is its temperature, so the game's stars genuinely change spectral colour, not tint), and sets the headlight beaming. One D, computed once per object per frame.

Contraction: real, and almost never what you see

A lattice at rest, measured in the ship's frame at one instant of ship time, is compressed along the direction of motion by exactly 1/γ: gate spacing, cube depth, the Earth–star distance itself. But that is the measured shape — positions at simultaneous ship-frame times — and it is not what a camera records. A camera records photons arriving together, and those photons left different parts of the object at different times. Correct for the light-travel time and the contraction is exactly compensated by the delay: a moving sphere's outline stays circular (Penrose's 1959 theorem), and a passing cube appears rotated — you see its trailing face, swung into view — rather than squashed. That is Terrell–Penrose rotation, and it is why this game needs two transforms, not one: the simultaneous one for the frame strips and telemetry, and the retarded one for the camera.

Clocks: the twin, and the sweep that resolves it

A moving clock ticks slow by 1/γ — not as an appearance, but as bookkeeping: along any worldline, proper time accumulates as dτ = dt/γ. The travelling twin ages less because their worldline is shorter through spacetime, and the asymmetry is the turnaround: when the traveller flips velocity, their line of simultaneity — the set of distant events they call "now," a line of slope β on the Minkowski diagram — sweeps rapidly across Earth's worldline, and the Earth clock they now call simultaneous jumps forward by years. Nothing jumps on Earth; what changes is which Earth events the traveller's new frame counts as present. The game's diagram draws that line live, marks where it crosses Earth's worldline, and prints the simultaneous Earth time — the audit checks the sweep is real arithmetic, and it is.

Rapidity: the throttle that adds

Velocities don't add in relativity, but rapidities do: η = atanh β. A rocket burning at constant proper acceleration gains rapidity at a constant rate, η = aτ/c, which is why the game's thrust steps η and converts back with β = tanh η — the ship can burn forever and never reach c, each equal burn buying less speed and the same rapidity. The Twin autopilot flies the same law in honest units: with c = 1 ly/yr, its acceleration is chosen in ly/yr², where nature has hidden a gift — 1 ly/yr² ≈ 0.97 g, so the autopilot's 2.42 ly/yr² is about 2.35 g, a hard but humanly imaginable burn.

The math the game runs

There is exactly one γ in the file, and one β it comes from:

gammaOf(β) = 1 / √(1 − β²),   rapidityOf(β) = ½ ln((1+β)/(1−β)) Every readout, toggle, and renderer calls these. The velocity slider is a three-segment piecewise map — 0–70% of travel covers 0–0.90c, the next 20% covers 0.90–0.99c, the last 10% covers 0.99–0.998c — so the interesting end of the range gets most of the slider. Its inverse is exact to within one slider tick everywhere (audit, Receipt 1).

The camera's world is built by one function, transformRelativisticPoint, which takes a rest-frame point (x, y, z) relative to the ship — z along the velocity — and returns where that point appears, plus its Doppler factor D = γ(1 + β·z/r). With all effects on, it returns the retarded position — where the point's light, arriving now, was emitted, expressed in ship coordinates:

(x, y, z) → (x, y, γ(z + β·r)),   r = √(x² + y² + z²) The Lorentz transform of the emission event (t = −r/c). This single line is exact Terrell–Penrose rotation, exact aberration, and length contraction, all at once — they are three descriptions of this one map. Switch Terrell off and the function falls back to applying the aberration formula to the simultaneous (contracted, z/γ) position; switch aberration off and it returns the pure simultaneous contraction; switch everything off and it is the identity. The toggles select which honest map draws the world — none of them selects a fake one.

The clocks are stepped, not scripted. Each frame, in free flight:

dτ = 2.0 · dtreal;   dtEarth = γ · dτ;   dz = β · dtEarth · (gate spacing) The 2.0 is a presentation rate — one real second is two ship proper seconds, so the corridor moves at a watchable pace. The physics is in the ratios: Earth time accumulates at exactly γ times the ship's, and the ship covers β·c per Earth second. Under thrust, rapidity steps first (η += a·dt, scaled — see the honest box) and β follows from tanh. The Twin autopilot runs the same stepper with its own clock rate (0.28 proper years per real second) and a six-leg hyperbolic profile: burn out, cruise, brake, burn home, cruise, brake — with the burn acceleration solved from the mission, a = (γcruise − 1)/(0.16 · distance), so each burn covers 16% of the leg.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own functions out of the shipped file — gammaOf, rapidityOf, the slider maps, dopplerColor, the full transform, and both physics steppers — and ran them headlessly with DOM stubs, at the game's own settings. Every number below is measured from the shipped code, not quoted from the page.

Every column of that table is a different part of the game — the telemetry's γ, the frame strips' contraction, the reticle's ring, the live formula box, the star renderer — and every column is the same textbook number, because every column is computed from the one gammaOf. The preset's on-screen γ readout, to its credit, prints the true 2.000-rounded value from the same function, not the comment's claim.

The starfield's own fast path — the same formula applied to unit vectors, with the transverse component scaled by 1/(γ(1 + β cos θ)) — is the same map again.

Receipt 3: the Terrell transform is the exact retarded position — and the sphere stays round

The full-mode transform was compared against the analytic retarded position (x, y, γ(z + βr)) over 6,000 random points at β = 0.5, 0.9, and 0.995: maximum disagreement 9.1 × 10−13 — floating-point noise; the code is the formula. Then a wireframe planet (the game's own R = 4.4 ringed giant, placed mid-pass at (40, −2.6, 25)) was rendered three ways and its outline measured in direction space:

The full mode's outline is circular as a matter of theorem, not approximation: Receipt 2 proves the transform's output directions are exactly the aberration image of the rest-frame directions, aberration is a conformal (Möbius) map of the celestial sphere, and Möbius maps send circles to circles — so the sphere's tangent circle lands as a circle. The audit's sampled measurement of that outline, taken about the aberrated direction of the planet's centre, reads 1.049 rather than 1.000 — and that residual is itself a known feature, not an error: the image circle's centre is displaced from the image of the centre point, so radii measured about the wrong centre vary by a few percent. Against the contraction-only outline's 2.08 distortion, the contrast is the lesson: the pancake is what simultaneity looks like; the circle is what a camera sees, and the game can show you both because it keeps both transforms. The planet also shrinks — 5.34° of sky down to 1.58° — because the retarded view places the approaching world farther away, γ(z + βr) rather than z. That is the other half of Terrell: approaching objects look farther, not nearer.

Receipt 4: the clocks — γ to machine precision, and a twin who really flies

Free flight first. The game's stepper was replicated exactly and run for 60 real seconds at three speeds. In every case the accumulated Earth time divided by accumulated ship time equals γ to the printed digit — 1.154701 at 0.5c, 2.294157 at 0.9c, 10.012523 at 0.995c — because the stepper integrates dt = γ dτ every frame; there is no other clock in the game to disagree with it. Then the Twin Paradox autopilot was flown end-to-end with the game's own stepTwinParadox — 4 ly out and back, cruise 0.92c, the game's chosen acceleration of 2.4243 ly/yr² — and set against the exact constant-proper-acceleration solution of the same mission:

The headline — the age gap, the number the exhibit exists to teach — agrees with the exact solution to 0.04%. The individual clocks each land a little short (Earth by 1.6%, the ship by 3.2%), and the audit can say why: the autopilot's braking legs are flown by a feedback law, reqA = max(0.6a, (γ−1)/remaining distance), recomputed every frame rather than held at the burn's constant a, and the turnaround and touchdown are closed with small snaps (at β ≤ 0.015 the ship is set to β = 0.001 and pinned to exactly 4.000 ly). Both clocks lose time in nearly the same proportion, so the difference — the physics — survives intact. Two more checks on the same flight: the recorded worldline stays timelike everywhere, and re-integrating proper time along it, ∫√(dt² − dz²), reproduces the ship's clock to 0.2% — the diagram and the clocks are the same object. And the simultaneity sweep is real: across the turnaround manoeuvre, the Earth time the ship's frame calls "now" sweeps forward continuously through roughly eight years of Earth history (the largest single recorded step is 0.83 yr), which is precisely the bookkeeping that makes the traveller's missing years add up.

Receipt 5: thrust is a relativistic rocket — in two different unit systems

Holding W at the default 2.0 g setting, the audit's replication of the thrust code reaches 0.99c in 3.90 real seconds — the code comment's "~4 seconds" is honest. The structure is the relativistic rocket's: rapidity grows linearly, β = tanh η, so the speed asymptotes and the cap at 0.998c is the tanh ceiling plus a clamp, not a wall the code paints. But read the units and the two flight modes part company. In free flight, rapidity grows at 0.17 per g per ship proper second — the "g" is a dial calibrated for feel; expressed in the game's own light-second units, the 2.0 setting is a proper acceleration of about 0.34 c per second, some ten million Earth g. In Twin mode the same word means the real thing: ly/yr², where the autopilot's 2.42 ly/yr² ≈ 2.35 g. Same label, two meanings — the honest box carries it.

How it's built — the coding choices

One transform, honestly branched

Everything the camera sees — stars excepted, which use the same aberration in a fast path — passes through transformRelativisticPoint: gates ring by ring (12 vertices each), rails segment by segment, cubes vertex by vertex with their depth edges subdivided four times so the light-time curvature bends smoothly, planets as meridian rings through the same map. The five toggles don't turn on five effects; they select among the transform's branches, each of which the audit verified separately. The cubes' faces carry distinct rest wavelengths (front 500 nm, back 660 nm, sides 580, top and bottom 540) precisely so the Terrell rotation is unmistakable: when the crimson back face swings into view, it is because its photons' transformed positions put it there. Objects are drawn far-to-near (painter's algorithm) on their transformed depths, in a corridor that repeats every 204 world units.

A seeded sky and a real spectrum

The 3,000 stars are generated from a fixed seed (19050926 — the annus mirabilis, worn as a date) so the cosmos is the same on every visit: 28% concentrated in a tilted galactic plane, the rest isotropic, each with a blackbody temperature drawn from the O-through-M spectral class weights. Colour is computed, not chosen: temperature is Doppler-shifted (T′ = D·T) and converted through a standard blackbody-to-sRGB approximation; emissive objects carry a rest wavelength (the gates at 565 nm, the planets at 590 and 505) that divides by D and converts through a piecewise spectrum approximation. At 0.995c dead ahead, D ≈ 20 — a solar 5,800 K star presents at ~116,000 K, far past the visible, and the colour functions have hand-built infrared and ultraviolet tails so the picture stays readable where physics has left the visible band entirely.

The diagram is drawn to the same standard

The Minkowski renderer enforces a 1:1 aspect ratio between its z and ct axes — the light cone is 45° because the scales are equal, not because the lines are drawn at 45°. The ship's simultaneity line is drawn with slope β (signed — it flips with the velocity at the turnaround), its intersection with Earth's worldline is computed as t − zβ and marked, and the split view's frame strips scale the ship and the cosmos by exactly 1/γ in their respective frames. The file is fully self-contained: no external references, no localStorage, no network.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The beaming label outruns the beaming code: the toggle is labelled "Relativistic Beaming — headlight intensity scales as D³," and D³ is indeed the textbook specific-intensity law (the bolometric law is D⁴). But the applied brightness is a deliberate display compression: objects get 1 + 0.95·ln(1 + (D³−1)), stars a gentler 1 + 0.55·ln(1 + (D³−1)), with a power-law floor behind. Measured at 0.9c dead ahead (D = 4.36): the code applies a 5.2× boost where a literal D³ would apply 82.8× — sixteen times less, on purpose, so the apex stays readable instead of blowing out to white. The physics input is exact; the transfer curve to your screen is a tone map wearing the un-compressed law's name.

The beacon clocks show what you see, not what your frame calls "now": each gate's label reads tseen = tEarth − r/c — the retarded reading, the reading whose light is arriving at your eye this instant. That is honestly computed (the code says so in its comment), but it is not the ship-frame simultaneous reading, which differs by the relativity-of-simultaneity term β·Δz/c — at 0.9c, for a gate 5 light-seconds ahead, the seen reading lags Earth time by 5.0 s where the simultaneous reading differs by 4.5 s the other way. The simultaneous Earth time appears only in the Minkowski panel, and only for Earth itself. Two more clock couplings worth knowing: the light delay is applied only when the Terrell toggle is on — switch Terrell off and every beacon silently starts showing Earth coordinate time instead — and there is no lattice of independent clocks underneath: all beacons read the one tEarth, delayed by their own distance. Synchronized is exactly what they are; independent is what they are not.

Classical mode is classical only in the picture: the Classical OFF button clears all five visual toggles, and the badge obligingly reads "Classical (Off) / Euclidean" — but the clock stepper is not wired to the toggles. Measured with every effect off at β = 0.9: Earth time still accumulates at exactly γ = 2.294 times ship time. The world looks Newtonian while its clocks keep Einstein's books. A student using Classical mode to ask "what would Newton predict?" gets the right picture and the wrong clocks.

The title undersells the throttle: the page is titled "0c → 0.995c," but the slider's top segment and the thrust clamp both run to MAX_BETA = 0.998 — γ = 15.82, Dfwd = 31.6. The ship goes faster than the cover says.

Free flight's "g" is a dial; Twin mode's is a unit: in free flight the proper-acceleration slider is scaled for feel (0.17 of rapidity per g per proper second — millions of Earth g in the game's own units). In Twin mode, acceleration is genuine ly/yr² (≈ 0.97 g each). Nothing on screen tells you the word changed meaning between modes.

The twin's braking is a feedback law, and its joints are snapped: the burn legs fly constant proper acceleration, but the braking legs recompute their deceleration every frame — reqA = max(0.6a, (γ−1)/remaining) — and the turnaround and touchdown close with small state snaps (β and z pinned to their targets). The measured cost: both clocks land 1.6–3.2% short of the ideal constant-acceleration mission, while the age difference — the invariant the mission teaches — lands within 0.05% (Receipt 4). The autopilot is a pilot, not a proof; it just happens to fly within a whisker of one.

Free flight quotes a destination that isn't there: the telemetry's "Contracted Dist" in free flight reads against a nominal 4.0 ly — the Twin preset's default, carried into a mode with no destination — and the frame strips likewise label a 4 ly Earth–star track while you are flying an endless corridor. The contraction ratio applied to it is exact; the 4.0 ly itself is a leftover reference, not a place.

Presentation rates and painted light: free flight runs 2.0 ship proper seconds per real second and Twin mode 0.28 proper years per real second — watchability scalings, chosen in the open in code comments. The apex sky-glow's radius shrinks as 1/√γ, the engine drone's pitch rides ln γ, the gate chimes scale as D0.55 — the audio and glow are set dressing keyed to real quantities, not measurements of anything. Earth and the target star in Twin mode are gradient sprites placed by the exact transform, not rendered spheres. And the colour pipeline's infrared and ultraviolet tails (below 850 K, above 42,000 K apparent) are hand-drawn — unavoidable, since at high β most of the shifted light is invisible in reality, and the game has to paint something.

No honesty note on the page: the game's field notes are accurate as far as they go — the perpendicular-photon claim (cos θ′ = β) checks, the Terrell description checks, the twin resolution checks — but unlike some exhibits in this collection, the page carries no "what this leaves out" section of its own. This box is that section, supplied after the fact.
