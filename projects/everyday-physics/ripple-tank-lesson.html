Ripple Tank — the lesson

Ripple Tank is the school-lab classic rebuilt in code: drippers tap a shallow tray, circular waves spread, cross, and add — and where they cancel, dark still threads appear in moving water. There is a game bolted to the side of the bench (park a buoy on a nodal line; then park it on an antinode), but the real exhibit is the water itself, because the water is not drawn. It is computed, cell by cell, 120 times a second, by the actual wave equation — and this page is the audit that checked which wave equation, how honest its readouts are, and whether the diffraction on screen is physics or paint.

Ripple Tank was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its wave step headlessly.

The physics

Superposition: waves add, and pass through each other

The game's own lesson line, printed across the top bar, is the whole subject: waves add — two waves can make a bigger wave, a smaller one, or, at the nodal lines, flat water out of moving water. That is the superposition principle: where two wave trains cross, the surface height at each point is simply the sum of the heights each train would have made alone. Crest on crest gives a double crest (constructive interference); crest on trough gives still water (destructive interference); and when the geometry holds that cancellation in place — two steady sources of the same frequency — the still places line up into nodal lines: hyperbolas of flat water threaded through a moving surface, with bright antinodal bands between them where the motion doubles. The two-source preset exists to make exactly that picture, and the dark threads you see are not shading — the renderer darkens water whose measured local amplitude is near zero, so a nodal line on screen is a place the simulation itself reports as calm.

Diffraction: a gap is a new source

Send a wave train at a wall with a hole in it and the part that gets through does not continue as a narrow beam. It spreads into the shadow behind the wall as if the gap itself were a fresh point source — diffraction, strongest when the gap is comparable to the wavelength. Put two gaps in the wall and the two new sources interfere beyond it: Young's double-slit pattern, bright fringes where the two spreading trains arrive in step, dark fringes where they cancel. Both presets are on the bench (Single-Slit, Double-Slit), and the audit below measured the field behind the wall to confirm the spreading and the fringes are in the numbers, not just in the colour map.

v = fλ — and the speed is a property of the water, not the dripper

Every wave train obeys the same bookkeeping: speed equals frequency times wavelength, v = fλ. The dripper chooses the frequency; the water chooses the speed; the wavelength is whatever is left over. Double the tapping rate in a real tank and the crests don't move faster — they move closer together. Ripple Tank keeps that faith: its speed is a constant of the tank (12.0 cm/s), the frequency sliders run 1.5–6 Hz, and λ follows. What real water adds — and the page's honesty card says this out loud — is dispersion: in actual water the speed is not perfectly constant. Long gravity waves outrun short ones, and at very short wavelengths surface tension takes over and the ripples speed up again. The tank's flat 12.0 cm/s is the textbook simplification. How flat is it inside the simulation itself? That turned out to be a measurable question, and the audit measured it — see Receipt 2.

The math the game runs

The commission allowed two honest engines: add up circular waves from each source (superposition), or simulate a height field. Ripple Tank runs the height field — and, unusually, the page confesses it in its own words:

"Simulation method: A 2D explicit finite-difference height-field solver (∂²u/∂t² = c²∇²u − γ ∂u/∂t) using a 9-point isotropic Laplacian on a 300 × 200 grid — a linear shallow-water height-field approximation, not 3D Navier–Stokes fluid flow."

The audit read the step, and the confession is accurate. The tank is a 300 × 200 grid of heights (each cell 0.20 cm of a 60 × 40 cm tray), kept in three rotating buffers — previous, current, next. Every step, each cell's next height is:

unext = u + (u − uprev) · (1 − γ) + (r²/6) · [ 4·(N+S+E+W) + (NE+NW+SE+SW) − 20·u ] The bracket is the 9-point isotropic Laplacian: the four edge neighbours weighted 4, the four diagonals weighted 1, the centre −20. The Courant number r = c·dt/dx is fixed at 0.50, the step is fixed at 1/120 s, and γ is the Water Damping slider (0.0080 per step by default) applied to the velocity term. The speed is not measured out of this — it is built in: c = r × 120 steps/s × 0.20 cm = exactly 12.0 cm/s. Diffraction, interference, and reflection are never coded anywhere; they fall out of this one update applied everywhere at once.

The drippers do not add waves to the field analytically. Each step, a source stamps its current sine value — amp · sin(2πft + phase) — into a small 5 × 5 patch, blending each cell toward that value (92% at the centre cell, falling to 7% at the corners). The field then propagates the stamp away on its own. Up to four sources can run at once, each with its own frequency (1.5–6 Hz) and amplitude (0–2.0) sliders, each draggable to a new cell — the position lives on the source object and is read fresh every step, so a dragged dripper leaves no ghost behind it. Calm Water zeroes all three buffers and the amplitude envelope: the tank really does go flat, not just dark.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the shipped update rule — same stencil, same constants, same source injection — into a headless harness and ran it. Every number below is measured from that field, not quoted from the page.

Receipt 1: the model is the height field, and the page says so

Verdict: code and confession match. No per-source sinusoid is ever summed into the display; there is one field, one update, and sources that drive it locally. This is the stronger of the two allowed engines — interference and diffraction don't have to be faked, because a height field can't help producing them — and it is also the riskier one to claim, which makes the page's precise wording ("explicit finite-difference height-field solver… 9-point isotropic Laplacian… 300 × 200") worth crediting: every particular in that sentence checks out against the code, including the grid size and the damping term.

Receipt 2: dispersion — the one place "exact" bends, by 1.3%

The honesty card claims "all frequencies travel at the exact same speed (v = 12.0 cm/s)." A discrete grid usually breaks that promise — short waves crawl — so the audit measured the phase speed directly: two probes a few cells apart, the phase lag between them read by DFT at the source frequency, sampled before any edge reflection could return. Damping was zeroed in the harness to isolate the effect; the update rule was otherwise the shipped one.

So the grid does disperse — a 6 Hz wave, only ten cells long, runs about 1.3% slow — but the 9-point isotropic stencil earns its keep: across the whole slider range the speed holds within 1.3% of the design value, where a plain 5-point stencil would sag several times further at the short end. The page's sentence is right in spirit and in size; only the word "exact" overstates it. In real water, remember, the effect it is simplifying away is not 1.3% — it is the whole behaviour of the surface.

Receipt 3: diffraction and the double slit are in the field, not the paint

Barriers are real obstacles in the update, not drawings: a wall is rasterized into a mask (stamped disks along the segment, making it about 4–5 cells thick), masked cells are pinned to height 0 every step, their stored water is zeroed when the wall is built, and a dripper cannot be placed or dragged inside one. The audit rebuilt the Single-Slit preset's geometry — wall at x = 126, one gap ≈18 cells wide — drove it at 3.2 Hz, and sampled RMS height 64 cells behind the wall:

If the gap merely punched a beam through, the shadow probes would read zero. They read a quarter to a third of the on-axis field: the wave is genuinely spreading from the gap into the shadow, as a new source would. The Double-Slit geometry (two ≈13-cell gaps) shows the second act: RMS behind the wall swings between 0.045 at fringe maxima and 0.003 at the nodes — a 15-to-1 contrast, with the near-zero lines landing where two-source interference says they should. Fringes in the numbers, not just in the colours.

Receipt 4: the edges reflect — measured, and the page agrees

After the interior update, the code copies each edge row and column from its nearest interior neighbour — the standard mirror implementation of a Neumann boundary (∂u/∂n = 0) — and the honesty card says exactly that: waves bounce off the walls here, unlike a real tank's absorbing beaches. The audit confirmed it energetically: with damping set to zero, a one-second splash was left to slosh in the closed tank, and the field's RMS height read 0.091 at t = 2 s, 0.098 at t = 10 s, and 0.122 at t = 20 s. Absorbing edges would bleed that steadily toward zero; reflective edges keep the energy in the tray, swapping it between height and motion indefinitely. (One asymmetry the card doesn't mention: the barriers use the opposite treatment — their cells are pinned flat, Dirichlet-style. Five cells of pinned water passes nothing measurable, so in practice walls and edges both reflect; they just do it by different rules.)

Receipt 5: the readouts are computed, not measured — and the game score is the opposite

The v / f / λ panel is arithmetic, not instrumentation. updateFormulaUI() takes the wave speed from the compile-time constant (12.0 cm/s), the frequency from the selected dripper's slider, and divides: λ = v/f. The equation line ("v = fλ → 12.0 = 3.00 × 4.00") and the in-tank scale bar are printed from those same numbers. No crest is ever located, no timing is ever taken. Two things soften the verdict: the arithmetic is honest — it is exactly the relation the tank is built on, and Receipt 2 shows the field itself honours it to within 1.3% — and the panel silently describes only the selected source, which matters when your four drippers run at four frequencies (the Sync f button exists for that reason). But a student should know the difference between a readout that reports the water and one that predicts it. This one predicts.

The game, by contrast, keeps its hands on the water. In Hit the Target, every physics step reads the actual field height at the buoy's single cell into a 64-step ring buffer; the live amplitude is a smoothed sliding-window peak over roughly 1.15 wave periods of that real signal. Odd rounds ask you to hold that measured amplitude under 0.075 for three seconds (park the buoy on a nodal line); even rounds ask you to hold it at or above 0.300 (park it on an antinode). No distance formula anywhere near the score — and the anti-cheat details show the builder knew the shortcuts: both drippers must be genuinely running (amplitude sliders are locked at 1.2 or above and disabled in game mode, and the win check demands two sources at amplitude ≥ 1.0), a 30-cell (6 cm) keep-out ring stops you dropping a dripper on the buoy, tuning one dripper's frequency retunes both so the interference stays coherent, and a settle timer — farthest source distance divided by the true wave speed, plus 0.35 s — runs before any holding counts, so you can't win with waves that haven't arrived yet. Rounds won and total time are kept on screen; your best (most rounds, fastest time) is the game's one localStorage key, ripple_tank_best_v1.

How it's built — the coding choices

Three buffers and a fixed step

The wave equation is second-order in time — the next height depends on where the surface is and where it was — so the field lives in three arrays that rotate each step: previous becomes current, current becomes next, and the old previous is recycled as the new scratch buffer. Nothing is allocated in the loop. The step is fixed at 1/120 s and the main loop pays real time into an accumulator, running as many whole steps as it's owed — capped at 8 per frame, with any larger debt forgiven. On a slow device the tank runs in slow motion rather than taking bigger, wronger steps: the physics you see is always the physics at 120 steps a second.

The picture is a measurement, smoothed

Rendering packs the 300 × 200 field into a pixel buffer directly. Brightness rides on the instantaneous height plus a slope highlight; darkness rides on a per-cell amplitude envelope with a fast attack and slow release — a running estimate of how much each cell has been moving lately. That envelope is why nodal lines read as still dark threads instead of flickering: a cell on a node has genuinely not moved, recently or ever, and the envelope remembers. The buoy's game meter is the same idea pointed at one cell.

Presets are geometry, set honestly

The four presets rebuild sources, walls, and damping from scratch each time. Two Sources boots the game — two coherent drippers at 3.2 Hz, symmetric about the centre, so the hyperbolic nodal pattern is on screen before you touch anything. Single-Slit and Double-Slit drop their damping to 0.0050 so the far-side patterns survive the longer trip, and their gaps are cut as fractions of the wall's length (the double slit's are ≈13 cells, about 2.5 cm each — set by the preset, not by the gap slider, which governs only walls you draw yourself).

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate — with one word trimmed: the model, grid, stencil, linearity, and reflective Neumann edges are all as the card describes, and the audit verified each against the code. The card's "no dispersion" bullet says all frequencies travel at "the exact same speed"; measured, they travel at 11.85–12.00 cm/s across the slider range — a numerical dispersion of at most ~1.3%, far smaller than the real-water dispersion the bullet is really confessing to, but not zero. Read "exact" as "to within about a percent."

The readouts are predictions, not measurements: v is a built-in constant, f is the selected dripper's slider, λ is their quotient, and the scale bar is drawn from the same three numbers. They are correct — Receipt 2 confirms the field obeys them — but nothing on the panel ever looks at the water. With several drippers at different frequencies, the panel describes only the selected one.

Two boundary rules, one mentioned: the tank's edges reflect by mirror (Neumann), as the card says; barrier interiors are pinned to height 0 (Dirichlet) every step, which the card doesn't mention. Both reflect in practice. A real tank's foam beaches do neither — they absorb — and the card is right to flag that difference.

The dripper is a stamp, not a sum: a source blends its patch of field toward the sine value each step (92% at the centre cell) rather than adding to it. That's what a driven dripper should do, and once the wave leaves the patch the linear update takes over and superposition is exact — but at the source cell itself, the field is overwritten, not accumulated.

The game's amplitude is an envelope, in envelope units: the win thresholds (0.075 and 0.300) apply to a smoothed peak of |height| at one cell over ~1.15 periods — a fair, playable definition of "amplitude at the buoy," measured from the real field, but not an RMS and not a quantity the page defines. The settle timer, keep-out ring, and locked amplitudes are anti-shortcut rules, and they're good ones; they're also part of the game the page doesn't explain.

Small print: the speed 12.0 cm/s is a chosen constant (Courant 0.50 × 120 steps × 0.20 cm), not something derived from a water depth or gravity — change any of the three grid numbers and the "water" changes with them. Damping is a per-step velocity factor, so its strength is tied to the 120 Hz step rate. And under heavy lag the loop forgives time debt past 8 steps a frame, so a struggling machine sees slow water, never wrong water.
