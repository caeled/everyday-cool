Butterfly — the lesson

Butterfly launches a fan of double pendulums that differ by almost nothing — a thousandth of a degree, a hundred-thousandth if you ask — and lets you watch them agree, agree, agree… and then, inside a few seconds, become a rainbow of completely different machines. Nothing random is added. Every pendulum runs the same exact equations from (nearly) the same start, and the future still evaporates. The game's own lesson line is printed under its title: deterministic doesn't mean predictable — tiny unseen differences double until the future looks random. This page is the other half of it: the physics it runs, and the audit that measured the evaporation — the actual doubling time this build's own mathematics produces, in both of its regimes.

Butterfly was built by Google Antigravity (Gemini) at Steven Powell's direction; this lesson and its audit were written by Muse, from reading the game's code and running its physics core headlessly.

The physics

One pendulum is a clock. Two, coupled, are a coin that hasn't landed

A single pendulum is the most predictable machine ever built: give it an angle and it swings that swing forever, which is why it ran the world's clocks for three centuries. Bolt a second pendulum to the end of the first and the system crosses a line. The two rods trade energy back and forth through the coupling at the joint — sometimes the outer bob whips around in full flips, sometimes both swing sedately — and which happens next depends on the state now with a sensitivity that has no bottom. The equations never change and never roll dice. What changes is that nearby starting points stop having nearby futures. That property — sensitive dependence on initial conditions — is the whole exhibit, and the fan of coloured pendulums is how you see it: while they overlap, the future is known; the moment the fan opens, it isn't, and no amount of extra care rewinds that, because the separation grows by multiplication, not addition.

The Lyapunov exponent: the interest rate on ignorance

Chaos has a measurable speed. Take two copies of a system separated by a tiny angle δ. In a chaotic regime the separation doesn't grow by a fixed amount per second — it grows by a fixed factor: δ(t) ≈ δ₀·eλt. The number λ is the Lyapunov exponent, and its friendlier form is the doubling time, ln 2 / λ: how long before your uncertainty is twice as big. Every doubling costs you one binary digit of knowledge about the start. Start knowing the angle to one part in a million and you hold about twenty doublings of foresight — total. After that, the two systems are simply doing different things, and the only honest prediction left is statistical. Butterfly's telemetry turns this into a stopwatch: it tracks the ensemble's actual angular spread live, plots its logarithm against time (a straight climbing line is exponential growth, drawn), and once the spread crosses 30° it reports a λ and a doubling time computed from the run you just watched. The audit checked those numbers against an independent fit, below.

Weather is this pendulum, wearing the sky

In 1963 Edward Lorenz, running a toy weather model, restarted a simulation from printed intermediate values — rounded to three decimals instead of the machine's six — and watched the new forecast walk away from the old one completely. Same equations, same model, a difference smaller than the printout could show. That experiment is the direct ancestor of this page, and the reason real forecasts have a horizon: the atmosphere's errors double every few days, so a forecast is a fan of possible weathers that stays bundled for about a week and is fully opened by two. When a forecaster shows you a cone of hurricane tracks or a spread of model runs, you are looking at Butterfly's rainbow fan with better funding. Deterministic physics, honestly integrated, still only buys a finite depth of future — the depth is set by λ and by how precisely the present can be measured, and no computer purchase changes the arithmetic.

Chaos is energy-dependent — and the build knows it

Turn the energy down and the same machine stops being chaotic. Released from small angles, a double pendulum settles into quasi-periodic motion: two incommensurate rhythms braided together, complicated to watch but stable forever — twins released together stay together, breathing slightly apart and back as the rhythms beat, never blowing open. Butterfly ships this regime as its Gentle Periodic preset (22° / 14°), and the audit verified it is genuinely there: this is not a build that fakes divergence at every setting. Chaos lives in the high-energy part of the house; downstairs, the clocks still work.

The math the game runs

The engine integrates the true coupled equations of the double pendulum, written as a 2×2 mass-matrix solve. With Δ = θ₁ − θ₂, the angular accelerations come from:

[ (m₁+m₂)L₁  m₂L₂cosΔ ] [ ω̇₁ ]  =  [ −m₂L₂ω₂²sinΔ − (m₁+m₂)g·sinθ₁ ] [ L₁cosΔ      L₂      ] [ ω̇₂ ]     [  L₁ω₁²sinΔ − g·sinθ₂             ] This is the Euler–Lagrange system for two point masses on massless rigid rods, divided through so the determinant of the system is m₁ + m₂·sin²Δ. Every term the physics demands is present: the centripetal coupling (ω²·sinΔ terms that fling the outer bob), both gravity torques, and the cosΔ off-diagonals that make each rod's acceleration depend on the other's position. The audit compared this function against the canonical textbook form of the accelerations on 2,000 random states with random masses, lengths, and gravities: agreement to 1.1 × 10−13. It is not two pendulums glued together, and it is not a scripted wobble.

Time is stepped with classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta at a fixed dt = 0.001 s — 1,000 steps per simulated second, whatever the speed button says; the speed controls only change how many fixed steps each real second buys. The fan itself is built with discipline: pendulum i starts at θ₁ + (i/(N−1) − ½)·ε, θ₂ identical for all, both angular velocities zero, and every pendulum in the ensemble is stepped by the same parameter object — same masses, same lengths, same gravity. The only difference anywhere in the fan is the starting angle it advertises.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The audit lifted the game's own derivative function, RK4 stepper, energy function, and spread measurement out of the shipped file, verbatim, and ran them headlessly. Every number below is measured from the game's mathematics, not quoted from its labels.

Receipt 1: the energy books balance — the chaos is real, not numerical

This is the first test any chaos exhibit has to pass. A sloppy integrator manufactures its own divergence: energy leaking in or out makes twins separate for arithmetic reasons, and the "chaos" is just error compounding. So the audit flew the lead pendulum and watched total mechanical energy (the game's own computeEnergy: both bobs' kinetic energy, cross-term included, plus potential):

Endpoint drift is the same size as the worst mid-run wobble — the error oscillates instead of accumulating, which is the signature of an honest integrator in a chaotic system. Over two minutes of violent flipping, the energy is constant to about eight decimal places. Whatever separates the twins in the next receipt, it is the dynamics doing it, not the arithmetic.

A least-squares fit of log-separation against time across the clean exponential window (8×10−4° to 3°) gives λ ≈ 2.15 s−1 — a doubling time of ≈ 0.32 s. The default fan (N = 24, ε = 0.001°) agrees: horizon at 6.42 s, fitted λ ≈ 1.87 s−1, doubling ≈ 0.37 s. Two honest qualifications the chart on the page will also show you if you watch it: the growth is bursty — the instantaneous rate surges and even briefly reverses as the perturbation sloshes between the two angles — so λ is an average over the climb, not a metronome; and the first second or so is a slow shoulder before the exponential takes hold. Certainty doesn't drain at a constant drip. It drains like a tank with a widening hole.

Receipt 3: the page's own Lyapunov meter, graded

The λeff readout is computed from the actual simulation — the spread it uses is the live ensemble's maximum pairwise angular separation, sampled as the fan flies, and the chart plots that real history on a log scale. It is not an animation. Its formula, though, is a two-point estimate rather than a fit: once the spread crosses 30°, it reports λ = ln(30/ε) / thorizon, the average growth rate across the whole climb from ε to 30°. For the twin run above that yields λeff = 1.76 s−1 (doubling 0.40 s) against the windowed fit's 2.15 s−1 (0.32 s) — the meter reads about 20% low, because the two-point average bills the slow opening shoulder and the saturated arrival at the same rate as the clean exponential middle. Same story for the default fan: meter 1.61 s−1, fit 1.87 s−1. Verdict: an honest instrument with a stated method, systematically a touch conservative. The page also shows no λ at all until the horizon is breached — before that, the correct report is "hasn't diverged yet," and that's what it says.

Ten minutes at low energy and the fan is still a fan of one pendulum, breathing gently as the two natural rhythms beat against each other. The build diverges where the physics says to diverge and holds together where the physics says to hold. That contrast — same code, same integrator, two regimes — is the strongest evidence the chaos upstairs is the genuine article.

How it's built — the coding choices

One derivative function, one stepper, no favourites

Lab ensemble, game lead, and game ghosts all advance through the same rk4Step and the same parameter builder, so the thing you practise on in the Lab is the thing that scores you in the game. The fan's construction (read in resetLabSimulation) offsets only θ₁, spread evenly across ±ε/2; θ₂, both velocities, and every physical parameter are shared. There is no per-pendulum jitter hiding anywhere — the audit read the loop, and the fan is exactly as honest as its label.

Chaos Fling: the score is arithmetic on a real release

In the game, five ghost pendulums trail the lead at θ₁ offsets of −0.10° to +0.10° in 0.05° steps, all stepped by the same engine. When you release, the projectile inherits the lower bob's actual position and velocity from the simulation at that instant and flies an exactly-integrated ballistic arc (closed-form step under constant gravity — no integration error in flight), tested against the target with a swept segment–circle test so a fast bob can't tunnel through between frames. The score is printed arithmetic: 500 base, up to 350 for closeness to the centre, a chaos bonus (150 + 30 per second) for releasing after the level's bonus time, and a shot bonus that decays 40 points per attempt. The mass-ratio slider feeds the game's integrator directly (m₂ = ratio × m₁ in the shared parameter builder), and the levels are designed around it — Level 3's hint about a lighter lower bob cracking like a whip is real dynamics, not flavour text.

The telemetry shows its work

The energy strip displays the lead pendulum's live total energy and its live relative drift ΔE/E₀ — the exhibit grades its own integrator on screen, continuously, which is why Receipt 1 could be checked against the page's own instrument as well as the audit's harness. The divergence chart is the recorded history of the ensemble spread, downsampled (every second point of the older half) only once it passes 900 samples, so a long run stays readable without inventing data.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate: massless rigid rods and frictionless point pivots at default; a 2D vacuum plane — no air drag, rod flex, or out-of-plane twist; and a floating-point ceiling, stated plainly: even an ε = 0 twin drifts from the true trajectory over long chaotic times, because 64-bit floats carry ~16 digits and chaos spends them. The audit's numbers sit comfortably inside those confessions. An optional Damping slider exists (a Rayleigh-style dissipation added to the equations); at its default of 0, everything on this page is the undamped truth.

The fan is smaller than the pitch: the commission described 20–50 pendulums. The presets fly 24, 24, 28, and 24, and the Ensemble Size slider tops out at 40. Nothing about the physics changes — but if you go looking for fifty, the shelf holds forty.

λeff is a two-point average, not a fitted exponent: as Receipt 3 shows, the meter divides the whole climb (ε → 30°) by the horizon time, so it understates the clean-window Lyapunov exponent by roughly a fifth (1.76 vs 2.15 s−1 on the audited run). It is computed from the real separation — but read it as "average doubling pace of the whole escape," not as the local exponent.

The code comment oversells the energy figure slightly: the engine's header promises energy conservation "typically ~10−11." The audit measured worst-case relative wobble of 7 × 10−9 to 2.5 × 10−8 over minutes of chaotic flight — far inside the comment's own "< 10−6" bound, and the live ΔE/E₀ readout on the page shows the true figure rather than the comment's. Superb bookkeeping; the round number in the comment is the optimistic end of it.

Set dressing after the release: once the bob is flung, the leftover upper rod switches to a simple damped single-pendulum stepped with plain Euler, and the ghost projectiles fly Euler steps while the real projectile uses the exact ballistic step. All of it is visual — the released bob, the hit test, and the score are computed from the true state — but the rod's post-release sway is theatre, not simulation.

The game fixes some dials: in Chaos Fling, gravity is locked at 9.81, damping at 0, and both rods at length 1 — the length, gravity, and damping sliders are Lab-only, and the page marks them so. What the game does hand you — both angles and the mass ratio — reaches the integrator untouched.
