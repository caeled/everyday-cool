Maxwell's Demon — the lesson

Two chambers of gas at the same temperature, a wall between them, and a trapdoor only you can open. Let the fast particles drift right and the slow ones left, and one side heats while the other cools — order out of chaos, apparently for free, in defiance of the second law of thermodynamics. Maxwell invented the demon in 1867 to ask whether the law could be beaten by nothing more than cleverness and information. This exhibit lets you play the demon for sixty seconds — and then presents the bill. This page is the other half: the physics it runs, and the audit that checked whether its gas, its thermometers, its entropy ledger, and its bill are real.

The physics

Temperature is just average random motion

The game's banner line is the whole subject in one sentence: heat is just random motion. There is no "heat fluid" in the box — 220 hard disks flying and colliding, and nothing else. Temperature is a bookkeeping name for their average kinetic energy: double the mean energy per particle and you have doubled the temperature. That is why the thermometers in this lab can be honest in a way most games' thermometers aren't — they don't read a temperature variable, they count the motion, every frame, and the audit below verified the counting.

The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve is the shape of chance

Start every particle at random and let collisions shuffle the energy, and the speeds settle into a distribution nobody imposed: a few slow particles, a few very fast ones, and a hump in between whose exact shape Maxwell derived in 1860. The shape depends on the dimension of the world. In three dimensions the curve rises as v² at low speed; in this game's flat, two-dimensional gas it rises as v — there is only one ring's worth of velocity directions at each speed instead of a shell's worth. The chart on the game's wall draws the 2D curve, and the audit compared the simulated gas against both shapes to prove it (Receipt 2).

Entropy is counting, and the demon is an accountant's problem

Entropy counts arrangements: a gas with its fast and slow particles mixed has vastly more ways to be than the same gas sorted, so the sorted state is the improbable one, and left alone it dissolves back. Maxwell's demon was meant to break that: a being who merely watches each particle and opens a door at the right moments sorts without pushing, heating nothing, doing no work — so where does the entropy bill land? The twentieth century found it, in three steps. Szilard (1929) shrank the demon to a one-molecule engine and showed information itself was the currency. Landauer (1961) found the exact tollbooth: erasing one bit of information costs at least kBT ln 2 of energy, dumped as heat — measurement can be free, forgetting cannot. Bennett (1982) put it together: the demon may measure and record for nothing, but its notebook fills up, and clearing the notebook to make room for the next measurement pays Landauer's price, every bit, forever. The demon doesn't break the second law. It defers the bill to its own memory.

The math the game runs

Three formulas carry the whole lab. The temperature of each chamber is read straight off the particles:

Tchamber = Tscale · ⟨v²⟩,   Tscale = T0 / vrms,0² In a 2D gas, mean kinetic energy per particle is exactly kBT, so temperature is proportional to the mean of v² — the factor of 2 lives inside the calibration (300 K is defined as the temperature at which the rms speed is 155 px/s). There is no thermostat variable anywhere in the engine; the gauges are averages over live particles.

The entropy readout is the real two-box formula for a 2D ideal gas — not a score dressed up as physics:

ΔSgas / kB = Σchambers Nc · ln[ (N/2 / Nc) · (Tc / T0) ] A 2D ideal gas has S ∝ N ln(A·T / N) per chamber (in 3D the T would carry a 3/2 power). With both chambers the same area, the areas cancel, leaving counts and temperatures measured against the uniform start: half the gas each side, all at T0. Sorting drives this number negative; mixing returns it to zero.

And the bill is Landauer's, charged per bit of demon information:

ΔSinfo / kB = bits × ln 2 One bit, erased, costs kB ln 2 of entropy — at the game's scale, 0.693 kB per bit, added to the universe's side of the ledger. What counts as a "bit" here is a design decision, and the audit itemises it in Receipt 4.

The audit — claims checked against the code, with receipts

The engine ships exposed for exactly this: the game's own IdealGasSimulation class was lifted out of the file untouched and driven headlessly — seeded runs, scripted demons, wedged doors. Every number below is measured from that engine, not quoted from the page.

The collision code is the textbook one, done carefully: for each overlapping pair it applies the equal-mass elastic impulse along the line of centres — the two particles exchange the components of velocity along that line, tangential components untouched — and it separates the overlap symmetrically without touching velocities, so the de-overlap can't smuggle energy in. Walls reflect specularly (the box takes the momentum, as a real box does). The page's own telemetry prints a KE-drift readout; the audit confirms it isn't flattering itself.

Two honest footnotes. The starting velocities are drawn as Gaussian components and rescaled per chamber to exactly 300 K, so the gas is born Maxwell–Boltzmann; the receipt is that 90 seconds of collisions keep it there at the measured temperature. And the formal χ² against the 2D curve is inflated (reduced χ² ≈ 109) because the samples are the same 220 disks re-measured every frame — correlated, not independent draws. As a shape comparison the verdict is unambiguous: this is the flatland curve, and the chart on the wall draws the flatland curve.

Receipt 3: the thermometers track mean energy exactly

One quirk the audit surfaced: with the door shut, each chamber's temperature is not merely steady, it is exactly constant forever — the reset normalises each chamber's total energy to precisely 300 K and sealed elastic collisions can never change it. Real thermometers jitter; these can't until something crosses the wall. Which sets up the honest noise number: let the two halves exchange freely and the ΔT between them wanders with a standard deviation of 37 K, swinging past 100 K on a 240-second watch, with no demon anywhere near it. With 220 particles, a ΔT of ±40 K can be pure weather. The page's honesty card says fluctuations are huge compared to a macroscopic gas; the audit measured how huge.

Sorting works, and the entropy formula is real — the audit recomputed ΔSgas from the raw counts and temperatures and matched the game's readout to the third decimal (−63.507). But read the bill carefully, because this is where the exhibit's physics becomes bookkeeping. A "bit" is charged: in auto mode, once per particle per entry into the door's sensor zone (a 1-bit hot/cold measurement, latched until the particle wanders off); in manual play, once per door toggle in either direction, plus once per particle crossing. The charge is then added to the entropy column — and nowhere else. No energy is debited from the gas, nothing is heated, no reservoir warms up; the gas's KE drift across a full sorting run is zero to fifteen decimal places. The bill is also generous: 847 bits were charged for a sort worth 63.5 kB of entropy — the ledger overpays Landauer's minimum about nine-fold, because it charges for every measurement, not for the net information that must eventually be erased. The consequence is structural: with a full bit billed per event, the net ΔS cannot go negative in any run the audit could produce, and the "2nd Law Intact" badge cannot be lost. That is the correct moral — the demon always loses on the accounting — arranged so that the accounting is never even close.

How it's built — the coding choices

A spatial hash, so 220 disks can collide 270 times a second

Checking every pair of disks against every other is 24,090 pairs per pass; the engine instead buckets particles into a grid of cells slightly wider than a disk's diameter and only tests neighbours — and allocates nothing per step, reusing two flat arrays as a linked list per cell. At the game's four substeps per frame it resolves roughly 270 collisions a second, each one the exact impulse from Receipt 1. The demon's sensor is the same style of thrift: it projects each approaching particle's arrival height at the door and weights its vote by how soon it arrives, so the auto-demon is a small physics forecast, not a peek at the answer.

The hot/cold line is the exact median of the distribution

The demon sorts at vmed = vrms·√(ln 2) — not an arbitrary "fast enough," but the precise speed that splits the 2D Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution in half, so at equilibrium exactly 50% of particles count as hot. The audit's snapshot found 49.1%. A demon sorting at the median is playing the fairest possible game against the gas — and still wins the temperature war while losing the entropy one, which is the entire 150-year-old point.

The flywheel is a scoreboard, and the page says so

The Carnot hub displays a genuine efficiency figure, η = 1 − TL/TR, but the "power" it integrates into work is a flat 0.15 W per kelvin of ΔT — and extracting that work never cools the hot chamber or warms the cold one. A real engine eats the gradient it feeds on; this one lets your gradient persist so the gas physics stays exact. The game's own honesty card confesses this in plain words ("without damping gas KE so collision energy conservation stays exact"), and the audit confirms the confession is accurate.

Simplifications and divergences, named

The game's own honesty card is accurate — the audit checked it line by line: the gas is 2D and 220 particles against a real ~1023 (and the measured noise — ΔT wandering ±37 K with excursions past 100 K — shows what that costs); collisions are perfectly elastic with no rotation, vibration, attraction, or radiation; the trapdoor is massless and frictionless; the Landauer accounting is, in the card's own word, "simplified." All of that holds.

The bill is bookkeeping, not physics: Landauer's cost enters only as bits × ln 2 added to the entropy readout. No energy is ever taken from the gas or dumped as heat anywhere in the simulation — the demon's notebook is charged on paper only. The charge is also per-event and over-counted (847 bits billed against a 63.5 kB entropy drop), so the net entropy stays comfortably positive and the second law is never actually at risk in play. A tighter model would charge only for erasures and let a careless demon flirt with breaking even; this one keeps the law safe by construction.

The demon's measurement is free and perfect: the sensor knows each approaching particle's exact speed and projected arrival height, instantly, at no cost — the entire charge is deferred to the erasure-style ledger. Real demons pay for sensing too; the page's card gestures at this ("real sensors dissipate orders of magnitude more") but the in-game sensor itself is idealised.

Work extraction doesn't eat the gradient: the flywheel's joules accumulate at 0.15 W/K without cooling the hot side — confessed on the page, confirmed in the code. Treat the work readout as a score for holding ΔT, not as energy that left the gas.

Sealed chambers have unnaturally calm thermometers: with the door shut, each chamber's temperature is exactly constant (its total energy is sealed and was normalised at reset), so all temperature weather in this lab happens across the doorway. And in manual play the billing is blunter than the card's "decision/crossing" phrase suggests: every toggle bills a bit — opening or closing — and every crossing bills another, whether or not the crossing helped.
