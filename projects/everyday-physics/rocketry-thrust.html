What you are looking at

Nine benches about one idea: a rocket moves by throwing mass backwards. Every number on the page comes from one small simulation core (marked ROCKET-CORE in the page source). All units are SI and labelled: metres, seconds, kilograms, newtons, pascals. Standard gravity is g₀ = 9.80665 m/s² exactly — the number that turns specific impulse in seconds into exhaust speed in metres per second.

1 · Push back

A cart on frictionless rails, loaded with bricks. Each brick leaves at speed u relative to the cart. Momentum before equals momentum after, so the cart gains m·u/(M + m) per throw. The "total momentum" readout adds up the cart and every flying brick — it stays at zero. Throwing one at a time beats throwing them all at once; throwing very many small ones approaches the rocket equation.

2 · The thrust equation and 3 · The nozzle

One engine, two views. The engine is an ideal de Laval nozzle: one-dimensional, isentropic flow with fixed gas properties (a textbook "frozen" model). Choose the propellant (it sets the gas temperature, molar mass and γ), the chamber pressure, the throat diameter, the expansion ratio ε = A_e/A_t, and the altitude (which sets the outside pressure from the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere). The page solves the exit Mach number from ε, then the exit pressure, exhaust speed, mass flow and thrust F = ṁ·v_e + (p_e − p_a)·A_e. Real engines lose a few percent to friction, heat and imperfect combustion, so the ideal Isp runs a little high — the proofs show by how much against two real engines. Flow separation (when p_e falls below about 0.4 × p_a, the Summerfield rule of thumb) is flagged but not modelled.

Rocketry & Thrust — the physics of rockets

Throw something backwards and you go forwards. Do it fast enough, long enough, with the mass you carried along, and you reach orbit. Nine benches: the push, the thrust equation, the nozzle, specific impulse, the rocket equation, liftoff, the Δv budget, model rocket motors, and falling around the world.

The one idea

A rocket carries its own push. It has nothing to push against — not the ground, not the air — so it pushes against its own exhaust: it throws mass out of the back as fast as it can, and by Newton's third law the mass pushes back. The faster the exhaust leaves (that speed, divided by g₀, is the specific impulse), and the more of the rocket is propellant (the mass ratio), the more speed the rocket can gain — and the rocket equation says exactly how much: Δv = ve·ln(m₀/mf). That logarithm is why rockets are mostly fuel, why they come in stages, and why getting to orbit is hard. Everything below is that paragraph made touchable.

1 · Push back — the whole of rocket science

You stand on a cart on frictionless rails with a pile of bricks. Throw a brick backwards and the cart rolls forwards: the brick's momentum one way is exactly paid for by the cart's momentum the other way. A balloon rocket on a string is the same trick with air for bricks (try it — it is the first experiment card in the Physics room). Guess first: is it better to throw all the bricks at once, or one at a time?

2 · The thrust equation — two ways a rocket engine pushes

A real engine throws hot gas instead of bricks — hundreds of kilograms every second, at a few kilometres per second. Its thrust has two parts. The big one is momentum thrust: mass flow times exhaust speed. The small one is pressure thrust: if the exhaust leaves the nozzle at a higher pressure than the air outside, it pushes on the nozzle's open end; if lower, the air pushes back. Climb the altitude slider and watch the outside pressure fall away.

3 · The nozzle — squeeze, then spread

The hourglass is a de Laval nozzle. Hot gas speeds up as the passage narrows, reaches exactly the speed of sound at the narrowest point (the throat), and then — the counter-intuitive part — keeps speeding up as the passage widens, because a supersonic gas expands, cooling and dropping pressure as it goes. The colour shows Mach number along the nozzle. A bigger bell (larger ε) squeezes more speed out of the gas, until the exhaust pressure drops below the air outside: then the air pinches the plume (over-expanded). In space there is no air to pinch, so the plume balloons outward (under-expanded) and a bigger bell always helps — that is why upper stages wear huge bells and boosters wear short ones.

Honestly: a vacuum bell fired at sea level does not just lose thrust — its flow tears away from the wall in an unsteady shock that can wreck the nozzle. This bench flags it (the Summerfield rule of thumb: p_e below about 0.4 × p_a) but keeps computing attached flow, so its sea-level number for a vacuum bell is a warning, not a prediction. For shock waves you can watch, open Mach — the supersonic lab.

4 · Specific impulse — the miles-per-gallon of rockets

Isp is how long one kilogram of propellant can push with one kilogram's weight (9.80665 N) — so it is measured in seconds. Multiply by g₀ and you get the effective exhaust speed. Higher is better, and it is the exponent's denominator in the rocket equation, so small gains matter enormously. Pick a job below and see how much propellant each engine needs to do it.

The job column uses each engine's vacuum Isp (sea-level for the model motor), and ignores the mass of tanks and engines — so it flatters every engine equally. The ion engines win the propellant contest by miles and lose the thrust contest by more: NSTAR pushes with at most about 92 millinewtons, the weight of a few sheets of paper, which is why they spend months on a burn.

5 · The rocket equation — and why rockets come in stages

Every kilogram of propellant you add must itself be carried — and pushed — by the propellant burned before it. That is why Δv grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio: doubling the propellant does not double the speed. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published this in 1903.

Staging: throw away the empty tanks

Same liftoff mass, same payload, same engines, same tank-to-propellant weight — cut into 1, 2, 3 or 4 stages. Each dropped stage is dead weight you no longer accelerate. Play the result in Stage It, the room's two-stage game.

6 · Liftoff — thrust against weight

A rocket leaves the pad only when its thrust beats its weight: the thrust-to-weight ratio must exceed 1. Every second it hangs in the air, gravity takes 9.8 m/s of the speed its engines made — the gravity loss. The air takes its share too (drag loss), most of it in the first minute, around max-Q, the moment of greatest aerodynamic pressure. This bench flies a single stage straight up; try TWR 0.9, then 1.1, then 2.

Honestly: straight up is the worst way to fly to orbit. It gathers height, not the sideways speed that orbit needs, so gravity keeps its full toll; real rockets pitch over within the first minute (a gravity turn) and arrive at orbit with roughly 1.5–2 km/s of losses instead. The rocket here also never throttles, so its acceleration climbs to ten g or more as the tanks empty — real crewed rockets throttle back or shut engines down to stay near 3–4 g. 9 · Falling around the world — the last step to orbit

Isaac Newton's thought experiment: fire a cannonball sideways from a mountain above the air. Too slow, and it falls back to Earth. Faster, and it lands further away — until, at just the right speed, the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It is still falling; it just never lands. That is an orbit, and that sideways speed — about 7.7 km/s at 400 km — is what most of a rocket's Δv buys. The ISS is doing it right now; watch it in Satellite Watch, or catch it crossing your sky in Tonight's Sky.

"Something to push against" — 1920, corrected 1969

In January 1920 an unsigned editorial in The New York Times scolded Robert Goddard, who had proposed a rocket that could reach the Moon. A rocket could not work in a vacuum, it said, because it would have nothing to push against — Goddard, it sneered, seemed to lack "the knowledge ladled out daily in high schools."

The correction, because this room teaches it: the exhaust is the thing a rocket pushes against. Bench 1 has no air in it at all, and the cart still rolls — and bench 2 shows a rocket pushes slightly harder in vacuum, because the air outside only ever subtracts. On 17 July 1969, with Apollo 11 on its way to the Moon, the Times printed "A Correction": "it is now definitely established that a rocket can function in a vacuum as well as in an atmosphere. The Times regrets the error."

The tyranny of the rocket equation

Astronaut Don Pettit gave the logarithm its nickname in a 2012 NASA essay written aboard the International Space Station. Pick where you are going and pick your propellant, he wrote, and the rocket equation dictates how much of the rocket must be propellant — "hope, wishing, or tantrums cannot alter this result." His comparison: a soda can is about 94% soda and 6% can by mass, while the Space Shuttle's external tank was 96% propellant and 4% structure. A rocket is a thinner container than a drink can.

Honestly: the tyranny is exactly bench 5's curve. Better engines (higher Isp) flatten it; staging sidesteps it; nothing repeals it. Every rocket you have ever seen is shaped by this one equation.
