What would you do next?

Dissect a link without visiting it

The hostname tells you which host is being addressed. Familiar words elsewhere in a link can mislead. HTTPS encrypts a connection; it does not prove the operator is honest.

This bench parses a URL; it does not scan for malware, identify its owner, follow redirects, or determine its registrable domain. A short link may hide its final destination. A clean-looking result is not a safety certificate.

If something has already happened

Stop further payments and preserve messages, receipts, transaction references, and the timeline. Contact the bank or payment provider promptly using its official app or a number you already trust; ask about stopping or reversing the transaction. Recovery is not guaranteed.

If you shared a password, change it through the real service and secure other accounts where you reused it. If someone gained remote computer access, disconnect that session and follow the FTC's computer-access guidance. Children: bring in a trusted adult.

FTC: choose steps for your payment or information exposure

The second scam: “We can recover it”

An unexpected recovery offer that demands an upfront payment or financial details is a major danger. Knowing facts about your earlier loss does not authenticate the caller. Independently verify any claimed agency. Do not send another payment to unlock a promised refund.

The lookup shelf

Mostly US reporting resources, with global domain tools. Elsewhere, use your country's official consumer-protection authority and local police. Reports do not guarantee reimbursement. Search results, reviews, registration, and lack of complaints are pieces of evidence, not proof of honesty.

Make your own evidence sheet

Write: What is claimed? What can I verify independently? What remains unknown? What pressure am I feeling? What is the safest next step? Keep private evidence offline; do not publish account numbers or accuse someone based on a single clue.

One more investigation

Search a suspicious offer's distinctive wording in quotation marks. Look for copied descriptions and conflicting contact details. Open the real organization's website independently. Screenshots, follower counts, and caller ID can all be misleading.
