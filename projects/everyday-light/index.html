Everyday
Light.

Light is the fastest thing there is, and the only one you see everything else by. This room slows it down. Mix colour the two opposite ways — lamps adding toward white, paints subtracting toward dark. Strike chords on a wheel of twelve hues, the way musicians strike them on a circle of twelve notes. Bend light through a lens and watch the picture land upside down. And read the whole sky — blue overhead, red at the edge of the day — off one rule about scattering.

01 / TWO WAYS TO MIX

Colour adds. Colour subtracts. Pick the right one.

Every colour you have ever mixed came from one of two opposite games. Light adds: coloured lamps pour their light into one pool, and the pool is brighter than anything poured in. Pigment subtracts: each paint is a filter that swallows part of the spectrum, and what survives all the filters is all you get. The two benches below are the same three sliders run by opposite rules — play them against each other.

Mixing light — additive

Mixing paint — subtractive

The printer's three: cyan, magenta, yellow. Printers use these and not the school paintbox's red-yellow-blue because each one controls exactly one channel of light — cyan swallows red, magenta swallows green, yellow swallows blue — so the three together can steer the reflected colour anywhere.

Honest limit: the paint bench runs on your screen, and a screen only emits light — so its "paint" is a simulation: the standard subtractive model, in which each pigment multiplies the light the others let through. Real pigments are messier than the model — they scatter as well as absorb, they dry darker, brands disagree — and none of them ever reaches a true black, which the bench honours: mix all three at full and you land in a deep warm murk, not at zero. That failure is real physics, and it is why printers cheat with a fourth, black cartridge.

02 / COLOUR CHORDS

Twelve hues on a circle. Chords included.

In Everyday Sound, twelve notes arranged by fifths close into a ring, and the distances around that ring are what chords are made of. Colour keeps the same geometry: twelve hues, thirty degrees apart, and a colour chord is a set of hues picked by their distances — the design world's name for the same move music makes. Pick a chord, then click any hue on the wheel to move the chord's root.

The geometry is the recipe

ANGLES, NOT TASTES

Complementary means 180° — straight across the wheel, the two hues that share nothing and sharpen each other. Triadic means 120° steps — an equilateral triangle of hues, bold and balanced; the painter's primaries, red yellow and blue, are one. Analogous hues sit side by side, 30° apart, and agree about everything. None of this is taste: it is positions on a circle, and the angles are the definitions — the same way a perfect fifth is not an opinion but 3:2. The full arithmetic of circles, angles, and ratios lives in Everyday Math; this wheel and Sound's circle of fifths are two of its exhibits.

Twelve points, two rooms: fifths in Sound, hues here.

03 / THE LENS BENCH

One lens, three rays, an upside-down world.

A convex lens bends light by a rule tidy enough to draw: rays through it meet where 1/f = 1/do + 1/di says they must — f the focal length, do the object's distance, di the image's. Three rays are enough to find the meeting point: one runs parallel then bends through the far focal point, one passes through the near focal point then runs parallel, and one goes straight through the centre, unbent. Slide and watch them agree.

Honest limit: this is the thin-lens idealisation — a lens with no thickness, no colour-splitting, no flaws. Real lenses bend different colours by slightly different amounts (chromatic aberration), blur at their edges (spherical aberration), and fight both with stacked elements: a phone camera is five to seven lenses in a trench coat, plus software that finishes the job the glass started. The idealisation is still the right first truth — every one of those corrections is a patch on this diagram, not a replacement for it.

Play it: Laser & Mirrors → — this bench bends rays through glass; the game bounces them off mirrors, by the rule that is exact both ways: angle in equals angle out. Rotate the mirrors, bounce the beam, light every target — seven puzzles, all reflection.

04 / THE SKY BENCH

Blue overhead, red at the edge of the day.

Air itself is a filter with a preference: its molecules scatter short wavelengths far more than long ones — the scattered share grows as 1/λ⁴, so blue light at 450 nm is scattered about 4.35 times as strongly as red at 650 nm. That one rule paints both ends of the day. Slide the sun down and watch the toll add up.

The sun's disc

The sky around it

Honest limit: the bench models the air as clean and the sun's colour from three sample wavelengths — a teaching model, not an atmosphere simulation. Real sunsets borrow extra colour from dust, smoke, and cloud edges, which is why no two are alike and why a smoky summer evening out-reds a clear one. The 1/λ⁴ rule underneath is exact; the swatches are its portrait, not its photograph. Everyday Earth takes the weather that rides on this sky.

05 / SHADOWS THROUGH THE DAY

A stick, a sun, and a clock you can read.

A shadow is the sun's position written on the ground: its length is the stick's height divided by the tangent of the sun's height, and it points exactly away from the sun. Pick a season, set the hour, and watch a metre stick's shadow sweep, stretch, and shrink — the same geometry Everyday Time's sundial bench squeezes into hour lines, and the same trick the Survival Guide's shadow stick uses to find north with two pebbles and patience.

Honest limit: the bench keeps solar time — noon is when the sun culminates — and uses the four season markers' exact declinations (±23.44° at the solstices, 0 at the equinoxes) rather than a date-by-date ephemeris. Your clock's noon differs from the sun's by the equation of time and your longitude, exactly as Everyday Time's sundial bench itemises; and near sunrise and sunset the model's shadow runs to the frame's edge and stops being drawn to scale, though its direction stays true.

06 / SEEING THE SPECTRUM

One narrow octave of light.

Everything you have ever seen arrived on wavelengths between about 380 and 750 nanometres — a band barely a factor of two wide, one octave, in a spectrum that runs from gamma rays to radio waves. Slide across the band; then read what lives just past its ends, where your eyes stop and other instruments — and other animals — keep going.

380 nm · violet edge750 nm · red edge

Just past red: infrared

LONGER THAN 750 NM

The heat channel. Your TV remote talks in it, a toaster glows into it before it glows visibly, and thermal cameras read the world by it — every warm thing, including you, shines in infrared whether any light is on or not. Pit vipers hunt by it with dedicated organs. It has no colour for us, not because it is dim but because no cell in your eye is built to answer it.

The remote control's whole vocabulary lives here.

Just past violet: ultraviolet

SHORTER THAN 380 NM

The sunburn channel — energetic enough to damage skin and eyes, which is why sunscreen and sunglasses are ultraviolet tools. Bees see into it, and many flowers wear ultraviolet patterns, invisible to us, that read as landing strips to them: the same flower is two different signs to two different visitors. Fluorescence is ultraviolet cashed in — absorbed UV re-emitted as visible light, which is all a blacklight poster is doing.

Open Everyday Body → — the eyes that draw our band's borders.

Honest limit: the swatch above is an approximation twice over. A single wavelength's colour is a pure spectral colour, and your screen cannot emit those — it mixes its three lamps to suggest them, the way the mixing bench upstairs fakes paint. And past the ends there is nothing to show at all: infrared and ultraviolet are not colours we are missing a name for, they are light our eyes return no answer to, so the bench labels them instead of painting them.

09 / HELP

Help — how Everyday Light works.

The manual for this workshop — what each part does, the simplifications it makes, and where its information comes from. This page and the full README work offline; the GitHub links need the internet.

The two mixing benches

Twins run by opposite rules. The light bench sums three lamps channel by channel (the stage's circles use the browser's own screen blend, so the overlaps are the same arithmetic); all three at full is white, and red over green is yellow. The paint bench runs the subtractive model: each pigment — cyan, magenta, yellow — is a reflectance profile that multiplies the light the others let through, with real pigments' leak built into the profiles, so all three at full lands at a warm near-black (about rgb(10, 8, 8)), not zero. That leak is why CMYK carries black, and the bench says so on its face.

The colour-chord wheel

Twelve hues at 30° steps on an SVG wheel. Four chord types are pure geometry: complementary (root + 6 steps, 180°), analogous (root ± 1), triadic (root + 4 + 8, 120° steps), split-complementary (root + 5 + 7, flanking the complement). Clicking a hue re-roots the chord; the palette below the wheel lists the chord's hues with their angles. The pairing with Everyday Sound's circle of fifths is deliberate and stated on the bench: twelve points on a circle, distances as the recipe, in both rooms.

The lens bench

A thin-lens ray diagram drawn from the equation 1/f = 1/do + 1/di with magnification m = −di/do. Sliders set focal length (5–20 cm) and object distance (12–45 cm); the three principal rays are drawn and meet at the computed image, inverted when real. Inside the focal length the bench switches honestly: no real image, dashed backward extensions, and the upright virtual image of the magnifying glass. Object at the focal point reports its image at infinity. Aberrations and multi-element lenses are named as the idealisation's limits on the bench.

The sky bench

Rayleigh scattering with the exponent where it belongs: scattered share ∝ 1/λ⁴, so 450 nm scatters (650/450)⁴ ≈ 4.35× more than 650 nm. A sun-height slider drives the air mass (≈1/sin(altitude), capped at 40); the sun's colour is a three-wavelength extinction model and the sky's colour is the scattered remainder, bluer overhead and paler at the horizon. It is a teaching model — clean air, three samples — and its Help and bench text say so; dust and smoke's extra colour is named as what the model leaves out.

The shadow bench

Solar position from the standard spherical formulas (latitude, declination, hour angle), with the four season markers at their exact declinations. Shadow length is height ÷ tan(altitude), direction exactly opposite the sun's azimuth, drawn on a compass rose with the sun's own position marked; at solar noon on an equinox it reduces to height × tan(latitude), the sundial identity, and the readout says so. Solar time is kept deliberately, with the clock difference handed to Everyday Time's itemisation rather than folded in.

The spectrum slider

Wavelength 380–750 nm with frequency from f = c/λ at the exact modern c — 400 THz at the red edge to 789 THz at the violet. The swatch is a hue-wheel approximation dimmed at the ends where the eye fades; the bench and Help both state that a screen suggests spectral colours by mixing its three lamps and cannot truly show them, and that infrared and ultraviolet are therefore labelled, never painted.

Honest limits

Every bench carries its limits on its face: screen-paint is a model of pigment, not pigment; full CMY leaks to near-black by design, as the real inks do; the lens is thin and flawless in a way no real lens is; the sky is three wavelengths and clean air; the shadows keep solar time and the solstices' exact declinations; the spectrum's colours are approximations and its ends are labels. Where this room simplifies, it says what it simplified.

Works offline?

Yes. The benches and this Help run entirely from the files in this folder, including inside the desktop app. Only the Links Out shelf needs the internet, because those pages live on other people's sites.
