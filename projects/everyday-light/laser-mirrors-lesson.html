The physics: one law runs the whole game

Every bounce in Laser & Mirrors obeys a single rule, the law of reflection. When a ray of light meets a surface, draw an imaginary line sticking straight out of the surface at the meeting point — that line is called the normal. Measure the incoming ray's angle from the normal (the angle of incidence) and the outgoing ray's angle from the normal (the angle of reflection). The law says they are equal, and both rays and the normal lie in one plane:

θi = θr  —  angle in equals angle out

Why a 45° mirror turns the beam exactly 90°

The game's mirrors lean at 45° to the beam's path. A beam running level meets a 45° mirror so that it arrives at 45° from the normal — θi = 45°. The law sends it away at θr = 45° on the other side of the normal. Incoming and outgoing directions therefore sit 45° + 45° = 90° apart. A 45° mirror is a perfect corner-maker: it turns a beam through exactly one right angle, no more, no less. Every turn in every level is that one fact, repeated.

Why a mirror shows your face and a wall doesn't

Both obey the same law. The difference is the surface. A mirror is smooth at the scale of light itself, so a bundle of parallel rays all meet it at the same angle and leave together, bundle intact — that's specular reflection, and it's why the bundle can carry an image. A wall is rough at that same tiny scale: each microscopic bump points a slightly different way, so each ray reflects by the law off its own little tilted facet and the bundle scatters in every direction — diffuse reflection. The wall is bright, but the image is shredded. Same law, different surface. The game's mirrors are perfectly specular, always; real mirrors are close, and the game's walls are blocks that simply stop the beam, which is a simplification named below.

Honest note — the glass: the pale glass blocks in the game let the beam pass straight through, untouched. Real glass bends light as it crosses the boundary — refraction — and that bending, not reflection, is what lenses are made of. The game leaves refraction out on purpose so reflection stands alone; the real story lives at the Light room's lens bench.

The maths: directions as pairs of numbers

On the game's grid, a beam's direction is a pair of numbers (dx, dy) — how many cells it steps across and down each move. East is (1, 0), south is (0, 1), west is (−1, 0), north is (0, −1). Reflection, in full vector form, flips the part of the ray that points into the surface and keeps the part that runs along it. For a mirror leaning at 45°, that flip-and-keep becomes a swap of the two numbers, with a sign change depending on which way the mirror leans:

“/” mirror: (dx, dy) → (−dy, −dx)   ·   “\” mirror: (dx, dy) → (dy, dx)

Work the east case by hand: an east beam, (1, 0), meets a “/” mirror and becomes (0, −1) — it leaves heading north. Here are all four arrivals, for both mirrors:

Beam arrives heading“/” mirror sends it“\” mirror sends it

SouthWestEast

Notice what the swap guarantees: a direction that starts axis-aligned — purely horizontal or purely vertical — stays axis-aligned forever. Swapping and negating (1, 0) can only ever produce (0, ±1) or (±1, 0). So after any number of bounces, the beam still travels at an exact right angle to its previous leg. That's why the game never needs diagonal beams: with mirrors only at 45°, right angles are the whole geometry — not a shortcut the game took, but a theorem it obeys.

Two levels, solved in words

Level 1 — First Bounce (the tutorial)

  1. The emitter on the left edge fires the beam east along its row. One mirror sits in the beam's path, five cells in; the target sits two cells above that mirror, in the same column.
  2. As the level starts, the mirror is set as “\”. Run the table: an east beam meeting “\” leaves south. The beam dutifully turns down the column and walks off the bottom edge of the board. Nothing is lit — the level starts unsolved, like all of them.
  3. Click the mirror once. It flips to “/”, and the same table now reads: east in, north out. The beam climbs the column, passes through the target two cells up, and keeps going to the top edge.
  4. Target lit, level complete — in a single move. Every later level is this same read-the-table move, chained: before you click, say out loud which way the beam will leave.

Level 6 — The Obvious Lie

  1. The target sits at the top of a column, four columns in from the emitter. The first mirror stands in the beam's row in that very column, and it starts set as “/” — the facing that turns the beam north, straight at the target. It looks solved before you've touched anything. That's the lie.
  2. Two solid blocks are stacked in that column between the mirror and the target. The beam turns north, meets the face of the lower block, and stops dead. The column is guarded; the target can only be reached along its own row, from the side. (The game's hint says exactly this — hints in this game are honest.)
  3. So the beam takes the long way round, and — the elegant part — the solution flips every mirror exactly once. The first mirror, clicked to “\”, turns the beam south, down the guarded column's safe side to the bottom row.
  4. A second mirror at the bottom, clicked to “\”, turns the southbound beam east along the bottom row to the board's right-hand column.
  5. A third mirror there, clicked to “/”, turns it north, up the full height of the right-hand column to the target's row.
  6. The last mirror, at the top of that column, clicked to “\”, turns the northbound beam west — and it runs along the top row and enters the target from the side, exactly as the hint promised. Four clicks, four right angles, one lie refused.

The collection's proof, stated plainly: all seven levels were verified solvable before this game shipped — not by playing them, but by running the game's own beam-tracing code on each level's intended solution and checking that every target lights, and by running the same code on each level's starting position and checking that none of them starts already solved. If a level ever fails that test, the game doesn't ship. The proof is only worth anything because the code being tested is the very code that draws your beam — which is the next section.

How it's built: the coding choices

One tracer, two jobs

The heart of the game is a single function, traceBeam(level, mirrorStates): hand it the level and the current mirror settings, and it returns the beam's path and the set of lit targets. It knows nothing about the screen — no drawing, no clicks, no colours. The game calls it every time you rotate a mirror and draws whatever it returns. And the solvability test calls the same function, in a plain script with no browser at all, feeding it each level's start and solution states. That's a deliberate design choice: because the proof runs the shipping code rather than a re-implementation, a level that passes the test cannot secretly differ from the level you play. When you separate the rules of a program from its display like this, the rules become something you can check — builders call the rules part a pure function, because its answer depends only on what you hand it.

Cell by cell, not angle by angle

The tracer doesn't do free-angle geometry. It steps the beam one cell at a time — east one cell, check what's there; north one cell, check again — the way you'd walk a finger along graph paper. A more "realistic" tracer would fire rays at arbitrary angles and solve intersections with trigonometry, and then spend its life fighting rounding errors: is the beam at 89.99999° or 90°? On this grid there is nothing to round. The maths section above shows why nothing is lost: with 45° mirrors, the true geometry only ever produces right angles anyway. The builder's lesson is to match the machinery to the problem — exactness beats realism when the problem is already exact.

The board is data; the beam is recomputed

Everything about a position — which mirrors exist, which way each one leans — lives in a small list of numbers, the state. A click changes one number in that list, and then the beam is traced again from scratch, from the emitter, as if for the first time. Nothing about the old beam is kept or patched. That costs a little computer effort and buys a lot of trust: there is no way for the displayed beam to drift away from the actual state, because it is re-derived from the state every single time. One more quiet guard lives in the tracer: it remembers every cell-and-direction it has already visited, and if the beam ever loops back on itself — possible in principle with mirrors facing each other — the trace stops instead of running forever. Programs that simulate should always carry a way to stop.

Simplifications, named

  • Two-sided mirrors. The game's mirrors reflect from both faces, whichever side the beam arrives on. Real mirror glass reflects from its silvered face; hit the back of a real mirror and you'd get a dim ghost through the glass, not a clean bounce.
  • Targets the beam passes through. A target lights up and lets the beam continue — that's what makes levels like Threading the Needle possible. A real sensor drinks the light that hits it; the beam would end there.
  • Glass without refraction. The glass blocks pass the beam straight through. Real glass bends light entering and leaving; the lens bench carries that story.
  • Grid-exact angles. Mirrors exist only at 45°, beams only at right angles. A mirror at 30° would turn a beam 60°, and the board would need free-angle geometry — a different, harder game, and a good one to imagine building next.
  • Walls that only stop. The solid blocks absorb the beam where it meets them. A real wall would scatter it diffusely in all directions (see the physics section) — visible as a lit wall, useless as a beam.
