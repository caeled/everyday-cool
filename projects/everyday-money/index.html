Everyday Money

The one system every kid and adult actually has to live inside — and the one nobody hands you a bench for. So here are four. What interest does to a card balance, and what the same interest does for a savings habit. Where a paycheck goes. And how to read a message that wants your money. Nothing here is advice about your money; it's the machinery, running where you can watch it.

Help ▾ — about this workshop

What this is. Four small benches about everyday money: a card balance paid down month by month, a savings habit compounding, a paycheck split three ways, and four messages to judge. The arithmetic is done live, in this page, by the plain method printed beside each bench.

Honest limits. The debt bench charges interest monthly at APR ÷ 12 and applies your payment after the interest — real cards differ in detail (daily balances, fees, penalty rates) but not in direction. The savings bench puts each deposit in at the end of the month. No bench here knows your life: these are teaching numbers, not financial advice, and a real decision deserves a real look at real terms.

Safety line. If a message is pressuring you to pay right now — especially by gift card, wire, or crypto — stop and check with the institution through a number you find yourself, never one in the message. That rule outranks every bench on this page.

Offline & privacy. Static page, works offline, stores nothing, sends nothing.

Bench 4 — Spot the scam

Four messages. For each one: does it hold up, or is it a scam? The tell is printed after you answer — the pattern is worth more than any single answer. Score: 0 / 4

"URGENT: Your bank account has been locked. Verify your identity within 2 hours or the account will be closed permanently. Click here and enter your full card number and PIN to restore access."

The tells: a deadline measured in hours, a link you're told to click, and a request for a PIN — a bank never asks for your PIN, and real lockouts don't evaporate if you call the number on your card instead. Urgency is the tool; slowing down is the counter.

"Congratulations! You've been selected to receive a $500 gift card. Just pay the $4.95 shipping fee with a prepaid card to claim your prize in the next 24 hours."

The tells: a prize you never entered for, a fee to claim it, and the fee demanded in the one payment form you can't reverse. A real prize never costs money to receive — the "fee" is the prize, and it's yours.

"Your statement is ready. Sign in the way you usually do to view it. If you didn't expect this message, call the number printed on the back of your card."

Why it holds up: no link to click, no deadline, no request for secrets — and it routes you to a number you can verify independently, on the card itself. Notice it still doesn't ask you to trust the message; it asks you to go around it. That's what the real ones sound like.

"Hi Grandma, it's me — I lost my phone and I'm in trouble. I need you to buy three $100 gift cards and read me the numbers. Please don't call my parents, I'll explain later. Love you!"

The tells: a new number, a crisis, gift cards, and — the loudest one — secrecy. Any request that needs you not to check is a request that can't survive checking. Call the family member on the number you already have. Every time.

The thread through all four: pressure, secrecy, and payment forms you can't undo. Slow down, go around the message, verify on a channel you chose. That's the whole defence — and it's the same move as Internet Detective's, pointed at your wallet.
