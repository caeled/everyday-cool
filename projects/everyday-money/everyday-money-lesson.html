The claim

Four benches whose arithmetic is done live by the plain method printed beside each one: a monthly loop for debt (interest at APR ÷ 12, charged first, then the payment), the same loop for savings (deposit at month's end), percentage arithmetic for the budget split, and four scam judgements with named tells.

What was checked

  • Both loops recomputed independently. The audit drove the shipped page's own sliders and compared its readouts against a separate implementation of the stated conventions: the debt defaults ($1,000 at 24% APR, $40/month) and the savings defaults ($100/month at 5% for 20 years) match to the dollar, as do the edge cases — a payment set below the first month's interest reports "never," and a 0% rate reduces both loops to plain addition.
  • The budget bench keeps its sum. At splits under, at, and over 100 the readout names the state and the unassigned or double-promised amount; the dollar figures are the income times each percentage, nothing cleverer.
  • The scam bench scores honestly. Each card's stored answer was checked against its printed tell; answering all four correctly scores 4 / 4, and a card locks after one answer so the score can't be farmed by clicking.

What bends

Real cards are daily, not monthly. Most card issuers charge interest on the average daily balance, add fees this bench ignores, and set minimum payments as a shrinking percentage — which makes real minimum-payment payoffs slower than Bench 1's fixed payment, never faster. The bench's direction is the truth; its months are a floor, not a quote.

The savings rate is a smooth fiction. Real returns wobble year to year; a flat rate is the teaching shape of compounding, not a prediction. The bench says "yearly rate," and the lesson says: the shape is real, the smoothness isn't.

50 / 30 / 20 is a starting point. Bench 3 prints it as a common split and immediately hands the sliders over; in a high-rent city needs can honestly run at 65%. The bench's job is to make the trade visible, not to grade a life.
