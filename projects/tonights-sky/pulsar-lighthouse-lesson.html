Pulsar Lighthouse — the lesson

A spinning neutron star with its magnetic axis tilted against its spin axis, sweeping two radio beams past Earth like a lighthouse. Six presets — Crab, Vela, a millisecond pulsar, an orthogonal rotator, a magnetar and an accreting binary — plus glitches, a dispersion waterfall and a folded pulse profile. This page is the audit: the shipped code was extracted and driven headlessly in Node, every preset was run through the page's own astrophysics engine, the beam geometry was swept phase by phase, glitches were triggered and measured, and every number below is what the code actually computed — not what its labels say it computed.

The verdict: the spin-down engine is real physics — three preset labels are not

The core engine is honest, and slightly better than the textbook formulas it is usually taught with. The page never asks for Ṗ. Instead it takes the spin frequency ν and the surface field B you set, computes the spin-down luminosity with the force-free magnetosphere formula (Spitkovsky 2006), and derives everything else from that:

Ė = B²R⁶Ω⁴ ⁄ (4c³) · (1 + sin²α)   ·   ν̇ = −Ė ⁄ (4π²Iν)   ·   τc = ν ⁄ (2|ν̇|) R = 11 km, I = 10⁴⁵ g·cm², α = magnetic tilt. Because ν̇ comes out of B, the characteristic age τc = P/(2Ṗ) and the dipole field B ≈ 3.2×10¹⁹√(P·Ṗ) G are not coded separately — the audit recovered Ṗ from the page's own ν̇ (Ṗ = −ν̇/ν²) and checked that the two textbook formulas reproduce the page's outputs. They do, to the last digit, for every non-binary preset.

The lighthouse geometry genuinely works. Flux at Earth is computed from the actual angle between the magnetic axis and the Earth line of sight at each rotation phase. When no beam can reach Earth, the measured flux is exactly zero at every phase — the page does not cheat a pulse in. Tilt, viewing angle and beam width change the duty cycle in the right direction and by sensible amounts (measured table below).

But three of the six presets wear real pulsars' names over numbers those pulsars do not have, the millisecond preset's dispersion measure is ten times too large, and a triggered glitch moves the star's frequency thousands of times more than its own banner announces. Each is flagged in place below and again in the bends.

Against the references (ATNF Pulsar Catalogue values, via the reference bank):

  • Crab — excellent. Real: P = 33 ms, Ṗ = 4.2×10⁻¹³, giving τ = 1,245 yr and B = 3.8×10¹² G; Ė ≈ 4.5×10³⁸ erg/s; DM = 56.8. The page lands on every one. The one honest caveat the page does not state: the Crab's true age is ~970 years (the supernova was seen in 1054), so the characteristic age overestimates it by about a quarter — τc assumes the star was born spinning much faster than it does now and has braked as a pure dipole ever since. That is a property of the formula, not a bug in the page, but a lesson page should say it, so this one does.
  • Vela — close, with a tilt tax. Real: P = 89.3 ms, Ṗ = 1.25×10⁻¹³, τ ≈ 11,300 yr, B ≈ 3.4×10¹² G. The page's B slider is right (3.39×10¹² G), but because its ν̇ comes from the force-free formula, the (1 + sin²α) factor at Vela's α = 53° inflates the implied Ṗ to 1.37×10⁻¹³, so the displayed age reads 10.37 kyr — about 8% young — and the standard-formula B recovered from that Ṗ (3.53×10¹² G) no longer exactly equals the B you set. The Crab, at α = 45°, happens to sit where the two conventions nearly coincide.
  • Millisecond pulsar — the spin physics is right. J0437−4715 really spins at 173.7 Hz, and recycled pulsars really do live at B ~ 10⁸–10⁹ G with characteristic ages of billions of years; the page's 5.8×10⁸ G and 1.82 Gyr are squarely in family (the catalogue Ṗ ≈ 5.7×10⁻²⁰ would give τ ≈ 1.6 Gyr). Its DM, however, is wrong — see the flags.
  • Light cylinder — exact. RLC = c/Ω is computed directly: Crab 1,580 km measured against 1,575 km from the reference period. At the millisecond preset the light cylinder sits at 275 km, only 25 neutron-star radii out — the visual squeeze is real physics, even though the drawn cylinder is log-compressed (see bends).

Lighthouse geometry, swept phase by phase

The audit set α (magnetic tilt), ζ (Earth's angle from the spin axis) and ρ (beam opening half-angle) directly, then sampled the page's evaluateFluxAtPhase() at 720 phases per rotation. Duty cycle is the fraction of the rotation with flux above 10% (and 50%) of the profile's own scale.

So the page's central claim — you only see a pulse when a beam sweeps Earth — is true in its own engine, and the sliders teach the real lesson: visibility is a three-way accident of tilt, viewing angle and beam width, which is why most neutron stars are never seen as pulsars at all. The interpulse case is also handled correctly: at α ≈ 85° the far pole comes within 9° of the sight-line, and the second peak appears half a rotation after the first, at 0.78× the amplitude in the page's beam model.

Glitches, triggered and measured

A real glitch is a sudden spin-up: the crust, braked by the magnetic field, lags the faster superfluid interior until it cracks and hands angular momentum back — Δν/ν of roughly 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁶ (Vela's giant glitches reach a few ×10⁻⁶; the Crab's are ~100× smaller), followed by a recovery over days to months, part of which never recovers. The audit triggered the page's glitch three times on the Vela preset:

#3+2.76×10⁻⁶11.370 → 11.461 Hz+8.0×10⁻³~2,900×

The sign is right (up, never down), the banner's scale is right (1–3.6×10⁻⁶, Vela-sized), and the recovery shape is a decaying exponential — the audit measured the page's glitch boost falling by exactly e^(−0.45t) — but the boost relaxes with a ~2-second time constant where real recoveries take days to months, and it relaxes only the audio/visual shimmer: the frequency step itself is permanent in the page, which is at least true to the permanent component of real glitches. The size of the step itself is the problem, and it is flagged as a false claim below — the code's own comment admits the jump was made visible on purpose.

Dispersion, timing, and what is not here

  • Dispersion sweep — right law, compressed scale. The waterfall delays each frequency channel by the cold-plasma law, Δt ∝ DM·(ν⁻² − νhi⁻²), low frequencies arriving last — the correct direction and shape. But the page maps even the largest DM to at most 0.24 of a rotation across its 0.8–1.8 GHz band. Measured against reality: for the Crab's DM = 56.8, the true sweep across that band is 4.15 ms × 56.8 × (0.8⁻² − 1.8⁻²) ≈ 296 ms — about nine whole Crab periods. Without dedispersion a real Crab pulse is smeared beyond recognition; on the page it leans by 5% of a turn. The lesson survives (that is why radio astronomers dedisperse), the scale does not.
  • The folded profile is half-measured, half-drawn. The profile curve blends the accumulated folded data with the analytic beam formula, 45/55 — so the green profile would look right even if no pulse had ever been accumulated. The strip chart above it is honestly sampled from the live flux.
  • No timing-precision claims are made — no time-of-arrival or microsecond-timing numbers appear anywhere in the page, so there is nothing false to flag there; equally, the exhibit titled "Radio Timing" never explains that millisecond pulsars are timed to ~100 ns and used as a galactic clock and gravitational-wave detector. An absence, not an error.
  • No death line, no P–Ṗ diagram. Neither appears in the page (the audit searched the shipped code: no death-line or diagram code exists), so there is no placement to verify. A pulsar pushed below the death line on this page would keep pulsing happily — the sliders let you build pulsars that in reality would have gone radio-quiet.
  • Rotation limits are a UI cap, not physics. The frequency slider runs 0.2–300 Hz. Nothing in the page mentions break-up (~0.5–1 ms periods, roughly 1,000–2,000 Hz for realistic equations of state) or the fastest known spinner, PSR J1748−2446ad at 716 Hz (1.4 ms). At the slider's own maximum, 300 Hz, the light cylinder is still 159 km out — 14 star-radii — so the page never gets near any limit it would have to warn about; but it also cannot reach the record-holders it names (see flags).

Where it bends

Flagged plainly — FALSE: the glitch banner and the glitch disagree. Triggering a glitch raises the star's frequency by exactly +0.8% (Δν/ν = +8.0×10⁻³, measured three times) while the banner simultaneously announces Δν/ν ≈ +1 to +3.6×10⁻⁶. The banner's number is the physically correct scale; the applied jump is roughly three thousand times larger — a real 0.8% glitch would be the largest spin change ever seen by orders of magnitude. The code comments say the jump was sized "so telemetry moves visibly": a deliberate bend, but the banner presents its small number as the measurement of the big one, and on a page about precision timing that contradiction is the one to fix first.

Flagged plainly — FALSE: the millisecond pulsar's DM is ten times too large. The J0437−4715 preset sets DM = 26.4 pc/cm³; the real value is ≈ 2.65 pc/cm³ — J0437 is famous precisely for being the nearest, lowest-DM millisecond pulsar. The Crab (56.8) and Vela (68) DMs are correct, which makes the factor-of-ten slip stand out.

Flagged plainly — FALSE: the "Interpulse Rotator" is labelled PSR B1937+21 but is not B1937+21. The preset runs at 2.35 Hz (P = 426 ms) with B = 1.1×10¹² G; PSR B1937+21 is the first millisecond pulsar ever found — 641.9 Hz, P = 1.56 ms, B ≈ 4×10⁸ G. Worse, the page's own slider caps at 300 Hz, so B1937+21 cannot be dialled in even by hand. As an anonymous orthogonal rotator the preset is a fine geometry demo (α = 85°, both poles measured above); wearing that name, it teaches the opposite of the pulsar's significance.

Flagged plainly — FALSE: the magnetar preset's spin and age. SGR 1806−20 rotates at 0.13 Hz (P = 7.56 s), not the preset's 0.65 Hz, and the page's engine consequently displays a characteristic age of 28 years — younger than the radio telescopes that would have had to watch it be born. Real magnetar characteristic ages are hundreds to thousands of years. The field (1.3×10¹⁵ G) is in the right range; the spin is ~5× too fast, and because τc and Ė are derived from ν and B, the error propagates into every other readout on the card. The frequency slider's 0.2 Hz floor also makes the true 0.13 Hz unreachable by hand.

Flagged plainly — the binary preset starts 29× too slow. SAX J1808.4−3658 spins at 401 Hz; the preset starts it at 14 Hz and then spins it up at a plainly theatrical rate (the simulation's spin-up would cross the whole millisecond range in minutes of viewing; the real recycling took ~10⁸ years of accretion). The direction is right — accretion torque really does spin a dead pulsar back up, the page's ν̇ correctly flips sign to +3.5×10⁻¹¹ Hz/s, and the age readout honestly switches to "Spin-Up" — but neither the starting point nor the rate is the named system's. Its Ė readout also still shows the dipole formula's 4.1×10³⁰ erg/s, which is not where an accreting system's luminosity comes from.

The simulation clock is not real time, in either direction. Visual rotation is log-compressed (everything above 1.8 Hz is squashed into a narrow readable band), and the secular spin-down is accelerated by ~15 orders of magnitude — the audit watched Vela shed 0.08 Hz in five seconds of simulation, where the real star takes ~10⁵ years to do that. Both are necessary to see anything move at all; neither is labelled on the page.

The drawn light cylinder is log-compressed. The telemetry's RLC is exact, but the drawn cylinder's radius is mapped from log ν into a fixed 88–245-unit band while the star is drawn 36 units in radius — so on screen the Crab's cylinder looks ~5 star-radii out where the true ratio is 144:1. A picture convention, disclosed here rather than there.

Small asymmetries of convenience. The south beam is hard-wired to 0.78× the north beam's amplitude, the north beam gets a two-component core-plus-cone profile while the south gets a single Gaussian, the interpulse is placed at exactly half a rotation in the audio, and ζ is only offered from 0–90° (the mirror geometries beyond 90° are unreachable). None of these is wrong as a model choice; all of them are choices, and real pulsars are messier.

One stale static number. The page's hard-coded opening telemetry reads the Crab's age as 1.26 kyr; the engine's own computed value, which replaces it at boot, is 1.23 kyr. Harmless, and exactly the kind of thing only a headless drive notices.
