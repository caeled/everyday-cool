Exoplanet Transit — the lesson

A transit and radial-velocity observatory: drag the sliders and a planet crosses its star, the light curve dips, the spectrum slides red and blue. This page is the audit of the physics underneath. The shipped code was extracted and driven headlessly — planet and star parameters were set through the page's own parameter system, light curves and RV curves were generated from the shipped integrator, and every headline number was measured off those curves and checked against the standard formulas. The core engine is genuinely good — in several places better than the page's own words about it. The bends are mostly in the summaries, the telemetry shortcuts, and one advertised feature that, as shipped, can never fire.

The verdict: a real integrator wearing a few wrong labels

The flux engine is exact where it matters. The page does not fake its light curve with a trapezoid. It solves Kepler's equation by Newton–Raphson, places the planet in 3D, and integrates the blocked light over a 64-point Vogel disk quadrature with quadratic limb darkening. Measured off the generated curve, a uniform-disk transit depth equals (Rp/R★)² to four decimal places at every size tested, the RV semi-amplitude equals the standard formula to the last digit, the period folds back out of the light curve exactly, and the duration follows the correct period, stellar-radius and eccentricity scalings. Multi-planet flux and RV superpose exactly.

But three of the preset summaries state numbers the page itself does not produce — most sharply the Eclipsing Binary summary (primary depth, secondary depth and RV swing are all off, §7) — and the "Spot Crossing!" detection the interface advertises is geometrically impossible in the default preset (§6). Those are flagged prominently below, with the measured values beside the claimed ones.

1 · Transit depth: (Rp/R★)², measured

depth = (Rp / R★)² A planet blocks light in proportion to the area of its disk against the star's. Jupiter against the Sun: (0.100)² ≈ 1%. Earth against the Sun: (0.00917)² ≈ 0.0084% = 84 ppm.

Starspots off, central transit (i = 90°, b = 0), depth read off the generated light curve at mid-transit:

Uniform disk: exact. A true Jupiter (k = 0.10045, P = 4,332.6 d) measures 1.0090% against the formula's 1.0090%; a true Earth measures 84.1 ppm against 84 ppm. Limb darkening: also correct physics — a real star is brighter at disk centre than at the limb, so a central transit blocks disproportionately bright light and dips deeper than k²: a factor 1.229 at b = 0 for the Sun's coefficients (u₁ = 0.44, u₂ = 0.24), shrinking as the chord moves toward the limb. That is why the presets' measured depths below are all larger than their k² values — and why the preset summaries, which quote the k² numbers, disagree with the instrument they sit next to (see bends).

Anyone reading the telemetry's "K" as "the star never moves faster than this" will be wrong by 57% on the eccentric preset — the page's own summary does call that curve a sawtooth, which is exactly right.

3 · Period recovery: fold the curve, get the period back

Light curves were generated over 3.2 orbits (starspots and noise off), dips were located by a half-depth threshold, and the intervals between successive dip centres were measured:

(The first interval in each run is slightly short — an artefact of the audit's own window opening mid-transit at t = 0, not of the page. Every complete interval is exact to the sampling step.) This is the Kepler mission's whole trick, and the page performs it honestly: three dips at equal spacing are the period.

4 · Transit duration: the scalings all check out

T₁₄ ∝ P1/3 · R★ · √(1−e²) / (1 + e sin ω) · √( (1+k)² − b² ) Duration grows only as the cube root of period (a wider orbit is bigger, but the planet also moves slower), grows in direct proportion to the star's radius, shrinks as the chord moves off-centre (b → 1), and shortens or lengthens with eccentricity depending on whether transit happens near periastron or apastron.

5 · Inclination and the grazing transit

Impact parameter b = (a/R★) cos i. Depth stays full while the whole disk is on the star (b < 1−k), falls through the grazing band (1−k < b < 1+k), and vanishes beyond it. Sweeping inclination on the grazing preset's star and planet (k = 0.145, P = 5.2 d):

The light curve itself behaves beautifully: depth erodes smoothly (limb darkening starts the erosion before the geometric grazing band, correctly), duration collapses, and the dip goes V-shaped. The grazing preset's own measured depth is 1.02% at b = 0.966 — the summary's "b ≈ 0.99" is close but not exact. The bend is in the telemetry number, not the curve: the HUD's depth is computed by a shortcut that approximates the partial-overlap area as linear in (1+k−b)/(2k), where the true circle–circle overlap is curved. Inside the grazing band the HUD and the page's own light curve disagree by up to ~15% (0.87% vs 1.02% on the grazing preset itself). Outside the band they agree to ~0.5%. Trust the gold curve; the number above it is an estimate that degrades exactly where grazing gets interesting.

6 · Starspots: real modulation, one dead feature

The spots are six fixed spots (fixed latitudes, longitudes, sizes, contrasts) that rotate with the star; the "activity" slider scales how many are active, how big they are, and how dark. Measured out-of-transit flux variation over one rotation (Sun-like star, 25.4 d rotation):

The mechanism is honest — spots really do modulate a light curve at the rotation period, and a planet crossing a spot really does produce a bright bump inside the dip (the audit produced bumps up to 15,459 ppm with a large planet at full activity, and 7,326 ppm for the Hot Jupiter itself at 100% activity). But the scale is theatrical: at the default 45% the spot modulation (0.93%) is more than half the Hot Jupiter's transit depth, and real Sun-like spot modulation is typically a tenth of that. The slider percent is not a surface-coverage percent, and the spots never grow, decay or drift in latitude — they are a fixed pattern on a carousel.

Flagged plainly — the "Spot Crossing!" feature cannot fire in the default preset. The status pill, the rose highlighting on the light curve and the audio shimmer all wait for a spot-crossing bump above 80 ppm. At the Hot Jupiter preset's shipped 45% activity, only 3 of the 6 spots are switched on, and none of their latitude lanes (y = −0.14, +0.22, −0.37) intersects the transit chord at y = +0.51: the closest a spot ever gets to the planet's centre during a transit is 0.286 R★, against the 0.225 R★ needed to touch. The audit scanned 30 consecutive transits: zero bumps. The feature only wakes up at 100% activity, when the sixth spot's lane (y = +0.43) switches on. The code is correct; the shipped geometry never lets it speak.

7 · Multi-planet systems and the eclipsing binary

Superposition is exact. For a constructed two-planet double transit, the joint depth measured 1.59916% against 1.22815% + 0.37101% for the planets taken one at a time — a difference of 0.0 ppm (the integrator also correctly avoids double-counting where two planet disks overlap on the star). TRAPPIST's RV at a test epoch: joint 1.8026 m/s, sum of singles 1.8026 m/s. The TRAPPIST preset's periods (1.51, 2.42, 4.05 d) are the real b, c, d periods — but double transits are genuinely rare: the audit's first found one at t = 48.3 d, so the summary's "generating overlapping double transits" is true on a timescale of weeks of watching, not minutes. Its star is also oversized against the real TRAPPIST-1 (page: 0.16 R☉, 0.12 M☉; published: ≈0.12 R☉, ≈0.09 M☉), which inflates its planets' depths and durations somewhat. The Hot Jupiter's secondary occultation measures exactly the 180 ppm the code intends — but note that 180 ppm is a hard-coded constant applied to any planet with Teq > 1000 K and k > 0.06, not a value computed from the planet's temperature or size; the Earth-twin correctly gets none.

FALSE claims flagged — the Eclipsing Binary preset summary. The summary promises "deep ~21% primary eclipses," "shallower ~8.5% secondary eclipses" and a "72 km/s RV swing." Measured off the page's own generated curves (starspots off): primary depth 25.4% (not ~21%), secondary depth 10.3% (not ~8.5%), and RV semi-amplitude 64.0 km/s (not 72 km/s). The secondary figure is at least derivable — L₂/L₁ = 0.46 × 0.5² = 0.115, diluted to 0.115/1.115 = 10.3% — so the summary's 8.5% matches nothing the page computes. The binary's telemetry depth (26.3%) also disagrees with its own light curve (25.4%) by the grazing-shortcut error of §5, and the binary's HUD density is a hard-coded "1.85 g/cm³" rather than a computed value.

Where it bends

The preset summaries quote uniform-disk depths; the instrument shows limb-darkened ones. Hot Jupiter summary: "1.46% transit" — the page's own light curve and telemetry measure 1.66%. Earth-twin summary: "84 ppm" — the page measures 100 ppm. Both summaries are the textbook k² numbers and both are honest physics for a uniform star; but the observatory next to them integrates limb darkening, so a student comparing summary to screen will find a 14–19% mismatch with no explanation offered. The limb-darkened values are the more correct ones.

Telemetry depth is a shortcut in the grazing band — linear overlap instead of the true circle–circle area, up to ~15% off the page's own curve (§5). Everywhere else it tracks the curve to ~0.5%.

Spot Crossing detection is dead at default settings (§6): correct code, impossible geometry — no active spot lane crosses the Hot Jupiter's chord below 100% activity.

Spot noise is scaled for drama. 45% "activity" yields 0.93% rotational modulation; the spots are a fixed six-spot pattern with no evolution, and at 100% the star sits permanently 2–3% dim — a very spotted star indeed.

The picture compresses distance, and only half of that is disclosed. The sky view is exact across the stellar disk but logarithmically compresses everything beyond 1.15 R★ so a 215 R★ Earth orbit fits the screen — a reasonable display choice the page never mentions. The barycentre inset's wobble amplification (up to ×250,000 for an Earth) is disclosed, by a badge on the inset itself — the right way to do it.

Small stale numbers. The static HUD printed in the HTML before the script runs (depth 1.46%, Teq 1,448 K, duration 3.08 hr) is replaced live by the computed values (1.67%, 1,345 K, 3.07 hr) — the computed equilibrium temperature runs ~100 K cooler than HD 209458 b's commonly quoted ~1,450 K, a consequence of the page's fixed albedo factor. The grazing summary's b ≈ 0.99 measures 0.966. The eccentric giant is classified "Cold Gas Giant" by the page's own classifier despite its dramatic periastron heating — a label, not a measurement.

Photon noise is honest but only skin-deep. The cyan scatter is Gaussian noise of exactly the dialled σ added to the clean curve — at the Earth-twin's 14 ppm setting against its 100 ppm dip, you can watch a real detection problem. But the noise is white only: no instrumental drift, no correlated ("red") noise, which is what actually eats Earth-size transits in real Kepler data.
