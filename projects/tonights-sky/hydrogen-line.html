What you are looking at

Five benches about one faint radio note: the 21 cm hydrogen line at f₀ = 1420.405751768 MHz. Every number on the page comes from one small core (marked HI-CORE in the page source). Constants that the SI fixes exactly (c, h, k) are used exactly. Speeds are km/s, distances kpc (1 kpc = 3,262 light-years), temperatures kelvin.

The Doppler sign

We use the radio convention: v = c·(f₀ − f)/f₀. A positive velocity means the gas is moving away and is heard below 1420.406 MHz; negative means approaching, heard above. Optical and relativistic definitions differ by under 0.1% at Galactic speeds — bench 2 shows all three.

The pretend Galaxy

A face-on toy Milky Way: the Sun R₀ = 8.2 kpc from the centre, moving at V₀ = 230 km/s, a flat rotation curve (every orbit at 230 km/s, with a small solid-body core inside 0.8 kpc) — a deliberate simplification — and hydrogen in a smooth disc with four drawn-on spiral arms. Gas has 7 km/s of random motion. The spectra are simulated, not recorded: real ones come with a sloping baseline, radio interference and gain drift, which the plan's software has to clean up.

Hear the Hydrogen Line — the 21 cm song of the Milky Way

Most of the Galaxy's ordinary matter that isn't in stars is cold hydrogen gas, and it is too dark to see. But every hydrogen atom can hum one radio note, and a dish in a backyard can hear the whole Milky Way humming it. The note's pitch slides with the gas's speed — and that slide is how astronomers learned the Galaxy spins, and that it spins too fast for what we can see. Five benches: the spin flip, the Doppler tuner, a pretend dish you can sweep along the sky, the tangent-point method that turns spectra into a rotation curve, and an honest look at what a backyard dish can hear.

1 · The spin flip — where the note comes from

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron, and both spin like tiny magnets. When the two spins point the same way the atom has a hair more energy than when they point opposite ways. Left alone, an atom in the higher state will eventually flip the electron's spin over and pay the difference out as one radio photon — always the same size, so always the same note: 1420.405751768 MHz, a wavelength of about 21.1 cm.

The catch: one atom waits on average about 11 million years before it flips by itself. We hear it anyway because there is so much hydrogen — a modest cloud of a hundred Suns' worth of hydrogen holds about 10⁵⁹ atoms, so even at one flip per atom per 11 million years it sends out roughly 10⁴⁴ photons every second. Collisions keep re-filling the upper state, so the sky never runs out.

2 · The Doppler tuner — speed changes the pitch

A siren sounds lower as it drives away. Radio does the same: gas moving away is heard at a lower frequency, gas moving toward us at a higher one. Slide the gas's speed.

v = c · (f₀ − f) / f₀   ⇔   f = f₀ · (1 − v/c)    (radio convention; v > 0 = moving away)
Same frequency, three definitions of velocityVelocity

Radio astronomers use the radio definition by convention; optical astronomers divide by f instead of f₀. At Galactic speeds the three agree to better than 0.1% — but when you compare your numbers with a survey, check which one it used.

Each dot on the line of sight is coloured by its speed toward (blue) or away from (red) us. Right: the spectrum your receiver would record — every cloud on the line adds its own little peak at its own Doppler shift.



The Earth orbits the Sun at 30 km/s and the Sun drifts about 20 km/s against its neighbours, so a raw spectrum is shifted until you apply the LSR correction (Local Standard of Rest). Leave this at 0 for a corrected spectrum.

4 · The tangent-point method — build the rotation curve

Look into the inner Galaxy (0° < l < 90°) and your line of sight passes closest to the centre at one spot: the tangent point, at distance R₀·cos l from us and radius R = R₀·sin l from the centre. There the gas's whole orbital motion points straight along your line of sight, so it is the fastest-receding gas on the line — the far edge of the spectrum. Read that edge, add back the Sun's own share, and you have the Galaxy's speed at that radius:

v_t = V(R₀ sin l) − V₀ sin l  ⇒  V(R) = v_t + V₀ sin l,  R = R₀ sin l

R₀ = 8.2 kpc (the Sun's distance from the centre) and V₀ = 230 km/s (the Sun's orbital speed).

What to notice: with the flat model the points line up near 230 km/s — a few km/s high, because the gas's own random motion smears the edge outward. With the Kepler model they fall away with distance like the planets do around the Sun. The real Milky Way, and almost every spiral galaxy measured this way, looks flat: the outer gas moves too fast for the mass we can see. That mismatch is one of the main clues to dark matter. Try the backyard dish: its wide beam and the thin gas at the far tangent points make the low-l points scatter — real, and why big surveys use big dishes. Then set a leftover motion in bench 3 and watch every point shift: that is why the LSR correction matters.

5 · What a backyard dish can really hear

Two numbers decide everything: how much the source warms your receiver (its antenna temperature) and how much the receiver's own hiss wobbles. The wobble shrinks with listening time — the radiometer equation:

ΔT = T_sys / √(B · τ)     T_A = η · A · S / (2k)     beam ≈ 70° · λ / D

    Honest caveats

    • The real fight is the baseline, not the hiss. On a real night the receiver's response across the band is sloped and lumpy, and it drifts with temperature. The plan's bandpass calibration (a dummy-load or off-plane reference) and software such as ezRA and VIRGO remove that shape; this page draws a perfectly flat baseline.
    • Interference is real. 1420 MHz is a protected band, but strong neighbours can leak in; the plan's filtered amplifier is there for that.
    • Correct to the LSR. Raw spectra carry the Earth's orbit (±30 km/s through the year), its spin (up to 0.46 km/s) and the Sun's own drift. VIRGO and ezRA apply the Local Standard of Rest correction for you — do it before comparing with any survey.
    • Continuum sources are a different game. The Sun and Cassiopeia A add broadband warmth, not a line, so receiver gain drift — not the radiometer equation — limits what a small dish can see. That is why the plan saves Cas A for Stage 4.
    • The spectra here are simulated from a toy Galaxy; their shapes are realistic in kind, not a prediction of what your dish will record on a given night.

    Hear it for real

    Everything above is a simulation, and the page says so. The real line is public, and you do not need a dish of your own to hear it:

    • PICTOR — book a real observation. A free, open-source radio telescope in Greece — a dish, a 1420 MHz feed and an RTL-SDR receiver: the plan's own receiver chain, grown up. Anyone can book an observation at 1420.405 MHz, and the real spectrum from the real sky is emailed back. Its example observations show the same spiral-arm peaks Bench 3 simulates.
    • A survey that never sleeps. A station on the US East Coast running a SAWbird+ H1 into an RTL-SDR — the plan's Stage 1 hardware — recording a spectrum around the line every second, around the clock, published openly for anyone to download, plot or sonify.
    • HI4PI at NASA LAMBDA. The definitive all-sky hydrogen survey (Effelsberg + Parkes) — the archive the professionals compare against.

    Predicted in hiding, heard through a window

    In 1944, in the occupied Netherlands, a student named Hendrik van de Hulst worked out that cold hydrogen should give off a radio line near 21 cm, and that it might be strong enough to detect. Nobody could try until after the war. On 25 March 1951, Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell at Harvard caught it first, with a horn antenna poking out of a laboratory window. Dutch and Australian teams confirmed it within weeks, and the three papers were published together.

    The curve that wouldn't fall

    If most of a galaxy's mass sat in its bright middle, outer gas should orbit slowly, the way Neptune crawls compared with Mercury. In the 1970s, hydrogen-line rotation curves of nearby spiral galaxies — reaching far beyond their visible stars — together with Vera Rubin's optical measurements, showed they stay flat instead. Something unseen holds the outer gas in. Bench 4 lets you see the difference between the two curves for yourself.
