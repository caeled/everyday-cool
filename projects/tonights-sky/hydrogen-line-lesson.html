The Hear the Hydrogen Line Lesson — the physics behind the exhibit

The back door to Hear the Hydrogen Line — the 21 cm song of the Milky Way, in Tonight's Sky. The exhibit is the front door: you flip a spin, slide a Doppler shift, sweep a pretend dish and build a rotation curve. This page is the same lesson with the sums showing: where the 21 cm line comes from, the Doppler formula and its sign, the geometry of the tangent point, what a flat rotation curve means, the radiometer equation, what a backyard dish can really hear, and the numbers the exhibit's own proofs produced.

The exhibit in one paragraph

Five benches about one radio note. A hydrogen atom's electron flips its spin and sends out a photon at 1420.405751768 MHz. A Doppler tuner shows how a cloud's speed slides that note up or down, with the sign stated: positive means moving away and heard lower. A pretend dish sweeps along the Milky Way in a face-on toy Galaxy; each cloud on the line of sight adds its own peak at its own speed, and the spectrum is drawn as a 0.8 m backyard dish, a 3 m club dish, a 25 m dish or a perfect pencil beam would record it, noise included. The tangent-point method reads the far edge of each spectrum and turns it into the Galaxy's rotation speed, one point per pointing, against a flat curve and a Kepler curve. And a calculator shows, with the radiometer equation, what a backyard dish can and can't hear — the hydrogen line, the Sun, Cassiopeia A.

The physics

The spin flip

The proton and the electron in a hydrogen atom each carry spin and a magnetic moment. In the ground state the two can line up parallel (total spin F = 1) or antiparallel (F = 0); the parallel arrangement sits higher by a tiny energy, ΔE = h·f₀ ≈ 5.87 micro-electronvolts. Divide by Boltzmann's constant and that is only 0.068 K — so even in cold interstellar gas, collisions keep about three atoms in four in the upper state. The flip down is "forbidden" in the technical sense: its Einstein coefficient is A₁₀ ≈ 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁵ per second, so one atom waits about 11 million years on average. The Galaxy has so many atoms that the line is still one of the brightest things in the radio sky.

f₀ = 1420.405751768 MHz   λ₀ = c/f₀ = 21.106 cm   E = h f₀ = 5.874 µeV   h f₀/k = 0.06817 K

The Doppler shift, and which way the sign goes

Radio astronomers define velocity from frequency by the radio convention:

v = c · (f₀ − f) / f₀    ⇔    f = f₀ · (1 − v/c)

Positive v means the gas is receding and the line is heard below f₀ (redshift); negative means approaching and above f₀. One megahertz of shift is 211.06 km/s; one kilohertz is 0.211 km/s. The optical convention divides by f instead, and the exact relativistic formula sits between the two. At 200 km/s they differ by 0.07 and 0.13 km/s — under 0.1% — but surveys label which one they used, and so should you.

Looking through a spinning disc

Put the Galactic centre at the origin and the Sun at distance R₀. A cloud at distance d along galactic longitude l (in the plane, b = 0) sits at radius

R = √(R₀² + d² − 2 R₀ d cos l)

If every cloud moves on a circle at speed V(R), the part of its motion along our line of sight, minus the Sun's own share, is

v_r = ( V(R) · R₀/R − V₀ ) · sin l

For a flat curve V(R) = V₀ this grows as R shrinks, so the fastest gas on a line of sight into the inner Galaxy is the gas closest to the centre — the tangent point, at d = R₀ cos l and R = R₀ sin l, where the circle just touches the line of sight. There the whole orbital velocity points along the line, and

v_t = V(R₀ sin l) − V₀ sin l    ⇒    V(R₀ sin l) = v_t + V₀ sin l

Read the extreme velocity at many longitudes and you get the rotation curve of the inner Galaxy, one radius per pointing. For the flat toy curve, v_t = V₀(1 − sin l): 115 km/s at l = 30°. Gas exactly on the Sun's circle (d = 2R₀ cos l) always shows zero velocity, and in the outer Galaxy (90° < l < 270°) there is no tangent point at all.

Flat curves and dark matter

If nearly all of a galaxy's mass sat in its bright centre, orbital speeds would fall as 1/√R, like the planets around the Sun. Hydrogen-line rotation curves of the Milky Way and of nearby spirals, which reach well beyond the visible stars, stay roughly flat instead. The extra pull is attributed to dark matter. The exhibit's "Kepler" button shows the falling curve so the contrast is plain; the real Milky Way's curve is close to flat but not exactly so.

Why a spectrum and not one number

Every cloud on a line of sight adds a small Gaussian peak at its own velocity, widened by its thermal and turbulent motions (7 km/s in the exhibit). In the optically thin limit the area under the spectrum measures the hydrogen column: N_H = 1.8224 × 10¹⁸ ∫T_B dv (cm⁻², with T_B in kelvin and v in km/s). The exhibit lays gas down, converts it with that factor, and caps the brightness with a 150 K spin temperature, T_B = T_s(1 − e^(−τ)), so the densest lines saturate the way real ones do.

The radiometer equation and the backyard

A receiver's own hiss is described by its system temperature T_sys (amplifier, ground spilling into the feed, the sky). Average a channel of width B for a time τ and the hiss wobbles by

ΔT = T_sys / √(B · τ)

A 2.5 km/s channel is 11.84 kHz; with T_sys = 120 K and one minute of listening, ΔT ≈ 0.14 K. A source of flux density S warms a dish of diameter D and efficiency η by T_A = η · (πD²/4) · S / (2k), and the dish's beam is about 70° · λ/D wide: 18.5° for a 0.8 m dish, 0.59° for a 25 m one. The plan's Rev 2 quotes 18° × 25° for the real offset dish, which is not round.

What the backyard dish can really do

This is the bridge to The Backyard Radio Telescope Plan — Rev 2.

  • The Milky Way's hydrogen line — yes. Nearby gas fills even a wide beam; the exhibit's 0.8 m dish sees a peak of roughly 10–30 K of antenna temperature along the plane, many times the hiss after a minute. The plan's first light is exactly this.
  • The tangent-point edge — with care. Distant gas at the tangent points lies in a layer only a few hundred parsecs thick, which fills a small slice of an 18° beam, so the far edge is fainter than the main peak. The wide beam also mixes longitudes. In the exhibit the backyard dish reads clear edges from about l = 30° up and misses the low-longitude points; a 3 m dish gets them all. That is honest, not a flaw.
  • The Sun — yes. Tens of solar flux units at 1.4 GHz (the exhibit assumes 50 sfu = 500,000 Jy; it varies with the solar cycle) make a big continuum rise in a drift scan.
  • Cassiopeia A — not reliably. About 1,700 Jy at 1.4 GHz today (it has faded about 0.67% a year for decades), it warms a 0.8 m dish by about 0.2 K, roughly 0.15% of T_sys — below a typical receiver's gain drift. The plan's Rev 2 reaches the same verdict and saves it for Stage 4. (The plan originally quoted the 1960s-epoch ~2,400 Jy; its page was corrected to ~1,700 Jy on 25 November 2026 — the conclusion is the same either way.)
  • The real fight is the baseline. A real band is sloped and lumpy and drifts with temperature; the plan's bandpass calibration and software (ezRA, VIRGO) remove that shape. Interference has to be avoided or filtered.
  • Correct to the Local Standard of Rest. A raw spectrum carries the Earth's orbital motion (up to ±30 km/s through the year, about ±142 kHz), the Earth's spin (up to 0.46 km/s) and the Sun's own motion relative to its neighbours (about 20 km/s in the classical "standard solar motion"; a modern value is U, V, W = 11.1, 12.24, 7.25 km/s). VIRGO and ezRA apply the LSR correction; the exhibit's "leftover motion" slider shows what happens if you forget.

What the proofs don't claim: the toy Galaxy is not the real Milky Way, so its spectra will not match a survey line for line. The half-height edge reads a few km/s high (about +5 km/s with the full toy, spiral arms and 7 km/s gas motion included) because random gas motion smears the edge outward; real tangent-point studies correct for that. With noise and a wide beam the scatter grows — that is the point of bench 4.

Two stories

Predicted in hiding, heard through a window

Hendrik van de Hulst predicted the line in 1944, in the occupied Netherlands, at a colloquium organised by Jan Oort. On 25 March 1951 Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell at Harvard detected it with a horn antenna aimed out of a laboratory window. Groups in the Netherlands (Muller and Oort) and Australia (Christiansen and Hindman) confirmed it within weeks, and the reports appeared together in Nature that September. Within a few years the line had mapped the Milky Way's spiral arms — something light could never do through the dust.

The curve that wouldn't fall

Through the 1970s, hydrogen-line rotation curves of nearby spirals (for example Roberts & Whitehurst 1975 for Andromeda, and Bosma's 1978 thesis) extended far past the visible discs and stayed flat, alongside Vera Rubin and Kent Ford's optical measurements of the inner parts. Together they made the case that galaxies hold far more mass than they show.

How the exhibit is built

One HTML file with no external requests: the banner is a local image drawn for the page with code (an SVG scene of a dish, the Milky Way and a spectrum, rendered to WebP), and everything else is canvas. A single core (HI-CORE-BEGIN … HI-CORE-END) holds the constants, the Doppler formulas, the toy Galaxy (gas density, rotation, lines of sight), the spectrum builder (Gaussian clouds → column density → brightness with a spin-temperature cap → Gaussian beam average in longitude with latitude dilution), the radiometer noise (a seeded generator, so every pointing repeats exactly), the half-height edge reader, and runProofs(). The interface only calls the core. Nothing moves until you press a button; with reduced motion the spin flip, the sweep and the rotation-curve build jump to their final state. Pages keep the browser's zoom.

The merge pass — one defect fixed, one door added

The independent audit (Muse, 25 November 2026) passed every bench's physics and all 57 automated checks, and found one real defect: Bench 2's Doppler slider was never wired — dopUpdate() ran once at load and no listener existed, so the tuner could not be tuned. At Steven's direction ("Fix the Doppler slider in the merge pass"), Muse applied the one-line fix during the merge of PR #11: $("dopV").addEventListener("input", dopUpdate); — verified after the fix by driving the shipped page: the readout now follows the slider at −100, 0 and +250 km/s, with zero exceptions.
