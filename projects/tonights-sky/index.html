    03 / ASK

    Why do the stars move?

    They don't, really, not over one night. You do. The Earth spins once a day, so the whole sky seems to wheel around us: stars rise in the east, swing across the south (if you live north of the equator), and set in the west.

    The turning has a centre: the point in the sky right above the Earth's North Pole. Polaris, the North Star, sits less than one degree from it, which is why it hardly moves.

    The stars also slowly shift with the seasons: each night they rise about 4 minutes earlier, because the Earth is also moving along its orbit around the Sun.

    What you should notice

    Around Polaris the trails are circles. In the east the stars rise at a slant; on the equator they rise straight up and Polaris sits on the horizon. In Sydney you cannot see Polaris at all: the southern sky turns around an empty spot near the Southern Cross.

    04 / NOTICE

    The planets all use one road

    The planets, the Moon and the Sun always appear along one line around the sky, called the ecliptic. That is because the planets orbit the Sun in nearly the same flat plane, like marbles rolling on one big table, and we are on that table too.

    The constellations along that road are the zodiac. The Sun appears to walk through them once a year, about one degree a day, because the Earth goes around it.

    Planets wander against the stars from night to night: "planet" comes from the Greek word for wanderer. Mars and the outer planets sometimes seem to go backwards for a few weeks when the Earth overtakes them.

    05 / UNDERSTAND

    Why the Moon changes shape

    Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, just like half the Earth always has daytime. What changes is how much of that sunny half faces us. The Moon goes around the Earth in about 29½ days from new Moon to new Moon.

    07 / WHERE YOU STAND

    The North Star tells you your latitude

    The height of Polaris above the horizon, in degrees, is almost exactly your latitude. Sailors used that for centuries to find how far north they were.

    South of the equator Polaris is below the horizon, and different constellations, like the Southern Cross, are overhead instead.

    08 / SPACECRAFT OVERHEAD

    Catch a satellite going over

    Satellites shine by reflected sunlight. You can see one when it is still in sunshine high above you while you are already in the dark: usually in the hour or two after sunset or before sunrise. It glides steadily, with no blinking lights, and can fade out in mid-sky when it slides into the Earth's shadow.

    The International Space Station is the brightest, often brighter than any star. SpaceX's Starlink satellites, launched in batches, travel in a line like a string of pearls for a few days after launch, then spread out and climb higher, where they become hard to see without a telescope. For every other kind of satellite — GPS, weather, Hubble, Tiangong, OneWeb, amateur radio and more — on a live world map, visit the sister room Satellite Watch (included in the full collection). SpaceX Dragon spacecraft show up here only while flying on their own; once docked, a Dragon travels as part of the station.

      09 / RADIO ASTRONOMY

      Listening to the sky

      Your eyes see one thin slice of the light the universe sends us. Radio telescopes pick up a much wider slice, coming from cold hydrogen gas, exploding stars, spinning dead stars and black holes, even by day and through clouds.

      BUILD YOUR OWN · PDF, 4 PAGES

      Build your own backyard radio telescope

      A family plan for turning a salvaged DIRECTV satellite dish (about 0.8 m across) into a receive-only radio telescope that hears the 21 cm hydrogen line. It goes step by step: Stage 0 measure what you have; Stage 1 "first light" with a 1420 MHz feed, a SAWbird+ H1 low-noise amplifier, an RTL-SDR Blog V4 receiver and free software (ezRA, VIRGO, SDR++), drift-scanning the Sun, the Milky Way and Cassiopeia A; Stage 2 baselines and logging, with an optional Raspberry Pi; Stage 3 motor tracking with steppers or OnStep and hamlib's rotctld; Stage 4 stretch goals. It can detect hydrogen in the Milky Way, trace the galaxy's structure, measure the Sun and pick out the brightest radio sources, but it will not make pictures of galaxies.

      3. Hydrogen's song and the spinning galaxy

      Cold hydrogen gas gives off radio waves at exactly 1420.406 MHz (21 cm). Gas moving toward us is heard a little higher, gas moving away a little lower: the Doppler effect. Point a radio telescope along the Milky Way and the shifts tell you how fast the galaxy turns.

      Simulation with a simple smooth galaxy. The real measurements stay fast far from the centre, which is one of the main clues to dark matter.

      4. Why radio dishes are huge

      The finest detail a telescope can see depends on its width compared with the wavelength. Radio waves are about 400,000 times longer than visible light, so a radio dish must be enormous, or several dishes must work together.

      Two dishes, one telescope

      Two dishes a distance apart (the baseline) add their signals. A point in the sky makes stripes called fringes; the stripes get finer as the dishes move apart. That is interferometry, used from the Very Large Array to the Event Horizon Telescope.

      Simulation: wavelength 21 cm, a single point source, angles near straight up.

      5. Listen to a pulsar

      Pulsars are collapsed stars, as heavy as the Sun but only about 20 km wide, spinning and sweeping a radio beam past the Earth like a lighthouse. Played as sound, each sweep is a click.

      Simulated sound: clicks made in your browser at each pulsar's real spin rate, not a recording. Sound starts only when you press a button.

      6. How it started, and a mystery

      1932, New Jersey: Karl Jansky, an engineer at Bell Labs, built a rotating antenna to track down static on radio-telephone calls. One hiss rose and fell every 23 hours 56 minutes, a sidereal day, the time the stars take to come back round. It came from the middle of the Milky Way. Radio astronomy was born.

      1937, Illinois: Grote Reber built a 9-metre dish in his own backyard and made the first radio map of the sky. The first radio astronomer was an amateur.

      1967, England: Jocelyn Bell Burnell, a graduate student, spotted a tiny repeating blip in long rolls of paper chart from a new radio telescope: the first pulsar.

      1977, Ohio: The Big Ear telescope at Ohio State University, in Delaware, Ohio, recorded a strong narrow signal near the hydrogen frequency from the direction of Sagittarius. Astronomer Jerry Ehman circled it and wrote "Wow!". It lasted about 72 seconds, the time a fixed source took to drift through the beam, and has never been seen again. Nobody knows what it was; many natural explanations have been suggested.

      7. Start your own radio astronomy

      • Hear Jupiter and the Sun: NASA Radio JOVE sells and documents a simple kit (a 20.1 MHz receiver and wire antennas) used by schools and families to record Jupiter's radio storms.
      • Hear hydrogen: a small dish or horn, a low-noise amplifier and an RTL-SDR receiver can detect the 21 cm line. See the family plan above, and the Society of Amateur Radio Astronomers for software like ezRA and friendly help.
      • Visit or explore: the National Radio Astronomy Observatory and the Green Bank Observatory have lessons, virtual tours and visitor centres; bigear.org tells the Big Ear and Wow! story.

      External links need internet; those organisations keep their own rights, and no endorsement is implied. Receiving radio is legal without a licence in most places; transmitting is not.

      10 / HOW THIS MODEL WORKS

      What is real, and what is simplified

      • Stars: 903 stars to magnitude 4.5 from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue, at their year-2000 catalogue positions, shifted for precession (the Earth's slow wobble) to the date you pick. Star motions through space are ignored; over a human lifetime they are far too small to see.
      • Sun, Moon and planets: calculated from simplified orbits (Paul Schlyter's elements and Jean Meeus's methods). Checked against NASA JPL Horizons: planets and the Sun within a few hundredths of a degree for November 2026; the Moon within about a tenth of a degree. Accuracy drops slowly for dates far from today.
      • Moon: includes the shift you get from standing on the Earth's surface instead of at its centre (parallax), which can move it by up to a degree. Without it, eclipses would be in the wrong place.
      • Left out: the bending of light by the air near the horizon (which lifts things by about half a degree), the small difference between clock time and the Earth's rotation, light-time and aberration corrections, and the Moon's and planets' exact sizes. The Sun, Moon and planets are drawn bigger than life so you can see them.
      • Satellites: SGP4 orbit model (the standard for public orbit data) via the satellite.js library, from a CelesTrak snapshot or a refresh. Old orbits drift: after a week or two predictions can be minutes off, and satellites change orbit on purpose. Sunlight uses a simple cylinder-shaped Earth shadow; brightness is not predicted.
      • Radio sky and radio demos: simulations to show ideas, labelled as such; source positions are real, the pictures and sounds are sketches.
      • Sky colour and faint-star limits are a sketch of twilight, not a measurement. Real skies depend on city lights, haze and your eyes.
      • Times are shown in the chosen place's time zone. For typed-in coordinates, the page uses your device's time zone.

      11 / FAMILIES & TEACHERS

      Take it outside

      Ideas

      • Moon diary: sketch the Moon at the same time each evening for two weeks. Predict tomorrow's shape with the Moon lab first.
      • Planet check: find "What's up tonight", then go out and find one planet. Planets usually shine steadily; stars twinkle more.
      • Human sundial: mark a shadow every hour; compare with the Sun's path on the dome.
      • Latitude detective: estimate the height of Polaris with your fist held at arm's length (one fist is about 10°).

      Stay safe and comfortable

      • Never look at the Sun, and never point binoculars or a telescope near it.
      • Let your eyes adapt in the dark for 15–20 minutes. Use the red night-vision mode so the screen does not undo it.
      • Go with an adult, dress warmly, and stay away from roads.

      Younger learners can start with the dome and the Moon lab. "Old light" and precession are good stretch topics. There is no timer, score, or grading.
