Solar Orrery — the lesson

A 3D solar system driven by real J2000 Keplerian elements: eight planets, Pluto and Halley's comet on their true eccentric, tilted orbits, a Mars retrograde visualiser, and an Earth–Mars Hohmann transfer lab. This page is the audit. Following the house rule, the shipped simulation was not just read — it was extracted and driven headlessly through its own presets, its own launch button logic and its own ephemeris, for tens of thousands of simulated days, and everything below is what it measured, not what the code comments promised.

⚠ One false claim, flagged up front. The Transfer Lab promises that launching at the 44.3° Hohmann window gives a Mars intercept in ~259 days — the launch banner says exactly that. Driven three times at the page's own “Jump to 44.3°” window, the shipped probe was never declared an intercept. Closest approaches were 0.310 AU, 0.068 AU and 0.056 AU (46, 10 and 8 million km), and every flight ended with the probe's own status flipping to “missed / heliocentric.” The textbook numbers behind the window (44.3°, 258.9 days, 5.59 km/s) are themselves correct — see below — but the probe in the page does not fly to where Mars actually will be. Everything else in this exhibit is sound, with the bends named further down.

The verdict: sound with bends — real orbits, real retrograde, a transfer probe that misses

The orbital data is excellent. Every planet's period in the shipped table matches the real period to within 0.03%, and every semi-major axis matches the standard value to within 0.5%. The Kepler solver itself is exact: stepping each body forward by precisely its shipped period returned it to its starting position to within 10−14 AU for all ten orbiting bodies, and stepping half a period put it on the far side of its orbit, every time. Orbital speeds come from the vis-viva equation and check out (Earth 29.3–30.3 km/s over its orbit; Mercury 38.9 km/s at aphelion, as shipped at epoch).

Retrograde is real, not scripted. Driving the ephemeris day by day, Mars's geocentric ecliptic longitude genuinely reverses around each opposition — three full episodes measured in detail below, plus 38 oppositions over 30,000 simulated days whose spacing averages exactly the real synodic period.

The Hohmann textbook math is right; the probe that flies it isn't. That split — correct numbers on the gauge, a probe that aims at the wrong point — is the one place the page fails its own claim, and it is detailed in its own section below.

Read the table this way: the periods — the quantity the clock actually runs on — are right everywhere. A few outer-planet distances are the “average distance” figures quoted in some textbooks rather than the Kepler-consistent semi-major axes (Saturn is the largest, +0.48%), which is why their T²÷a³ sags to 0.986–0.995 instead of 1.000. Retrograde: measured, episode by episode

The page's claim: when faster Earth overtakes Mars at opposition, the Earth→Mars line of sight sweeps backwards and Mars traces a loop. The audit enabled the page's own retrograde mode, ran the ephemeris forward day by day, and recorded the page's own geocentric longitude (λ), latitude (β) and retrograde flag:

Episode (sim days from J2000)DurationLongitude reversedβ range during loop

Real Mars retrogrades last roughly 58–81 days and back up roughly 10–20° depending on where in its eccentric orbit the opposition falls — the shipped episodes sit squarely inside both ranges, and they vary from opposition to opposition for the right reason (Mars's e = 0.093), which a scripted animation would not do. Over a 30,000-day run the audit counted 38 oppositions: intervals ranged 764–811 days with a mean of 779.9 days, against the real synodic period of ~780 days (itself an average — real opposition spacing varies the same way). The retrograde preset also works as advertised: it jumps to 72 days before the next opposition, and driving it forward produced the full prograde → stationary → retrograde → prograde sequence in the page's own sky-loop trail. One exaggeration in the drawing is listed in the bends: the 3D dome multiplies Mars's ecliptic latitude by 5.2 so the loop reads clearly, while the small inset chart plots the true latitude.

The Hohmann lab: right numbers, wrong rendezvous

atransfer = (1.000 + 1.524) / 2 = 1.262 AU  ·  flight time = ½ × a3/2 = 258.9 days The page's static claims, all verified: ideal phase angle 44.34° (theory: 180° minus the 135.7° Mars travels during the flight = 44.3°), flight time 258.9 d (audit recomputation 258.87 d), and Δv 5.59 km/s — which the audit reproduced as the heliocentric sum, 2.94 km/s leaving Earth's orbit + 2.65 km/s matching Mars's. That Δv is the orbit-to-orbit figure, not a from-the-launchpad figure, and the page doesn't claim otherwise.

Then the audit used the page the way a visitor would: jump to the window with the page's own button logic, launch the probe with the page's own launchMarsProbe(), and step time forward. Three separate launch cycles:

Launch (all at Δθ = 44.3°)Probe's own flight timeClosest Mars approachPage's verdict
Cycle 1 (next window after 2026-07-15)256.7 d0.310 AU (46M km)Missed
Cycle 2 (~800 days later)256.3 d0.068 AU (10M km)Missed
Cycle 3 (~1,700 days later)258.1 d0.056 AU (8M km)Missed

Why it misses: the probe's transfer ellipse is aimed at the point opposite the Sun from the launch point, at Mars's semi-major distance — the circular-orbit textbook construction. It is never aimed at Mars's actual future position. In the real (and shipped) eccentric system, Mars's true longitude runs ahead of or behind its mean longitude by up to ~19° over a transfer, so the rendezvous point and Mars arrive at different times. In cycle 1 the probe reached its aphelion point while Mars was still 19° of longitude — half an AU — away. The audit also scanned other launch phases: launches near Δθ ≈ 55–65° did intercept in two of three cycles tested (closest approach 0.029 AU, intercept declared on day ~190–255), which confirms the probe flies a real Keplerian transfer — it's the 44.3° label on the window that doesn't fit the eccentric simulation underneath it. Two smaller wrinkles: each probe's actual flight time (256.3–258.1 d) differs slightly from the advertised 258.9 d because the probe builds its ellipse from Earth's instantaneous distance that day rather than 1.000 AU; and the intercept detector only counts a pass under 0.12 AU during a middle window of the flight, so even the 0.056 AU cycle-3 pass was never announced. The gauge, the window countdown and the phase geometry are all honest — it is specifically the “launch in the window and intercept Mars” payoff that the shipped code does not deliver.

Halley's comet: one return exact, the next one early

The comet's orbit is the real one. a = 17.834 AU, e = 0.967 gives a perihelion of 0.586 AU and aphelion of 35.08 AU — both correct — and a period of 27,510 days = 75.32 years, inside the historical 75–76 year range. Scanning the shipped ephemeris for perihelion passages, the audit found the last return at r = 0.586 AU on 1986-02-09 — the exact real date of Halley's 1986 perihelion. The Halley preset (jump to 25 May 2061, dive at 15 days/second, tails igniting as r falls — the tail model brightens steeply inside ~3.6 AU and points the ion tail anti-sunward and the dust tail between anti-solar and backwards along the orbit, both correct) reaches perihelion in the simulation on 2061-06-05. The real next perihelion is 28 July 2061. The 53-day gap is the honest price of a fixed Keplerian period: real Halley's period is stretched and squeezed by planetary perturbations on every return, and this particular return runs long. The year on the preset is right; don't set a calendar reminder by the simulation's date.

Axial tilts and the seasons claim

Every shipped tilt matches the real obliquity: Sun 7.25°, Mercury 0.03°, Venus 177.36° (upside-down, hence its retrograde spin), Earth 23.44°, Mars 25.19°, Jupiter 3.13°, Saturn 26.73°, Uranus 97.77° (rolling on its side), Neptune 28.32°, Pluto 122.53°. Saturn's rings are drawn in the plane those 26.73° imply, and Earth's card states the one seasons claim the page makes — “Its 23.44° axial tilt drives the seasons” — which is true. What the page does not do is model seasons: there is no insolation, solstice or equinox readout, and the drawn pole for each planet points in a fixed display direction chosen for readability rather than at that planet's true celestial pole. The tilt magnitudes you'd read off the inspector are all real; treat the 3D pole directions as diagrammatic. The axis direction is at least held fixed in space as a planet orbits, which is the geometry that actually causes seasons.

Radial rescaling preserves each body's direction from the Sun, so the page's compressed-mode note is right that the 3D angles stay true even while the distances don't. But “True Distance” is true about distances only:

Where it bends

Planet sizes are never to scale, in any mode — and the page doesn't say so. The drawn radii are readability sizes: Sun ÷ Earth is 2.6 on screen (real: 109), Jupiter ÷ Earth is 1.9 (real: 11.0), Mercury ÷ Earth is 0.59 (real: 0.38), Pluto ÷ Earth is 0.53 (real: 0.19). “True Dist” mode shrinks planet spheres by a token 15% and otherwise leaves sizes untouched. The distance badge and scale note are careful about distances; no equivalent disclosure exists for diameters, so a visitor in “True Distance” mode can reasonably — and wrongly — assume the whole scene is to scale.

“Log-compressed” isn't logarithmic. The compressed-mode note calls the radii “Log-compressed.” The shipped formula is a power law, 22 + 56 × r0.56, with a 22-unit offset so Mercury clears the Sun's disc. The effect is similar in spirit — outer distances squeezed — but the named function is wrong.

The retrograde loop's height is exaggerated ×5.2 in the 3D view. Mars's true ecliptic latitude during a loop is a few degrees (measured above: spans of ~3–5°). The main 3D dome multiplies that latitude by 5.2 so the S-loop stands out against the zodiac ring — the code comments admit it, the page itself doesn't. The small inset chart, by contrast, plots the true latitude. Same data, two honesties: the chart is the one to measure from.

A few outer-planet distances are textbook “average distances,” not semi-major axes. Saturn 9.5826 vs 9.537 AU is the largest (+0.48%); Uranus and Neptune run +0.15% and +0.13% high on the same basis. Periods are unaffected and exact — see the main table.

Halley's next perihelion lands 53 days early in the simulation (2061-06-05 shipped vs 28 July 2061 real), because a fixed 75.32-year Kepler period can't follow the perturbation-stretched real return. Its previous perihelion is reproduced to the day (1986-02-09).

The “Next Window” countdown is an average-rate estimate. It divides the phase gap by the mean synodic rate (0.4616°/day). Because both orbits are eccentric, the true wait can differ by days; the page's own jump-to-window search (which scans the actual ephemeris) is the accurate tool, and the audit verified it lands within 0.1° of the target phase.

Small print that checks out anyway. The asteroid belt (2.15–3.33 AU) and Kuiper belt (30.8–48 AU) are procedural — invented individual rocks, disclosed by their nature as belts, with Keplerian periods computed from their invented distances. Moon distances and sizes around the giant planets are display values, not scale values, though their orbital periods are the real ones (Io 1.77 d through Titan 15.95 d, Triton retrograde at −5.88 d). The Sun's telemetry “230 km/s” is its galactic orbital speed, labelled as such in its inspector card (230 Myr galactic year).
