Is the Earth Flat? — a walk through the proofs

Meet your friend. He has watched the videos, done his own research, and brought five proofs, each one exactly as it is actually argued. He is not stupid and he is not lying — he is sure. Your job is not to win the argument. Your job is to run the measurement at each bench and keep the receipt. When the numbers land, he will play his card — “Yes, but…” — and the card goes in your deck, and the next proof unlocks. Collect all five, and there is one test left that both sides agreed in advance would settle it. It involves a plane ticket to Antarctica.

The designer, on why this game exists

“As assumption is a map as well. Sitting with an assumption can lead to a lot of 'solutions' to an observation that on the surface look both scientific and correct, but be so incredibly wrong. Perspective, stepping away from that surface observation can correct a map in ways you might not even be able to concieve by just using your eyes and looking closely. One has to learn to look just over that potential horizonn and lift the view to a broader expanse.”

— Steven Powell

How to play — and the honesty notes

How to play. Each station opens with your friend's proof, stated the way it is really stated. Work the bench — sliders, buttons, a telescope zoom — until the measurement lands. The receipt is written in plain numbers. He then plays his “Yes, but…” card: the standard deflection, also stated the way it is really stated. The card joins your deck and the next station unlocks. Five receipts earn the finale.

The drawings exaggerate; the numbers don't. A curve of 24 feet over six miles cannot be drawn to scale on a screen and seen at all, so the pictures bend the curve up to fifty times over so your eye can follow them. Every number printed is the true, unexaggerated figure, computed from a radius of 6,371 km.

Refraction is real. Air bends light — on a standard day by about a seventh of the curve — and the first station's receipt says so out loud. The 1870 redo of that experiment (Alfred Russel Wallace, on a wager) raised the sightline and used mid-course markers precisely to defeat refraction, and the curve was still there. A fair bench names the other side's best point; this one tries to.

The sources are real. The Bedford Level sightings (Rowbotham, 1860s–70s; Wallace's wager, 1870). The $20,000 ring-laser gyroscope bought by flat-earth experimenters, filmed in Behind the Curve (2018), drifting 15 degrees an hour. And the finale: The Final Experiment (December 2024), where flat-earthers and globe-earthers travelled to Union Glacier, Antarctica together to watch the sun for a day — a test both camps had agreed beforehand would settle the question.

What the game is for. Not mockery — measurement. Every proof here was once somebody's honest observation, and several of them are good observations whose explanations are one layer deeper than they look. The receipts stand whether or not your friend agrees. That is rather the point of receipts.

On a touch screen: every bench here is buttons and sliders — the whole game plays by tap.

Designed by Steven Powell from the public arguments themselves; built by Muse for Tonight's Sky, beside the Backyard Radio Telescope Plan — the instrument that listens to the sky these proofs argue about. Read the lesson for the audit of every number on this page.
