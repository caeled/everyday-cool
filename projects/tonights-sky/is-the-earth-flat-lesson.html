Is the Earth Flat? — what the audit measured

Steven's design: a social game that walks the five classic flat-earth proofs as they are actually argued, benches each one with real numbers, answers every measurement with the standard “Yes, but…” deflection as a collectible card, and ends with The Final Experiment — the December 2024 Antarctica trip both camps agreed would settle it. Muse built it; this lesson is the audit of the build, run before the page went live.

Verdict: PASS — 25 of 25 checks, zero exceptions. Every figure on the page was recomputed from scratch by the auditor (never by the page's own code), and the whole game was then played end-to-end through its real buttons and sliders: five receipts earned, the deflection deck completed at 5/5, the Antarctica finale ran to its end.

The numbers, checked independently

  • The Laser. Drop over 6 miles: page 24.0 ft, independent 24.0 ft (d²/2R, R = 6,371 km — the surveyor's “8 inches per mile squared” is this parabola in a hat). At the classic Bedford settings — telescope 8 inches up, flag at 6 miles — the horizon is 1.0 mile away and the curve hides 16.7 ft of the far shore. A 5-foot flag is eleven feet underwater, geometrically speaking.
  • The Horizon Dip. dip = arccos(R/(R+h)): at 35,000 ft the page reads 3.31°, independent 3.31°. At 10 ft, 3.4 arcminutes; at 1,000 ft, 0.56°. The flat prediction of 0.00° at all heights is the claim the bench tests, and it fails by exactly the predicted amount at every altitude probed.
  • The Ship. From 10 ft, the horizon is 3.87 miles; at 8 miles the curve hides 11.4 ft of a 12-foot hull. The zoom interaction restores the superstructure only — the occlusion line is geometry, not resolution, and the page's cut sits where the formula puts it.
  • Three Cities' Shadows. Shadow angles from a spherical Earth are the central angles themselves: 22.48° at 2,500 km and 44.97° at 5,000 km from the subsolar point. A near sun fitted to the second city needs H = 6,036 km; fitted to the third, 5,006 km — the same sun at two heights 1,030 km apart. The auditor's own least-error search over every H from 3,000–10,000 km found the same best compromise the page offers and the same irreducible total error (≈4.6°). One radius fits all three exactly; no one height fits any two. This is Eratosthenes' experiment run in reverse, and it is the strongest bench in the game.
  • The Gyroscope. The page's rate is the sidereal rate, 360°/23.9345 h = 15.04° per hour — the figure the flat-earth experimenters' own $20,000 ring-laser gyroscope reported in Behind the Curve (2018). Their instrument, their footage, the page invents nothing.
  • The Finale. Solar altitude at Union Glacier (78°S) on December 21, computed from sin a = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H with δ = −23.44°: 35.4° at noon, 11.5° at midnight, and the auditor sampled the full 24 hours at quarter-hour steps — the minimum is the midnight value; the sun never crosses zero. The real Final Experiment travellers watched this happen; the page's sky is the sky they reported.

The bends, printed

  • The drawings exaggerate the curve up to ×50 — stated on the game page itself. A true-scale 24-foot drop over six miles is a fifth of a pixel; the pictures are schematics, the numbers are not.
  • Refraction is not modelled in the benches — it is conceded in words instead: standard refraction bends light by roughly a seventh of the curvature, which is why the page's Help retells Wallace's 1870 redo (raised sightline, mid-course markers) rather than pretending Rowbotham's setup was careful.
  • The companion is a composite. No line of his is a quotation of a living person; every proof and every deflection is the argument as it circulates publicly, which was Steven's brief — the game argues with positions, not people.
  • The ship's hull is a tidy 12 feet and the sea is glass. Swell and haze — his third card — really do eat hulls on real water; the bench isolates the geometric effect, which is the part that survives a clear day.

Why it belongs in Tonight's Sky

Every proof in the game is a claim about the sky — where the horizon sits, where the sun is, whether the ground turns under a gyroscope — and it stands one click from the Backyard Radio Telescope Plan, the instrument this room is building to listen to that sky. Steven's rule for the whole collection applies here unchanged: breakage is the only honest audit — so the game doesn't argue. It measures, keeps the receipt, and lets the “yes, but” cards pile up where everyone can see them.
