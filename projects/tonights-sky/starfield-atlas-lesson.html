Starfield Atlas — the lesson

A 3D star field you can fly through: zoom from the Sun's planets out to a whole galactic neighbourhood, drag the sky around, click any star and read its data card. Underneath sit two very different claims. One is a real catalog — 59 named stars placed in 3D from their real right ascension, declination and distance. The other is a procedural field of 3,200 invented stars filling the space between them. This page is the audit of both: the shipped code was run headlessly and driven through its own controls, every catalog star was checked against the published catalogues, and every bend — including the places where the picture quietly stops being the data — is named.

The verdict: a real catalog, honestly placed, wrapped in an invented sky

The 59-star catalog is real data, converted losslessly. The page stores each star as J2000 RA (hours), Dec (degrees) and distance (light-years) and converts to 3D Cartesian coordinates with the standard spherical transform. The audit inverted the transform on the shipped coordinates and recovered every star's RA and Dec exactly (worst discrepancy 0.00° after rounding) — nothing drifts in conversion. Against the references, the ten nearest stars agree with the Gaia-based nearest-star list to the arcsecond in position and to the third decimal in distance (Proxima: shipped 4.246 ly, reference 4.2465 ly). Distances across the whole catalog: 51 of 59 within 4% of the reference value, most of the rest inside genuinely unsettled published ranges. Spectral classes match the references for 57 of 59 stars outright; the two exceptions are a half-subclass on Wolf 359 and Achernar's older B6 label against the current B3 (see bends).

The controls do what they promise — measured, not read. Following the Orbital Pinball rule (a launch path that was only ever read, never driven, hid a dead game for five weeks), the audit drove this page's own handlers headlessly:

  • Picking: 59 for 59. Each catalog star was selected through the page's own selectStar(), the animation loop was run until the camera settled, and the page's own pickStarAt() was asked what lives at that star's projected screen position. It returned that star every time — no neighbour steals a click. A hover over Vega's projected position likewise set the hover tag to Vega.
  • Dragging is exact. A simulated 100 px × 20 px drag changed yaw by −0.5500 rad and pitch by +0.1100 rad — precisely the handler's own 0.0055 rad/px, both axes.
  • Zoom clamps are exact. Driving the wheel handler to both stops left the target log-distance at exactly −2.6 and +3.55, the coded limits (roughly 0.0025 ly to 3,550 ly of camera range).
  • The data card never lies about which star you picked. For all 59 catalog stars, selecting the star and reading back the HUD's name, spectral pill, distance, temperature, luminosity, radius and mass gave that star's own catalog values — 0 mismatches. Clicking Vega's row in the catalog list selected Vega and the HUD followed.
  • The counts are true. The catalog holds 59 stars (the drawer's live label reads "59 Notable"; a static "56" in the HTML is replaced at boot) and the procedural field holds exactly 3,200 stars, as the page's "~3,200 explorable stars" claims.
  • Travel times are real arithmetic. The "Voyager 1" readout uses 17,600 years per light-year; at Voyager 1's actual ~17 km/s, one light-year takes c/v ≈ 17,635 years. Vega's card reads 440.7 kyr for 25.04 ly — correct.

Constellations: the lines are the real asterisms

Six constellations are drawn as 3D line segments between catalog stars. Every link ID resolves, and the angular separations computed from the shipped coordinates match the real figures: Orion's Belt stars sit 1.38° and 1.36° apart (Mintaka–Alnilam–Alnitak); the Big Dipper's bowl closes (Dubhe–Merak 5.38°, Megrez–Dubhe 10.23°) and its handle runs Alioth–Mizar–Alkaid; Cassiopeia's W chains Caph–Schedar–Gamma–Ruchbah–Segin at 3.6–4.9° a step; the Southern Cross has its true long axis (Gacrux–Acrux 6.02°) and crossbar (Mimosa–Imai 4.27°). The star notes also survived spot-checking: Merak really is ~43 ly closer than Dubhe, Caph ~10× closer than Gamma Cassiopeiae, Polaris's Cepheid period is 3.97 days, Spica's binary period 4.0 days, Regulus spins in under 16 hours, the Crab Pulsar spins ~30 times a second, and Cygnus X-1's black hole is the 21 M☉ of the 2021 revision. Two asterism bends are listed below (Scorpius is abbreviated; Cygnus gains a spoke).

Where it bends

Two stars are drawn in the wrong place — and the page half-says so. The Crab Pulsar (true distance 6,500 ly) and Cygnus X-1 (7,200 ly) are rescaled onto a ~2,500 ly shell "for viewing": the audit measured their drawn positions at 2,490 ly and 2,546 ly. Their data cards still report the true distances, and their notes disclose the scaling — though the Crab's note says "~2,450 ly" while the code actually draws it at 2,490. Every other star is drawn at its true distance.

Flagged plainly — one internal contradiction. 55 Cancri's note calls it a "Famous 5-planet system," and five planets are indeed known — but the page's own planet list shows only four (Janssen, Galileo, Brahe, Harriot); the fifth, Lipperhey (55 Cnc d), is missing from the system you can visit.

Planet lists age faster than star data. Barnard's Star is shown with one planet; four are now confirmed (b, c, d and e, announced 2024–25). Tau Ceti's g, e and f and Gliese 581's c are unconfirmed candidates, and Aldebaran b is disputed — all are presented as plain planets, no caveat.

Some far-star numbers sit at the top of their published ranges. Alnilam is the extreme: shipped 2,000 ly / 537,000 L☉ / 40 M☉ against a current reference 1,252 ly / 271,000 L☉ / 28.4 M☉ (its distance is genuinely unsettled, 1,180–2,000 ly in print). Also high: Mintaka 1,200 vs 900 ly, Sadr 1,800 vs 1,500, Albireo 430 vs Gaia's ~365, Gamma Cassiopeiae 550 vs 610, Sargas 300 vs 330 with a mass of 5.7 against a current ~3.1 M☉, and Segin's 410 ly is the Hipparcos value where Gaia says ~466. Betelgeuse runs the other way in one field only: its 548 ly and 764 R☉ match Joyce et al. 2020 exactly, but its 126,000 L☉ is above the current ~65,000–105,000 L☉ range. Polaris's radius (37.5 R☉) predates the 2024 interferometric 46.3 R☉, and Imai's temperature (22,570 K) runs ~11% above the 20,400 K of Sharma 2022. Deneb's classic values (2,615 ly, 196,000 L☉, 19 M☉) are the standard textbook ones — a 2026 study revises all three sharply down, which the lesson notes rather than adjudicates.

There is no magnitude anywhere. The one number skywatchers sort by — apparent magnitude — appears in no data card, list row or label. The catalog's brightness ordering therefore can't be checked on the page; the reference mV column in the table above is supplied by this lesson instead.

The 3,200 "explorable" stars are invented, wearing real uniforms. The procedural field stars carry plausible catalog-style names (HD, HIP, Gliese, TYC, Gaia plus a number) that do not correspond to real stars, at invented positions — 37 of them within 15 ly of the Sun, a volume where every real star is known and named. Their data cards do disclose "Procedural" in the note, and their spectral mix (46% red dwarfs) and property spreads are reasonable; but a name like "Gliese 10437" invites a trust the star hasn't earned.

The habitable-zone ring is a display convention. Every system's green band is drawn at the same 0.78–1.28 display units. A real habitable zone scales with the square root of luminosity — TRAPPIST-1's (L = 0.00055 L☉) should sit ~40× closer in than the Sun's. The planets are placed to match the band, so the "(HZ)" tags are true to the drawing, and for the famous systems (Proxima b, TRAPPIST-1 d–g, Kepler-186f) they are also true in fact — but the ring itself never moves with the star.

The Solar System close-up is exact inside Mars and compressed outside it. Mercury through Mars carry their true distances (0.39–1.52 AU) and true periods (0.24–1.88 yr). The giants are squeezed: Jupiter drawn at 2.45 units with a 4.2 yr period (real: 5.2 AU, 11.9 yr), Neptune at 4.65 units and 12.4 yr (real: 30.1 AU, 164.8 yr). As a picture it reads clearly; as a scale model it stops at the asteroid belt.

Two asterism liberties. Scorpius is drawn as three stars — Antares, Sargas, Shaula: the heart and two tail stars, with the claws and most of the tail absent, though the segments drawn are between real Scorpius stars in the right order. And Cygnus gains a spoke from Sadr to 61 Cygni: positionally honest (the stars really are 8.7° apart), but 61 Cygni is not part of the Northern Cross figure — it's a famous neighbour invited into the lines.
