Eclipse & Tides — the lesson

One geometry — Sun, Earth and Moon falling into line — drives everything on this page: whether a solar eclipse is total or annular, whether the Moon turns red in Earth's shadow, and whether the day's tides run spring or neap. The audit extracted the shipped computeSystemMetrics() core and drove it headlessly through the page's own presets, sliders and phase jumps, measuring distances, apparent discs, shadow cones and tidal amplitudes against the real numbers. The headline physics is right, and one calibration inside it is genuinely elegant. The distances printed in the page's own notes, however, are numbers its engine never produces — that is flagged first-class below.

The verdict: total at perigee, annular at apogee — measured, and the crossover is calibrated to a tenth of a pixel

The total/annular rule is implemented exactly as physics states it: an eclipse at New Moon is classified total when the Moon's apparent disc is at least the Sun's, annular when it is smaller. Driving the page's own two presets:

That last row is the elegant part. The real crossover — the distance at which the Moon's disc exactly equals the Sun's — is 373,410 km by the same angular-size formula, and the real Moon's umbral cone is ≈373,400 km long: the two numbers are the same coincidence, which is why total eclipses exist at all. The page's schematic optical geometry was calibrated to reproduce it, and the audit confirms the calibration in the page's own pixels: at 373,500 km the drawn umbra is 156.3 px long and the Moon-to-Earth-surface gap is 156.4 px — the cone tip falls short by 0.1 px, exactly as a borderline-annular geometry should. At the perigee preset the cone (157.9 px) overshoots the gap (136.0 px) and reaches Earth; at apogee it stops at 152.3 px against a 208.0 px gap, and the page correctly draws the diverging antumbra beyond the tip. The shadow cones converge and diverge the right way: the Moon's narrows to a point, Earth's (722.6 px in the same schematic, against a 218 px mean orbit) sweeps far past the Moon, as the real ~1.4 million km Earth cone does.

FALSE — the distances and disc sizes printed in the page's own notes are never produced by its engine. The Optics tab states perigee 356,500 km with a 33.5′ Moon (ratio 1.048×) and apogee 406,700 km with a 29.4′ Moon. The shipped Kepler formula, d = a(1 − e²)/(1 + e·cos ν) with its eccentricity rescaled, bottoms out at 359,337 km / 33.24′ / 1.039× and tops out at 409,463 km / 29.17′ — the slider's maximum e = 0.065 cannot reach 356,500 km at all (that would need e ≈ 0.072, outside the slider's range). The direction of every claim is right and the eclipse outcomes are right; the quoted numbers are the real-world extremes (real perigee ≈ 356,400 km, real apogee ≈ 405,500 km, real Moon 0.49°–0.56°) pasted onto an engine whose range is shifted ~3,000 km inward at perigee and ~4,000 km outward at apogee. Read the live telemetry, not the notes panel.

Nodes and eclipse seasons: the tilt is real, the seasons are yours to dial

Eclipses only happen near a node — measured. With the real 5.14° orbital tilt set, the audit held a perigee New Moon and rotated the line of nodes: nodes aligned with the Sun–Earth line gave β = 0.00° and totality; nodes 90° away gave β = +5.14° and No Eclipse — the shadow passes over Earth's pole, exactly the page's “why eclipses are rare” preset (which itself measures β = +5.09°, no eclipse, at a waning-crescent Moon 14° short of New). The same test at Full Moon switches a Blood Moon on and off identically. The β threshold in the engine is honest within its geometry: at perigee, β = 0.60° still yields a partial eclipse and β = 0.80° yields none.

What the page does not have is any motion of the nodes themselves. There is no nodal precession — in reality the nodes regress through a full circle in ~18.6 years, which is why eclipse seasons drift earlier each year on a 346.6-day “eclipse year” instead of the 365.25-day calendar year. The node axis here is a manual slider: eclipse seasons exist only when you align the nodes yourself. That is an absence, not an error — the page never claims precession — but a visitor could leave thinking the nodes sit still, and in reality their slow regression is the whole reason eclipse seasons move.

Phases: a 29.53-day cycle, exact to the hundredth

The synodic clock is the real one. The engine carries SYNODIC_MONTH_DAYS = 29.530588 and maps orbital angle onto it linearly. Measured along the cycle: First Quarter at Day 7.38 (50% lit), Full Moon at Day 14.77 (100% lit), Third Quarter at Day 22.15 (50% lit), back to New at Day 29.53. Illumination follows ½(1 − cos) of the synodic angle, and the four phase-jump buttons all land on the phases their labels promise (the button labelled “1st Q” does deliver a First Quarter — an internal label/map swap cancels out). Two honest simplifications: the wall-clock animation runs one cycle in ~28.5 s at 1× speed regardless of the day readout, and the Moon's angular speed is modulated by a Kepler factor (1 + e·cos ν)² — perigee motion 1.134×, apogee 0.874×, a ratio of 1.30 against the real ≈1.25 for the Moon's true eccentricity. The form of the law is right; the slider's exaggerated e inflates it slightly.

Tides: two bulges, the right cause, springs and neaps on the right days

The explanation is the correct one. The Tides tab states it plainly — gravity pulls harder on Earth's near ocean and weaker on the far ocean, stretching the water into two opposite bulges. That is differential (tidal) gravity, full stop; the word “centrifugal” never appears. The drawing follows the words: the ocean envelope is a cos(2φ) figure with a bulge on the Moon side and the opposite bulge, and stepping the observer pin through 24 hours at New Moon measured two high waters (+1.68 m, twice) and two low waters (−1.68 m, twice) — a semidiurnal tide, as most coasts have.

Spring and neap land where they should. At mean distance the measured combined amplitude is 1.75 m at New and Full (syzygy, bulges adding) and 0.71 m at both quarters (bulges at 90°, partially cancelling): the page labels the first Spring Tide and the second Neap Tide, and perigean syzygy earns “King Spring Tide.” The lunar amplitude obeys the inverse-cube law the page advertises (∝ 1/d³): 1.47 m at the perigee preset against 0.99 m at apogee, a ratio of 1.48 — the real (409/359)³ is 1.48. The solar component is held at 0.55 m, labelled “46% of mean lunar,” against the real solar-to-lunar tidal ratio of ≈0.46.

height(φ) = AMoon·cos 2(φ − αMoon) + ASun·cos 2(φ − αSun) The shipped interference formula, verbatim in effect: two cos-2φ bulge systems added as vectors. Springs and neaps fall out of it; they are not scripted.

The bends in the tide model are about time, not geometry. The model's springs peak instantly at New and Full Moon; real coasts lag by the “age of the tide,” typically 1–2 days, because oceans take time to respond. The one lead the page does model — a “friction bulge lead,” default +10°, slider to 22° — is a different, real effect (Earth's spin dragging the bulge ahead of the Moon), but 10° is a generous classroom value, and a visitor could conflate the two leads. The solar amplitude also never varies with Earth's own orbital eccentricity, and the “Tidal Range” percentage is normalised to a perigean spring maximum, so an ordinary mean-distance spring reads 79% rather than 100% — true to its definition, easy to misread.

The Blood Moon: the reddening is explained, and explained right

Present and correct. The Optics tab: during a total lunar eclipse, “Earth's atmosphere bends red wavelengths into the umbra via Rayleigh scattering while filtering out blue light.” That is the accepted mechanism — refraction bends sunlight around Earth's limb into the shadow cone while Rayleigh scattering strips the blue out of it, leaving the copper-red residue; the drawing even adds the thin turquoise ozone fringe at the umbra's edge, a real and subtle detail. The geometry underneath checks out too: Earth's umbral radius at the Moon is taken as 0.68° at mean distance (real ≈ 0.72°), its penumbral radius 1.24° (real ≈ 1.24°), both shrinking correctly as 1/d, and totality requires the Moon's centre within umbra-minus-Moon (0.42°; real ≈ 0.46°). The Blood Moon preset measures a Full Moon at 363,193 km, Day 14.77, fully immersed — Blood Moon, as labelled.

Where it bends

Flagged plainly — printed numbers the engine never reaches. Perigee “356,500 km,” apogee “406,700 km,” Moon discs “33.5′” and “29.4′” and the “1.048×” ratio in the page's notes (and its opening static telemetry) do not match the live engine, which measures 359,337 km / 33.24′ / 1.039× and 409,463 km / 29.17′. The real extremes those numbers copy (≈356,400 km and ≈405,500 km) are themselves produced by solar perturbations shifting the Moon's perigee month to month — no single fixed ellipse, this page's included, reproduces both ends at once.

The eclipse window is widened for the mouse. Horizontal Sun–Moon separation is compressed 0.28× (and vertical β 0.85×) inside the eclipse test, so a partial solar eclipse persists to ±~1.9° of orbital travel instead of the real ±~0.54° of sky separation. The threshold in degrees of separation (sum of the two radii, 0.54°) is the real one; the mapping from orbit angle to separation is the bend, and it is disclosed only in a code comment.

The Sun never changes size. Its disc is fixed at 31.99′. In reality the Sun's apparent diameter swings 31.5′–32.5′ between Earth's aphelion and perihelion, and that swing co-decides total vs annular alongside the Moon's distance — some annular eclipses are the Earth's doing, not the Moon's. Here the Moon carries the whole effect.

No nodal precession, no eclipse year. Nodes regress ~18.6 years and eclipse seasons recur every 346.6 days in reality; here the node line is a static slider and nothing ever drifts. See the nodes section above.

Springs are instantaneous. No age-of-tide lag (real: ~1–2 days behind New and Full). The +10° default friction lead is a separate effect, drawn large.

Eccentricity is exaggerated, then still falls short. The slider tops out at e = 0.065 against the Moon's real mean 0.0549 — labelled in code as a deliberate reach for “supermoon perigee” — yet, per the flagged item, the rescaled distance formula lands at 359,337 km anyway. The exaggeration buys drama in the drawing (orbit radii 182–254 px) more than in the telemetry.

Tidal amplitudes are a classroom ocean. A single global 1.20 m mean lunar amplitude with no coasts, basins or resonance: fine as the interference demonstration it is, but no real port's range (0 m amphidromes to 16 m in the Bay of Fundy) can be read off it, and the page's metre values should be taken as the model's, not a forecast.
