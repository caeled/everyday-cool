Stellar Evolution — the lesson

Pick a birth mass — a tenth of a Sun, one Sun, twenty-five Suns — and this exhibit runs the whole life: protostar, main sequence, giant, and whatever is left at the end, with a live Hertzsprung–Russell track and a cutaway of the fusion shells stacking up inside. This page is the audit. Following the house rule, the shipped code was not just read: its own evolution engine (evaluateStar() and mainSequenceProps()) was extracted and driven headlessly in Node, swept from birth to remnant in 1,000 steps at every mass below, and the numbers the engine actually produces are the ones in the tables. Most of the big story is right. A few claims are not, and those are flagged in red where they occur.

The verdict: the life stories are right; some of the numbers on the labels are not

The architecture is correct, and it is measured, not read. Driven through its own engine, a 1 M☉ star lives 10.0 Gyr on the main sequence, swells to a 168 R☉ red giant at 3,150 K, flashes helium, sits on the horizontal branch, pulses on the AGB, ejects a planetary nebula and ends as a 0.55 M☉ carbon–oxygen white dwarf. A 15 M☉ star becomes a 686 R☉ red supergiant, stacks the full onion of shells in the right order — H → He → C → Ne → O → Si → iron core — collapses as a Type II supernova and leaves a neutron star. A 25 M☉ star (no preset; set on the slider) is stripped to a Wolf–Rayet star and ends as a black hole. The regime boundaries are hard lines in the code and the audit stepped across each one: 7.99 M☉ → white dwarf, 8.00 → neutron star; 21.99 → neutron star at 2.01 M☉, 22.0 → black hole.

FALSE — the Red Dwarf preset's “Trillion-yr burn.” The preset is 0.2 M☉, and the engine's own lifetime for 0.2 M☉ is 352 Gyr — about a third of a trillion years. Even the smallest star the slider allows, 0.1 M☉, measures 868 Gyr, short of a trillion. The direction is right (red dwarfs vastly outlive the current age of the universe, which is why no blue dwarf or helium white dwarf has ever been observed — the page is showing a far-future life, honestly labelled in its narrative), but the preset label overstates its own engine's number by roughly 3×.

FALSE — the Sun's planetary nebula is drawn at less than one solar luminosity. At the end of the AGB the engine has the Sun at 3,500 L☉. The very next stage, Planetary Nebula Ejection, restarts its luminosity interpolation from the main-sequence value, and the audit measured it entering the nebula stage at 0.70 L☉, climbing only to ~130 L☉ even as its temperature is driven to 115,000 K. A real exposed core stays near a few thousand L☉ while it heats — that near-constant-luminosity crossing is the signature move on the H–R diagram. On this page's H–R track the Sun instead falls off a cliff at exactly the moment it should cross. The stage sequence is right; the plotted point is wrong.

FALSE, as labelled — the “1.38 M☉” Type Ia white dwarf starts at 1.08 M☉. The preset is named “Chandrasekhar WD · 1.38 M☉ Binary,” but the engine's accreting white dwarf begins, measured, at 1.08 M☉ and only reaches 1.38 halfway through the accretion stage, then 1.44 at detonation. The physics that follows is correct — runaway carbon fusion, total disruption, no remnant left — but 1.38 is a waypoint in this engine, not the starting mass the label says it is.

1 · Main-sequence lifetime vs mass

The engine does not use the textbook power law directly. It computes a luminosity from a piecewise mass–luminosity relation (next section) and then sets the lifetime from the fuel-over-burn-rate identity:

life = 10 Gyr × M / L M and L in solar units. At M = L = 1 this gives the Sun's 10 Gyr by construction — and the audit confirmed the engine returns exactly 10.00 Gyr there.

Measured against the standard scaling the lesson set out to test, t ≈ 1010 yr × (M/M☉)−2.5:

Read fairly: from 0.2 to 15 M☉ the engine sits within about ±40% of the M−2.5 scaling — good for a teaching exhibit, and the Sun is exact. The drift at the ends is not an arithmetic slip; it is the piecewise luminosity law showing through. Real stars do flatten at high mass (radiation pressure and the approach to the Eddington limit bend L away from M3.5), so a 32 M☉ star really does live longer than the naive power law says — the page's ~4 Myr is in fact nearer modern values than 1.7 Myr is. At the low end the opposite holds: fully convective dwarfs can burn nearly all their hydrogen, which should push lifetimes up toward the power-law figure, and the engine's 868 Gyr at 0.1 M☉ is if anything conservative.

2 · The luminosity–mass relation actually shipped

There is no single L ∝ M3.5 in this code. The shipped relation is piecewise, and the audit recovered it exactly by dividing the measured L by M3.5:

L = 0.23 M2.3 (M < 0.43) · M3.9 (M < 2) · 1.45 M3.45 (M < 20) · 32,000 (M/20)2 (M ≥ 20) Measured L / M3.5: 3.65 at 0.1 M☉, 1.00 at 1, 1.27 at 15, 0.64 at 25, 0.32 at 40 — a single M3.5 law would give 1.00 everywhere.

That is a bend in the honest direction: the piecewise form resembles the empirical relations in textbooks (shallow for fully convective dwarfs, steep near a Sun, flattening for the most massive stars) more closely than the one power law it replaces. The companion radius law (R ∝ M0.8 below a Sun, M0.57 above) and Teff from the Stefan–Boltzmann law, T ∝ (L/R²)¼, give measured main-sequence temperatures of 2,674 K at 0.1 M☉, 5,778 K at 1, 9,997 K at 2.2 and 34,523 K at 25 — all plausible for their classes.

The shapes are the textbook shapes. The red dwarf stays low and right — cool, dim, essentially parked — for hundreds of billions of years, then moves up and left as a blue dwarf instead of climbing the giant branch, which is the correct, counter-intuitive result for a star too light to ignite helium. The Sun climbs the giant branch twice (RGB and AGB) with a horizontal-branch rest between, as it should. The 15 M☉ track crosses to the red supergiant corner and stays there through every advanced burning stage — from outside, a supergiant looks almost unchanged in its last million years, and the page honours that: its surface holds ~3,300 K while its core runs from 550 MK to 3,800 MK. The 25 M☉ track never goes red at all: mass loss strips it to a hot Wolf–Rayet point on the far left of the diagram. That divergence at high mass is real behaviour, not a bug.

The Chandrasekhar limit — 1.44 M☉, used twice, once exactly and once approximately

The value quoted everywhere on the page is 1.44 M☉ — the correct Chandrasekhar mass. Its Type Ia use is exact: a degenerate C–O white dwarf supported by electron degeneracy pressure really does run away as it approaches this mass, and the engine drives its dwarf 1.08 → 1.38 → 1.44 M☉ before detonating it (see the labelling flag in the verdict). Its second use — “the inert iron core hits the 1.44 M☉ Chandrasekhar limit” in a massive star — is the standard teaching approximation: an iron core's effective Chandrasekhar mass is somewhat lower (entropy and composition corrections put collapse nearer ~1.2–1.4 M☉), but 1.44 is the number every textbook reaches for first, and the page is in that company. One honest caveat: no iron-core mass is ever computed by the engine — the measured stellar mass during silicon burning at 15 M☉ is still 11.1 M☉ total, and the “1.44” in that stage lives in the fuel label and narrative, not in a state variable. The claim is true to physics; it is not a measurement the page itself makes.

The TOV limit — named, and the neutron-star masses respect it

For black-hole formation the page invokes the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (~2.2 M☉ in its hypermassive fuel label — inside the real, still-uncertain ~2–2.3 M☉ range). Consistently, its neutron-star remnants are clamped to at most 2.05 M☉, so no page pulsar ever exceeds the limit the page quotes. The bend is the cliff: at 21.9 M☉ initial the remnant is a 2.01 M☉ neutron star; at 22.0 the remnant is a 6.16 M☉ black hole. Real collapse outcomes in that neighbourhood are genuinely uncertain — fallback can make either — so treat the line as a teaching boundary, not a prediction for any individual star.

Where it bends

The progress slider is a stage dial, not a clock. Measured on the Sun: by p = 0.42 — less than halfway along the slider — the star is already 9.0 Gyr old, 90% of its main-sequence life spent. The massive star's entire onion phase (p 0.54–0.78) covers only the last 1.5% of its age, and silicon burning still gets a generous slice of the dial for its single day. This is the right choice for teaching — linear time would make every post-main-sequence stage invisible — but a student reading the “Lifecycle %” bar as elapsed time will be misled, and the bar is labelled as a percentage of the lifecycle without saying which.

Several end-stage numbers are hard-coded, so different masses converge to identical remnants on screen. Measured: every red dwarf, 0.1 or 0.5 M☉, enters its white-dwarf stage at exactly 16,000 K, 0.060 R☉ and 0.212 L☉ (with a temperature jump from ~7,800 K the step before); every neutron star cools 900,000 → 220,000 K at the same luminosity; every black hole is assigned Teff = 18,000 K — a property a black hole does not have; the 18,000 K belongs to its accretion disk, which the narrative does describe. And in the hypermassive onion stage, 25 and 32 M☉ produce identical temperatures, radii and luminosities (48,000–62,000 K, 18–28 R☉, ~4.3×106 L☉): the formulas there contain no mass at all.

The Type Ia remnant keeps a luminosity a disrupted star shouldn't have. Long after “No Core Left,” the engine still reports 50,000 L☉ and a “radius” of 2,200 R☉ — that radius is the expanding ejecta shell, and plotting it as a stellar H–R point is a display convention, as it also is (more defensibly) during the supernova stages themselves.

Sirius gets a helium flash it probably shouldn't. The page's flash/ignition threshold is 2.25 M☉, so the 2.2 M☉ Sirius preset flashes. The real boundary is near ~2–2.3 M☉, so this is borderline rather than wrong — but the preset's own narrative text for the stage describes the smooth-ignition case, while the stage title over it says “Helium Flash!”

Small narrative overreaches. The pulsar narrative credits the supernova remnant with “newly forged gold” — the r-process in core-collapse supernovae may contribute, but most cosmic gold is now attributed to neutron-star mergers. The red-giant narrative has the Sun engulfing Mercury and Venus at 168 R☉ (0.78 AU) — Venus at 0.72 AU is a marginal call the measured radius does support, Earth at 1 AU is (correctly) not claimed. The Betelgeuse preset is 15 M☉; the real Betelgeuse is usually put somewhat higher, ~16–19 M☉, though its mass is genuinely uncertain.

What is not bent. The p–p chain below ~1.35 M☉ and CNO above it, the fully convective red dwarf, degeneracy and the helium flash, double-shell AGB burning, neutrino-accelerated late stages, total disruption in a Type Ia, and a Schwarzschild radius computed as 2.95 km per solar mass for the black-hole remnants — all measured in the engine or read in its labels, and all correct.
