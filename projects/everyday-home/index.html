Everyday Home

A house is a system: water in, water out, power in circuits, heat in a loop, drains running downhill. You don't need to be a tradesperson to live in one well — you need to know what the system is doing, where its shutoffs are, and which checks are worth doing before you call someone. Three benches: the breaker, the water, and the honest triage.

Help ▾ — about this workshop

What this is. Three teaching benches about a house's systems. The breaker bench adds up real appliance wattages on one 120-volt, 15-amp circuit. The water bench rehearses the first move for four leaks. The triage bench walks the checks a good technician runs first — the ones that cost nothing.

Safety lines — these outrank the benches. Smell gas? Leave the house and call the gas company or 911 from outside; don't flip switches on the way out. Water near live electrics: keep clear, and only touch a breaker if you can do it dry-handed and dry-footed. Never mix drain cleaners, and never use one after another has failed — the mixture is the hazard. Anything inside a panel beyond flipping a breaker, anything gas beyond a thermostat, anything structural: that's a professional's job, and calling one is the correct move, not a defeat.

Honest limits. Wattages here are typical nameplate figures; your appliance's label is the authority for your circuit. Houses differ — shutoff locations, panel layouts, heating types — so the benches teach the shape of each system and where to look, not your house's map. Drawing your own map is the homework, and it's genuinely worth an afternoon.

Bench 1 — The breaker

One circuit, everything plugged into it

A standard kitchen circuit: 120 volts × 15 amps = 1,800 watts, total, for everything on it at once. Switch appliances on and watch the load. This is why the space heater and the microwave can't share — it's not the house being difficult, it's arithmetic with a switch in it.

Bench 2 — Stop the water

Four leaks. For each one, pick your first move — before the bucket, before the towels, before the phone. Score: 0 / 4

Under the kitchen sink: a supply line splits and water is spraying the cabinet, hard.

First: the fixture valve. Almost every sink and toilet has its own shutoff right there — under the sink, beside the toilet. Seconds of water, not minutes. Then towels, then the call if the valve itself failed. Water damage is measured in minutes of flow; the valve is what buys them back.

The water heater lets go at the base and is flooding the utility room. Its own valves are corroded stiff.

First: the main shutoff. Usually where the water line enters the house — basement wall, crawl space, or near the meter. When a fixture valve won't serve, the main stops everything. Find yours on a calm day and tag it; the middle of a flood is the worst classroom. The street meter is the utility's, and opening faucets just gives the flood more company.

The toilet is overflowing onto the bathroom floor, bowl still rising.

First: the valve behind the toilet, at the wall, usually a quarter-turn or an oval handle. It stops the refill, which is what's rising. Flushing adds a tankful to the problem; plunging is step two, after the water stops coming in.

A pipe bursts in the wall upstairs — water is coming through the ceiling and you can't see any valve near it.

First: the main shutoff — when you can't find the local valve, the main is the answer, every time. Then move what's under the drip, then photos, then calls. (A small hole in bulging, water-heavy ceiling drywall is a real technique — after the water is off, and it's a judgement call, not a first move.)
