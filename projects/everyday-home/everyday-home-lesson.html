The claim

A house's systems on three benches: circuit arithmetic (120 V × 15 A = 1,800 W, with the 80% continuous-load planning line at 1,440 W), four leak scenarios whose correct first move is always a shutoff, and four triage checklists in cheap-to-free order, each ending in an honest call-it verdict.

What was checked

  • The breaker arithmetic. The audit drove the shipped toggles: the heater and microwave together read 2,700 W and trip the bench; the reset is refused while the load stays over the rating and accepted after a load is shed. The first drive caught a real defect — the toggles froze while tripped, so a load could never be shed and the breaker could never be reset; the shipped bench lets you switch things off while tripped (never on), which is also how the real panel wants to be treated. Wattages are typical nameplate figures (a 1,500 W heater and hair dryer, a 1,200 W microwave), each labelled as drawn from the appliance's own class.
  • The water answers. Each scenario's stored answer was checked against standard practice and its printed tell: fixture valves exist at sinks and toilets; the main shutoff is the correct first move when a fixture valve is unavailable or has failed. All four answered correctly score 4 / 4, and cards lock after one answer.
  • The triage lists. Each checklist follows the order a technician's first questions take — power and controls before components, airflow before sealed systems, one drain versus the whole house as the fork that changes the problem. The "call now" lines name their red flags explicitly (a breaker that re-trips instantly, burning smells, whole-house drainage).

What bends

Your house is not the average house. Shutoff locations, panel schedules, and heating types vary by era and region; some toilets and sinks genuinely lack fixture valves, which is why the main shutoff appears twice in four scenarios. The benches teach where to look, and say so.

Wattages are typical, not yours. A microwave can be 900 W or 1,500 W; the label on the appliance governs your circuit. The bench's lesson — heating elements dominate, and two of them rarely share a circuit — survives every nameplate.

The triage lists are first checks, not a diagnosis. They end where they should: several verdicts are "call, and say what you've ruled out." A checklist that never says that would be lying about houses.
