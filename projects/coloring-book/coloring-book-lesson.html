The Coloring Book — the lesson

The front door is a colouring book: twelve picture pages, a box of 24 crayons, and a way to turn your own photo into an outline you can colour — then print, either blank for real crayons or finished to hang up. This page is the back door: what the colouring is quietly teaching while it feels like play, and how the machine does its two tricks — finding the edges of a photograph, and filling a patch without spilling.

The real curriculum: aim

Clicking inside a patch of the picture is a targeting task. Your hand has to move a pointer across a distance and land it inside a boundary — and the smaller the patch, the harder that is. That trade is not a feeling; it was measured in 1954 by Paul Fitts, and it is called Fitts's law:

movement time = a + b × log2( distance ÷ target width + 1 )

The time to hit a target grows as the distance grows, and shrinks as the target gets wider. Double the distance or halve the patch and the tap gets measurably slower and less certain — for children and for fighter pilots alike.

That is why the twelve pages are graded the way they are. The Little hands pages — Rocket, Robot Buddy, Robot Pup — are built from 13 or 14 big, forgiving patches. The Getting steady pages (Baby Bot, Bulldog, Fish, Butterfly) carry 19 to 21 medium patches. The Fine detail pages (Train, Dinosaur, Owl, Submarine, Castle) hold 27 to 31 patches, some of them small enough that landing inside one is a genuine shot. Nobody is graded and nothing is timed: the practice is the progression itself, big targets first, and the miss — the wrong patch filling — is feedback a child reads instantly, with no adult required to explain it.

This collection keeps a soft spot for lessons that arrive through the back door. Steven Powell, who curates Everyday Cool, puts it this way: a generation that had never touched a mouse learned drag-and-drop from Solitaire and learned the mouse buttons from Minesweeper — the training was real because the game was. This book's fills belong to the same family: they teach aim to hands that already tap, one patch at a time.

How a photo becomes an outline

Five steps, each one simple, all of them running on your own device — the picture is never uploaded anywhere. The first version of this bench ran only steps 1, 3 and a single-bar version of step 4, and a real family photo showed why that wasn't enough: a stone wall and a hedge behind the people turned into thousands of speckled lines, while the soft outline of a face — the line that mattered most — fell under the same bar that was supposed to calm the stones down. The five steps below are the fix, a classic arrangement called a Canny-style detector.

1. Grayscale — forget the colour, keep the brightness

Every pixel is replaced by a single brightness number, mixing the red, green and blue it arrived with (roughly 30% red, 59% green, 11% blue — green counts most because your eyes are most sensitive to it). Shape lives in brightness; colour would only be noise for what comes next.

2. Blur — calm the grain before judging it

First, the picture is softened with a Gaussian blur — every pixel averaged with its neighbours, the near ones counting most. Fine grain lives at the smallest scale: the pocks in a stone, the leaves on a hedge, the weave of a shirt. Blurring melts that grain toward its own average while barely touching the big, slow changes — a shoulder against the sky — that carry the real shapes. The Detail slider sets the blur: toward Simple it strengthens (calmer pages, best for busy photos); toward Detailed it lightens (finer lines survive).

3. Sobel edges — find where brightness changes fast

An edge is a place where brightness changes fast: the side of a dark dog against a pale wall, the rim of a cup on a table. The Sobel operator visits every pixel and asks two questions — how fast is brightness changing sideways? and how fast up and down? — by comparing the pixel with its eight neighbours, the near ones counting double. A flat patch of sky scores zero both ways. A border scores high. The length of the two answers together is that pixel's edge strength, and their angle is the edge's direction — both are kept, because the next step needs the direction.

4. Thinning, then two bars — keep real contours, drop speckle

Thinning (non-maximum suppression) walks each edge and keeps only the strongest pixel across it — the one-pixel-wide ridge down the middle — so contours come out as clean single lines instead of smears. Then comes the step that separates a faint-but-real line from background speckle, called hysteresis, and it uses two bars instead of one. A pixel stronger than the high bar — the Line strength slider — is a definite line. A pixel between the two bars is a maybe, and a maybe counts only if it is connected to a definite line: a faint line counts if it holds hands with a strong one. The soft curve of a cheek usually touches a stronger stretch of the same outline somewhere, so it survives whole. One pebble's edge in a stone wall holds hands with nothing, so it drops away. In the workshop's own check on a grainy test picture, the stony background went from 12,031 stray line pixels under the old single-bar method to none — while the smooth shape in front of the stones kept its outline.

5. Sealing — close the fences before the paint arrives

Last, every line is grown by one pixel in all directions. A hairline gap in a contour — one pixel where the line blinked — is enough for the paint-bucket fill to leak through and flood the whole page, so the lines are sealed shut first. The sealed version is the one the book displays, prints and fills against, which is why printed lines may look a touch bolder than the raw edges the detector found. In the workshop's check, a drawn ring with a deliberate one-pixel gap let the fill paint 896 squares outside it; sealed, it painted none.

The paint-bucket walk

Filling a patch on a photo page is flood fill — the paint-bucket tool every drawing program has, and a first piece of real computer science hiding in plain sight. Start where you tapped. Paint that square. Then look at the squares next door: any square the same colour as where you started (close enough — the book allows a small tolerance, so a slightly shaded edge still counts as the same patch) joins the patch and gets painted, and its neighbours get checked in turn. The fill walks outward square by square until every direction is fenced off by a line or by a different colour. It is a graph walk — the same family of walking that routes a packet across a network or a robot across a floor — wearing an apron.

The workshop proof walked it against a drawn box: the fill coloured exactly the 216 squares inside a one-square-thick wall and not one square outside it. And a tap that lands on a line itself fills nothing at all — the walk refuses to start on a fence.

Honest edges. A converted outline is only as clean as the photo's contrast. The blur-and-hysteresis pipeline narrows the gap between a clean photo and a busy one — texture is calmed, faint connected contours survive — but it does not close the gap. A busy photo still makes a busier outline, because many of those texture borders are real edges honestly reported, and no step in the pipeline can know which lines you meant. The two sliders are the fix we offer, not a cure: a shape whose outline never once reaches the high bar, and touches nothing that does, will still be lost. And the sealing step grows every line by a pixel, so converted lines sit slightly bolder than the edges themselves — the price of fences that don't leak.

Two different engines, on purpose. The twelve preloaded pages are hand-built vector shapes — every patch is one clean closed outline, filled exactly. Your photo is a raster, a grid of squares, so its patches are found by the flood-fill walk instead. The vector pages will always be tidier; the photo pages will always be yours. The book keeps both because they are good at different things.
