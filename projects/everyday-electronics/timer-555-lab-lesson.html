555 Timer Lab — the lesson

The exhibit opens up the most famous chip in electronics — the NE555, in production since 1972 — and lets you watch its insides work: two comparators, a flip-flop, a discharge transistor, and three 5 kΩ resistors, all arguing over one capacitor. Underneath the animation is a single, beautiful idea: a resistor and a capacitor charging toward a rail, stopped at two fixed fractions of the supply, make a clock whose speed does not care what the supply voltage is. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the physics, the math, the worked answers, and an audit of the exhibit itself: every number below was measured by driving the shipped page's own code headlessly, not typed in from the formulas it is checked against.

What this is

Inside every 555, three matched 5 kΩ resistors divide the supply into two reference voltages: ⅔ Vcc and ⅓ Vcc (the three 5s are the classic lore behind the name). A threshold comparator watches the timing capacitor against the upper reference; a trigger comparator watches it (or pin 2) against the lower one. Between them sits an SR flip-flop. The whole chip is a referee for one exponential:

  • Charging. The capacitor charges through the resistors toward Vcc. The instant VC crosses ⅔ Vcc, the threshold comparator resets the flip-flop: the output goes low and the discharge transistor turns on.
  • Discharging. The capacitor discharges through R₂ into pin 7. The instant VC falls to ⅓ Vcc, the trigger comparator sets the flip-flop again: output high, discharge off, and the cycle repeats.
  • Monostable. Wire it so the capacitor starts empty and only charges when triggered, and the same referee produces one clean pulse per trigger instead of a clock.

Because both trip points are fractions of Vcc, the supply cancels out of the timing math — the audit below confirms the exhibit honours that: its frequency is bit-identical at 5 V, 9 V, 12 V and 15 V.

The timing math

An RC charge from ⅓ Vcc to ⅔ Vcc always takes ln 2 ≈ 0.693 time constants, whatever the supply — the exponential needs the same fraction of the remaining distance every time. That one fact generates every 555 formula:

tH = 0.693 · (R₁ + R₂) · C   ·   tL = 0.693 · R₂ · C Charging goes through R₁ + R₂; discharging only through R₂ (pin 7 is a switch to ground). That asymmetry is why the plain astable's high time is always the longer half.
f = 1.44 / ((R₁ + 2R₂) · C) The TI NE555 datasheet form. The 1.44 is itself a rounding of 1/ln 2 = 1.4427 — keep that in mind when the audit shows a steady +0.19% "error" against it: the exhibit computes with exact logarithms, so most of that gap is the datasheet's rounding, not the exhibit's.
Duty = (R₁ + R₂) / (R₁ + 2R₂) Always above 50% in the plain circuit: as R₁ → 0 it approaches 50% but never reaches or crosses it. Getting below 50% needs the bypass diode (charge through R₁ alone, duty = R₁/(R₁ + R₂)) or pin-5 control — both are in the exhibit, and both are measured below.
Tmonostable = 1.1 · R · C Charging from 0 to ⅔ Vcc takes ln 3 ≈ 1.0986 time constants; the datasheet prints 1.1. The audit drove the exhibit's engine and found it uses ln 3 = 1.098612 exactly — the printed "1.1" on the page is the rounded label, the engine underneath is exact.

How the audit measured it

The shipped page's script was extracted and run in Node with the DOM and canvas stubbed out. The audit set R₁, R₂ and C through the exhibit's own state, in both modes, and read two independent things: the values its computeTheoreticalMetrics() returns, and the actual simulated waveform produced by its event-driven stepper stepPhysics() — edge times were read off the scope samples the stepper stamps at the exact crossing instants. Both were then compared against the TI datasheet formulas, which the exhibit's code never consults. One honest caveat: the stepper advances the capacitor with the exact analytical exponential and snaps to the crossing time, so simulated-vs-theory agreement near 0.000% is partly by construction. The check that is not by construction is exhibit-vs-datasheet — and that is the one reported as the verdict.

False claims — flagged prominently

FALSE — the "50%" badge lies whenever the diode is on and R₁ ≠ R₂. Turning on the bypass diode renames the mode badge to "Astable 50%" (and the mode note to "DIODE 50%") unconditionally. With the Blinker resistors (10 kΩ / 68 kΩ) and the diode on, the measured duty is 12.82%, not 50%. The 50% figure is true only for the preset's equal resistors (47 kΩ / 47 kΩ, measured 50.000000%). Move either resistor slider with the diode on and the badge keeps claiming a number the waveform no longer has.

FALSE in a stated case — the Trigger button does not always deliver the "precision 1.1·R₁·C pulse" the page promises. The button (and the on-canvas pin-2 button, and the monostable preset's welcome shot) holds pin 2 low for a fixed 0.16 s, and the exhibit's latch gives the trigger priority over the threshold for as long as it is held — exactly like a real 555 with its trigger held down. Measured through the page's own button: with RC = 1 ms the output pulse is 160.0 ms instead of 1.10 ms (≈145× long) and the capacitor charges all the way to 9.00 V — the full supply, straight past the ⅔ threshold; with RC = 0.1 s the pulse is 160.0 ms instead of 109.9 ms and VC peaks at 7.17 V. The printed formula holds only when 1.1·RC is longer than the 0.16 s hold (the shipped preset, RC = 1 s, is unaffected: 1.099 s measured) or when the trigger is released quickly, as in the audit's short-trigger runs above. The tooltip's unconditional "fire a single precision output pulse of T = 1.1·R₁·C" does not say any of that.

Rounded names, flagged so nobody quotes them as specs. The "1 Hz LED Blinker" preset runs at 0.988 Hz (−1.2% against its name) and the "Fast Astable" preset, subtitled "10 Hz brisk clock wave", runs at 10.232 Hz (+2.3%). These are friendly labels, not errors in the engine — the values in the tables above are what the presets actually do. The "440 Hz" synth preset, by contrast, earns its name: 439.85 Hz, −0.03%.

Bends named honestly

Analytic engine, not a numerical integrator. The stepper advances VC with the exact exponential solution and computes each crossing time in closed form. That is why simulated periods land on theory to 0.000% — it is a strength (no integration drift, razor-sharp scope edges at 440 Hz) but it means "simulated" here verifies the implementation against the datasheet, not against an independent physics model. The exhibit's own scope samples are where the edge times above were read.

Ideal diode. The bypass diode D₁ charges the capacitor through R₁ alone with no forward voltage drop. A real diode's ~0.7 V drop changes the effective charge target and shifts the duty a little, especially at a 5 V supply. The exhibit's 50.000000% symmetry is the ideal-diode number.

Ideal switches, two different models. In astable discharge, pin 7 is a perfect short: τ = R₂·C exactly, no saturation voltage. In monostable, the same transistor is modelled as a fixed 18 Ω saturation resistance, and the capacitor dumps all the way to ~0 V (measured 0.00 V shortly after the pulse) rather than stopping at ⅓ Vcc — which is correct behaviour for monostable wiring, since pin 2 is the button there, not the capacitor.

Rail-to-rail output. The exhibit's pin 3 swings the full 0 to Vcc. A real bipolar NE555's high output sits noticeably below Vcc under load. Cosmetic here — it drives an LED drawing and a synth, not a real load — but the telemetry's "HIGH (9V)" is the ideal number.

The pin-5 slider is inert in plain astable. Unless the "Pin 5 External Override" toggle is on (or PWM mode is selected), moving the Vctrl slider changes nothing: the thresholds stay at ⅔ and ⅓ Vcc. That matches a floating pin 5 with its bypass capacitor, but the slider does not tell you it is disconnected.

No tolerances, no temperature, no leakage. Resistors and capacitors are exactly their labelled values forever. A bench 555 circuit built with 5% parts will land within a few percent of these numbers, not on them — the exhibit is the datasheet's typical-values world.
