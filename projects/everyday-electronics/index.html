Electronics.

Electricity obeys one small law, and this room is built on it. Start with a circuit you can switch on and off while the sums happen in front of you. Work out any voltage, current, or resistance from the other two. Find the right resistor to keep an LED alive. Then meet the room's centrepiece: a radio with no battery at all — the crystal set — how it works, and how to build one, plus a fruit battery, a paper circuit, and a throwie for the bench.

01 / THE CIRCUIT BENCH

One loop, one law.

Every circuit in this room is a loop. The battery pushes, the bulb resists, and the current is whatever the push and the resistance settle on: current = volts ÷ resistance. Move the sliders and watch the bulb. Open the switch and the loop breaks — no loop, no flow, no light.

The circuit is warming up.

The little dots are the flow, drawn the way textbooks draw it (from + around to −); their speed follows the current. The bulb's brightness is a picture scale — about 3 watts, a small torch bulb's worth, reads as fully bright. Real bulbs also change their resistance as they heat up; this bench keeps the resistance you set, and says so.

02 / THE SUMS BENCHES

Ohm's law, and keeping LEDs alive.

The Ohm's law bench

Voltage, current, and resistance are locked together: V = I × R. Fill in any two and the bench works out the third, showing its working. Leave exactly one box empty — that empty one is the question.

Fill in two boxes and press the button.

The LED resistor bench

An LED is a one-way door that keeps a fixed voltage for itself — and will eat far too much current and die if it meets a battery bare. The resistor drops the leftover voltage and limits the flow. Pick the LED colour (or type its forward voltage), say what is feeding it, and the bench finds the resistor, showing every step.

Give it a supply and press the button.

Forward voltages here are the typical datasheet values (red ≈ 2.0 V, blue and white ≈ 3.2 V). Your LED's packet is the authority for your LED.

03 / HOW A SIMPLE RADIO WORKS

The crystal radio: a radio with no battery.

Follow the signal from left to right. This is the whole machine — five parts, and not one of them is a power supply.

  1. The antenna catches everything at once. Every station in the air is shaking the wire at the same time, all mixed together. The wire also catches the tiniest dribble of energy from those waves — remember that dribble; it is the whole power supply.
  2. The coil and capacitor pick one station. A coil and a variable capacitor together resonate — they happily pass one frequency and shrug off the rest, the way one swing in a row answers when you push at its own rhythm. Turning the capacitor (or sliding a contact along the coil) changes which frequency resonates. That is tuning: not reaching out and grabbing a station, but agreeing to listen to exactly one.
  3. The detector keeps half the wave. The radio wave wiggles both ways, far too fast to hear, and the wiggle's shape carries the voice. The crystal — or its modern stand-in, a 1N34A germanium diode — is a one-way door: it lets the wave through in one direction only, keeping half of it. What survives wobbles at the speed of the voice instead of the speed of the wave.
  4. The earpiece turns it back into sound. Those voice-shaped wobbles push and pull the earpiece's diaphragm, and you hear the broadcast. Because the only energy available is the dribble the antenna caught, the earpiece must be a sensitive high-impedance kind (a crystal or ceramic earpiece) that can whisper on almost nothing — ordinary earbuds and speakers need far more power than a radio wave delivers.
  5. There is no battery — that is the point. The station's transmitter pays the entire energy bill. Your radio is a very clever listener living on crumbs, which is why the sound is faint, why a long antenna and a good ground matter so much, and why it works in a blackout, forever, for free.

04 / BUILD PLANS

Four builds for the bench.

Nothing in this section plugs into a wall. The strongest battery here is a 9-volt or a handful of fruit; the crystal radio uses no battery at all. Sharp tools, outdoor wires, and coin cells carry their own safety lines on each card — read them before starting, not after.

Build a crystal radio

The 3-penny radio's big brother — a real radio, no battery

You need: a wide cardboard tube (an oatmeal canister is ideal); about 30 m (100 ft) of thin enamelled copper (magnet) wire; a 1N34A germanium diode; a variable capacitor of about 365 pF if you can find one (salvaged from a dead AM radio is the classic source) — or skip it and tune with a sliding contact instead; a high-impedance crystal or ceramic earpiece (ordinary earbuds will not do); a wooden board to mount everything on; sandpaper; tape; screws or clips; a long wire for the antenna (10–20 m) and a way to ground (a clamp on a cold-water pipe, or a ground rod).

  1. Sand two thin lines of enamel off the tube's winding area later — first, wind the coil: about 100 neat turns of the magnet wire side by side on the tube, taping the ends down and leaving tails of wire at both ends. Keep the turns touching, not overlapping.
  2. Mount the tube on the board. Gently sand a straight line along the top of the coil, through the enamel, so a sliding contact can touch any turn — that slider is your tuning if you have no variable capacitor. (With a capacitor, wire it across the two ends of the coil instead, and skip the slider.)
  3. Wire the antenna to one end of the coil. Wire the other end of the coil to your ground.
  4. From the antenna end of the coil (or from the slider), wire to one leg of the diode. From the diode's other leg, wire to the earpiece, and from the earpiece back to the ground wire. Diodes are one-way: if it stays silent, try the diode the other way round before suspecting anything else.
  5. Put the earpiece in, wait for evening if you can (distant AM stations travel much further after dark), and move the slider or turn the capacitor very slowly. The sound is faint and only in one ear. That faintness is physics, not failure — the station is powering your radio from kilometres away.

What it shows: the whole of section 03 with your own hands — resonance choosing one station, a one-way detector keeping half the wave, and a radio wave's own energy doing all the work.

The 3-penny / foxhole idea: in the Second World War, soldiers built "foxhole radios" from scrap — a coil, an earpiece from a damaged set, and a detector made of a rusty razor blade with a pencil lead (or a safety pin) pressed on it. The blade's surface and the graphite point form a crude crystal detector, the same one-way door as our diode, found by hunting for a sensitive spot. Pocket-money builds in the same spirit picked up the "3-penny" nickname — the detector cost pennies. It works, barely, and finding the good spot can take all evening: the 1N34A is the same idea made reliable, which is why it is the practical version here.

Safety: an antenna wire must never go anywhere near power lines — look up first, and keep every wire well clear of them. Never put an antenna up, and disconnect the lead-in before, any thunderstorm — a long wire and lightning are a dangerous pair. A grown-up helps with any outdoor wire, any ladder, and the ground connection.

The fruit battery

Run an LED from an orange

You need: 3–4 oranges (lemons or potatoes work the same way — one fruit is one cell); for each fruit, one copper piece (a copper coin, or a short length of thick bare copper wire) and one galvanized nail — galvanized means coated in zinc, and the zinc is the point; one red LED; a few short wires, with clips if you have them. A multimeter is optional and very satisfying.

  1. Roll each orange firmly on the table, pressing gently. This breaks the juice sacs inside so the juice — the acid electrolyte — can flow and touch the metals.
  2. Push the copper piece and the galvanized nail into each orange, a few centimetres apart. The two metals must not touch each other inside the fruit.
  3. If you have a meter, measure one orange: about 0.9 volts. Real electricity — but a red LED needs roughly 2 volts before it lights at all, so one orange alone will not light it. That is not the build failing. That is the next step announcing itself.
  4. Wire the fruits in a chain — in series: copper of the first orange to the nail of the second, copper of the second to the nail of the third. Three oranges give about 2.7 volts; four, about 3.6. In series, each cell's push adds to the last.
  5. Connect the two free ends to the LED: the free copper end to the LED's longer leg (the + side), the free nail end to the shorter leg. The LED glows — gently. A fruit battery's current is tiny, so expect a glow in a dim room, not a torch beam.

What it shows: the fruit's acid reacts with the two metals differently — the reaction frees electrons at the zinc and they travel through your wires to the copper. Chemical energy has become electrical energy, the same trade every battery makes. And it shows series in your hands: one cell, 0.9 volts and darkness; three cells, the pushes added, and light.

Safety: fruit that has hosted a battery is for looking, not eating — the metals leave traces in it. Wash hands afterwards. Galvanized nails are sharp; a grown-up pushes them in for younger builders.

The paper circuit

Draw a circuit, then make it real

You need: a sheet of card or thick paper; copper tape (foil tape with conductive adhesive is easiest; plain aluminium foil strips and glue will do); one CR2032 coin cell; one LED; tape or a small binder clip.

  1. Draw your circuit on the card first, in pencil: a loop, with a gap marked "battery" and a gap marked "LED". Drawing it first is not a formality — it is the design step every engineer does.
  2. Lay copper tape along the drawn lines, folding it round corners rather than cutting it (a cut corner can break the path; a fold keeps it whole). Leave the two gaps open.
  3. Flatten the LED's legs and tape them across its gap, one leg on each side: the longer leg must be on the side that will meet the battery's + face. An LED is a one-way street — backwards, it simply will not light, and nothing is harmed; turn it round.
  4. Set the coin cell into its gap with its + face (the marked side) meeting the tape that runs to the LED's long leg, and hold or clip it down. The loop is complete, and the LED lights.
  5. Add a switch the paper way: leave one stretch of tape on a folded flap of card, so folding the flap down presses tape to tape and closes the loop.

What it shows: a circuit is a loop and nothing more mystical — it can be drawn flat on a greetings card. It also shows polarity (the LED and the battery both have a right way round) and switching (a switch is just a respectable gap). One honest exception to know: a coin cell can drive an LED with no resistor because the cell's own internal resistance limits the current — it is a small, weak source. Any stronger battery plays by the LED bench's rules: resistor, every time.

Safety: coin cells are a serious swallowing hazard for small children — keep them out of reach, tape or clip the cell firmly into the card, and let a grown-up be in charge of the cells.

The LED throwie

A light that sticks — and a pocket torch without the magnet

You need: one LED (a bright one is fun); one CR2032 coin cell; strong tape; a small strong magnet if you want it to stick to steel. That is the entire parts list.

  1. Look at the cell: the marked face is +. Slide the LED's longer leg onto the + face and its shorter leg onto the − face, so the cell is sandwiched between the legs. The LED lights the moment both legs touch.
  2. Squeeze and tape tightly round the legs and cell, so the sandwich cannot slip.
  3. Tape the magnet to the back if you are making the sticking kind, then throw it gently at something steel — a fridge door earns its keep here. Without the magnet, the same sandwich is a pinch torch: squeeze for light, let go for dark.
  4. Expect it to glow for days, dimming slowly. When it dies, the cell is spent — take it to a battery recycling point, and build another.

What it shows: polarity again (it only works one way round), and why the light lasts: the current is a trickle, so the cell's stored energy drains slowly. It also quietly teaches the coin-cell exception from the paper circuit — the cell's own internal resistance is the LED's only protection, which is exactly why this trick belongs to coin cells and not to bigger batteries.

Safety: coin cells and small magnets are both swallowing hazards — grown-ups keep charge of them around small children. Collect throwies back afterwards; batteries do not belong in hedges or soil.

06 / HOW ELECTRICITY WORKS HERE

Five ideas that run the whole room.

The loop rule

No loop, no flow

Current only flows round a complete loop from the battery and back to it. Every switch, every dead torch, every "it just stopped working" is this rule in costume: somewhere, the loop is open. Half of all fault-finding is asking where.

Circuits are circles

Push and resistance

Volts push; ohms resist

Voltage is the push, resistance is everything that pushes back, and current is the result: I = V ÷ R. Double the push, double the flow. Double the resistance, half the flow. The benches above are this one sentence with numbers on.

One law, three faces: V = I × R

Series and parallel

Chains add pushes; branches share

Cells in series — nose to tail, like the oranges — add their voltages: 0.9 + 0.9 + 0.9 lights the LED. Resistors in series add their resistance. Things in parallel share the same voltage and split the current between them, which is why house lights can be switched one at a time.

Series adds; parallel shares

Polarity

Some parts are one-way streets

Batteries have + and −. LEDs light only with their long leg toward +. Diodes — including the crystal radio's detector — pass current one way only. Getting a part backwards usually means silence, not damage, at this room's voltages: turn it round and try again.

Backwards usually means "not yet"

Power becomes heat

Why resistors get warm

Power is volts × current, and in a resistor every watt of it becomes heat. That is why the LED bench checks the resistor's power as well as its value, why we round resistor values up (less current, less heat, LED safe), and why a short circuit — a loop with almost no resistance — is dangerous with a strong battery: enormous current, all of it turning to heat at once.

P = V × I, and it all lands somewhere

07 / HELP

Help — how Everyday Electronics works.

The circuit bench

Sliders, switch, and dots

Set the battery voltage (1.5–12 V) and the bulb's resistance with the sliders; the drawing redraws, the bulb's brightness follows the power (volts × current), and the result box shows both sums in words. The switch button opens or closes the loop. Nothing you set is kept or sent anywhere.

Brightness follows power

The Ohm's law bench

Any two, get the third

Fill in exactly two of voltage, current, resistance and press the button; the bench solves the empty one and shows the working, then writes out the full set so you can check it backwards. It refuses — in plain words — one box, three boxes, zeros, and non-numbers, because each of those would make the sum meaningless rather than merely hard.

The bench shows its working

The LED resistor bench

Colours, leftovers, and rounding up

Pick a colour to load its typical forward voltage, or type your own from the LED's packet. The bench subtracts the LED's share from the supply, divides by the current you want, then rounds UP to the nearest standard E12 value — the values resistors are actually sold in — and checks the heat the resistor must shed. Rounding up is deliberate: it can only ever give the LED slightly less current than planned.

Always round the resistor up

How the crystal radio works

The five-step walk-through

Section 03 follows one signal: antenna (catches everything) → coil and capacitor (resonate with one station) → crystal or diode detector (keeps half the wave) → high-impedance earpiece (turns the voice-shaped remainder into sound) → and no battery anywhere, because the transmitter supplies the energy. The diagram is a signal-flow picture, not a wiring schematic; the build card carries the wiring in words.

Safety, plainly

The whole room's rule

Nothing in this room connects to mains electricity — wall sockets are never a hobby material, full stop. The hazards that remain are small and named: antenna wires and power lines never mix, and no antenna work or listening on a long wire during thunderstorms; coin cells and small magnets stay away from small children; battery fruit is never eaten; short circuits on anything stronger than a coin cell can get hot fast. When in doubt, stop and ask — that is an electronics rule too.

Mains electricity is never a material
