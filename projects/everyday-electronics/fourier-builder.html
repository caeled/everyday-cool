What this is

A Fourier series takes a repeating waveform apart into plain sine waves — one fundamental and its harmonics — and adds them back up. Pick a target wave, choose how many sine terms (N) the builder may use, and watch the stack of glowing harmonics climb toward the target. Where the target jumps, the stack overshoots by about 8.95% of the jump no matter how many terms you add: that stubborn ear is the Gibbs phenomenon, and this exhibit measures it instead of hiding it.

Every control

Target waveform — Square (±1, jumps at 0 and ±π), Triangle (peaks ±1, no jumps), or Sawtooth (ramps −1 → +1, jumps at ±π where it wraps).

Harmonics N (1–51) — the number of sine terms in the partial sum. Square and triangle use only odd harmonics (1, 3, 5, …), so N terms reach harmonic 2N−1. Sawtooth uses every harmonic (1, 2, 3, …), so N terms reach harmonic N.

▶ Animate stacking — adds the harmonics one at a time, oldest first, so you can watch each new sine pull the sum closer (and watch the Gibbs ear refuse to shrink). Reset view stops the animation and shows the full sum for the chosen N.

Show partial sum — the bright amber curve: the target rebuilt from N sines.

Show target — the dashed white outline of the exact wave being rebuilt.

Show individual harmonics stacked — the lane chart underneath: each harmonic on its own glowing lane, in the order it is added (first 15 lanes shown).

Show residual error — the rose chart: target minus partial sum, drawn on its own ±0.35 scale so the leftover error stays visible even when it gets small.

The maths

Square: (4/π) · Σ sin((2k−1)x)/(2k−1) · Triangle: (8/π²) · Σ (−1)^(k−1) sin((2k−1)x)/(2k−1)² · Sawtooth: (2/π) · Σ (−1)^(k+1) sin(kx)/k, each summed over the first N terms.

Max error away from jumps ignores samples within 0.35 rad of a discontinuity — near a jump the error is dominated by Gibbs ringing, which is reported separately as overshoot %: (peak − 1) ÷ jump × 100, with jump = 2 for the square wave.

Simplifications, named

Targets are idealised to amplitude ±1 and period 2π; real signals have finite rise times, which soften (but do not remove) Gibbs ringing. The plots sample the exact series on a finite grid, so a peak can be missed by at most one grid step. The lane chart shows at most 15 harmonics even when N is larger. Nothing here is a fitted or fudged curve — every pixel and every readout is evaluated from the series above by the same core code the lesson's proofs run against.

Individual harmonics, stacked

Each lane is one sine term of the series, drawn at its true amplitude on a shared ±1 scale, in the order the builder adds it. Square and triangle lanes shrink fast or slow exactly as their coefficients say: 1/m for the square, 1/m² for the triangle — which is why the triangle settles down and the square keeps ringing at the jump.

Residual error — target minus partial sum

Drawn on a fixed ±0.35 scale. Away from a jump the residual dies as N grows; at a jump it concentrates into the Gibbs ringing — narrower with more harmonics, but never shorter.
