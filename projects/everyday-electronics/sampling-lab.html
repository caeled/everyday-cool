What this is

Two views of the same trick. Top: a continuous sine wave is sampled — measured at regular instants, fs times a second — and then rebuilt from those measurements alone. Bottom: a spinning wheel is “sampled” by a strobe light, which is why wheels in films and under fluorescent lights can look frozen, slow, or running backwards (the wagon-wheel effect).

The one rule: Nyquist

To capture a signal of frequency f you must sample faster than twice per cycle: fs > 2f. Sample slower and the samples stop describing your signal and start describing a different, slower one — its alias. Nothing downstream can tell the difference, because the samples themselves are identical. The information is not hidden; it is gone.

Controls

Signal frequency f (0.5–20 Hz) and sample rate fs (4–40 Hz): drag fs down through 2f and watch the amber reconstruction peel away from the true wave and lock onto the alias. Wheel: rotation rate and strobe rate sliders, plus ▶/❚❚ Play/Pause for both views.

Reading the top view

Cyan = the true continuous sine. Mint stems/dots = the samples — the only numbers a digital system ever gets. Amber = the reconstruction, an ideal sinc interpolation computed from those samples alone: each sample contributes a sinc bump and they are summed. It is honest band-limited reconstruction with a finite window, so it wobbles slightly near the edges of the window — that wobble is real, and the lesson names it.

Reading the readouts

Predicted alias is the fold formula |f − k·fs|, k = round(f/fs), folded into [0, fs/2]. Measured alias is not the formula echoed back: the core runs a DFT over the sampled sequence alone (200 samples, peak search over [0, fs/2] in 0.05 Hz steps) and reports where the energy actually sits. The two agreeing is the experiment. Signed alias keeps the sign: a negative signed alias is a wave whose phase runs backwards — try f = 9 Hz at fs = 10 Hz.

Reading the wheel

The faint cyan wheel is the true rotation. The bright wheel only moves when the strobe flashes — it holds the last sampled angle between flashes, exactly like your eye under a strobe. Amber dots on the rim are past sampled positions of the one amber-painted spoke. One spoke is painted amber so your eye can follow a single point; six identical unmarked spokes would add their own 60° symmetry aliasing on top of the strobe effect. If each flash catches the wheel slightly behind where it was, the bright wheel creeps backwards: that is a film wheel rolling in reverse.

Things to try

1) f = 3, fs = 10 — clean capture. 2) f = 7, fs = 10 — same samples as a 3 Hz wave running backwards. 3) f = 12, fs = 10 — folds to 2 Hz. 4) Wheel 9 rev/s, strobe 10/s — reversed at −1 rev/s. 5) Wheel rotation exactly equal to the strobe rate — frozen solid.

The proofs

The panel at the bottom runs runProofs() against the exact core this page displays with. Predicted aliases, measured aliases, the reversal proof (the samples of 9 Hz at fs = 10 are numerically identical to the samples of −1 Hz), the wheel rates, and reconstruction RMS errors are all computed live, not typed in.
