Transient Lab — the lesson

Most beginner electronics is about where a circuit settles. This lab is about the journey: the fraction of a second after you flip the switch, while voltages are still climbing, falling, or ringing. A capacitor cannot change its voltage instantly, and an inductor cannot change its current instantly — and those two refusals, plus one resistor bleeding energy away, generate almost every timing behaviour in electronics: delays, filters, oscillators, switch-on surges, and the ring you hear in a badly damped supply. This page is the other half of the lab — the physics, how the simulation core works, and the proof that its numbers are the textbook's numbers.

What this is

Transient Lab is an oscilloscope connected to two circuits you can rebuild with the sliders:

  • RC mode — a source, a resistor, and a capacitor in series. Press Charge and the capacitor voltage climbs toward the source voltage along an exponential. Press Discharge and it falls along the mirror-image exponential. One number, the time constant τ = RC, sets the whole pace.
  • RLC mode — an inductor joins the loop. Now the circuit has two energy stores that can trade back and forth, so instead of creeping it can ring: oscillate at a frequency set by L and C while the resistor drains the ring away at a rate set by R and L.

Every number on the lab's screen — the trace, the τ readout, the ring frequency "measured" off the trace — is computed from one simulation core inside the page. The core integrates the circuit equations step by step; it never evaluates the textbook formulas to draw the curve. Those formulas appear only in the proof harness, as the independent answer the integration is checked against.

The physics

The RC exponential, and why τ runs it

Kirchhoff's voltage law around the charging loop says the source voltage is split, at every instant, between the resistor and the capacitor: Vs = I·R + Vc. The capacitor's own law says its voltage changes at a rate set by the current flowing into it: dVc/dt = I/C. Put them together and you get the equation the core integrates:

RC · dVc/dt + Vc = Vs One first-order differential equation. Everything about the RC transient is in it.

Read it as a story: the current — and so the rate of climb — is proportional to the remaining gap between the capacitor voltage and the source. Big gap at the start, fast climb; small gap later, slow creep. "Rate proportional to what's left" is the definition of exponential behaviour, and its solutions are the two curves on the scope:

Charge: Vc(t) = V0 · (1 − e^(−t/τ))   ·   Discharge: Vc(t) = V0 · e^(−t/τ)   ·   τ = RC After one τ a charging capacitor has closed 63.2% of the gap; a discharging one has 36.8% of its voltage left. After 2τ: 86.5% / 13.5%. After 4τ: 98.2% / 1.8%. The fraction per τ never changes — that is the fingerprint of the exponential.

τ also tells you the opening current: at t = 0 the capacitor is empty, the full V0 stands across R, and the current starts at V0/R — the largest it will ever be in this circuit. That is why real capacitor-input supplies surge at switch-on, and why the lab shows the current trace starting at its peak and decaying with the same τ.

The RLC ring: two stores, one leak

Add an inductor and the loop gains a second state variable. The system the core integrates is now a pair of coupled first-order equations, equivalent to one second-order ODE:

L · dI/dt + R·I + Vc = Vs   ·   dVc/dt = I/C The capacitor stores energy in an electric field (½CV²); the inductor stores it in a magnetic field (½LI²). Undisturbed, they would trade it back and forth forever.

Two rates decide what you actually see. The natural frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC) is how fast the energy wants to slosh. The damping rate α = R/(2L) is how fast the resistor skims energy off the sloshing. Their ratio is the damping ratio ζ = α/ω₀:

  • ζ < 1 — underdamped. The circuit rings. But not at ω₀: friction slows the slosh, so the observed ring frequency is the damped natural frequency ωd = √(ω₀² − α²), and the peaks shrink inside an envelope ±V0 · e^(−αt). Both facts are checked by the proofs below.
  • ζ = 1 — critically damped. The fastest possible return to rest with no overshoot. The boundary case engineers aim for in meters, suspensions, and control loops.
  • ζ > 1 — overdamped. No ring at all; the response creeps home like a sluggish RC. Dial R up in the lab and watch the ring die into a creep — that transition is ζ crossing 1.

One elegant check hides in the ring: at the exact peaks of Vc, the current is zero (that's what makes them peaks — dVc/dt = I/C = 0), and at those instants the solution simplifies so that |Vc| = V0 · e^(−αt) exactly, with no cosine factor left over. Proof 4b uses one of those peaks, a full period in, as its measuring point.

How the core works

The simulation core sits between the markers /* === CORE START === */ and /* === CORE END === */ in the lab page. It touches no DOM, no canvas, no clock — given the same circuit and step size it produces the same trace in the browser or in Node, which is exactly how the proofs below were run. The page's scope, readouts, and proof harness all call this one core; there is no second implementation to drift out of sync.

  • RC stepping uses the exact exponential update: over one step dt, the linear ODE's true solution is Vc ← Vs + (Vc − Vs)·e^(−dt/τ), so that is the update. It is still integration — the state is advanced step by step from the differential equation, and mid-step source changes or a future nonlinear part would slot into the same loop — but for this linear equation the per-step update happens to be exact. See the honesty box for what that does to Proof 1's error numbers.
  • RLC stepping uses classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta on the (Vc, I) pair, with automatic substeps: the internal step h is held to 0.08 / (α + √(α² + ω₀²)) — small against the fastest rate present in the loop — so the integration stays accurate and stable from the gentlest ring to the stiffest overdamped corner of the sliders. (Slider extremes were stress-tested in Node, ζ from 0.01 to 63: no instability, no NaNs.)
  • Measurements are read off the trace, not computed from the dial settings. τ comes from finding where the recorded samples cross 63.2% of V0 (linear interpolation between the two straddling samples). The ring frequency comes from timing the recorded trace's zero crossings. The envelope check finds actual local extrema in the samples. If the integrator were wrong, these readings would be wrong with it — which is the point of measuring this way.
  • The display is a recording. A button press integrates the full window (1,400 samples) once, then the scope sweeps a playhead across the finished trace over about 3.4 seconds. The time-scale slider changes the window being integrated, never the circuit.

Honest note on the tiny RC errors: errors like 2.31e-13% are floating-point round-off, not a triumph of numerical method. The RC per-step update is the closed-form solution applied stepwise, so Proofs 1–3 mostly verify that the core implements the equation it claims to — worthwhile (a wrong sign or a swapped R and C would fail loudly), but not evidence of integrator heroics. The genuine numerical test is Proof 4: RK4 has no closed form to lean on, and it still lands the ring frequency within 5.04e-11% and the envelope within 1.08e-8% of the analytic answers at the proof's sampling. Loosen the sampling to 200 points per ring period and the envelope error grows to about 6.7e-6% — still far inside tolerance, and a fairer picture of RK4 earning its keep.

Bends & simplifications, named

  • Ideal components. No capacitor ESR or leakage current, no inductor winding resistance beyond the R you dial in, no core saturation, no temperature drift. A real 1000 µF electrolytic would add its own small series resistance and shift both τ and the damping.
  • A perfect step source. The source jumps to V0 (or to 0) instantly at t = 0 and has zero internal resistance. Real supplies slew and sag; switch contacts bounce. None of that is modelled.
  • An ideal scope. Infinite input impedance, unlimited bandwidth, no quantisation, no noise. It reads the simulated state exactly, so the "measured off the trace" numbers are limited by sample spacing and interpolation, not by an instrument.
  • Linear interpolation in the measurements. Crossing times and peak values are read from samples spaced 1/500 τ (proofs) or 1/1400 of the window (lab display) apart, with straight lines between neighbours. That — not the integrator — is the largest error source in the τ and frequency readouts, and it is still orders of magnitude inside the stated tolerances.
  • The envelope identity is exact only at true extrema. Proof 4b compares |Vc| to V0·e^(−αt) at a sampled peak. Between peaks the trace sits inside the envelope (the cosine factor), and the sampled peak is within one sample of the true extremum. The claim made is only the one checked: at that peak, within 5%.
  • What is not claimed: nothing here validates behaviour the sliders can't reach — no claim about nonlinear parts (diodes, saturating cores), AC steady state, or coupled circuits. This is a two-circuit lab and its proofs cover exactly those two circuits.
