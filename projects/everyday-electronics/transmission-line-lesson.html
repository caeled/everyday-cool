Transmission Line — the lesson

The exhibit fires a voltage step down a simulated coaxial line and lets you change what waits at the far end. Underneath it is one of the oldest and most useful ideas in electrical work: a wire is not a wire. It is a road, the signal is a wave travelling on it, and every ending is a negotiation. End the road in exactly the resistance the wave expects and it stops dead, absorbed. End it in anything else and part of the wave turns around and comes back. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the physics, the math, the worked answers, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The game was built by Muse for Everyday Cool; curated by Steven Powell. The framing is his, and it is literal: carriers all the way down. His father spent five decades as a ham operator sending signals down feedlines; a network cable carrying packets is the same physics wearing a newer jacket.

What this is

When you close a switch at one end of a long cable, the far end does not know instantly. The news travels as an electromagnetic wave, guided by the cable, at a good fraction of the speed of light — fast, but not instant, and on a long enough line the delay is something you can measure, budget, and trip over. While the wave travels, it does not "see" whatever is connected at the far end. It only sees the line itself, and the line presents a characteristic impedance, Z₀, set by its geometry and materials. Only when the wave arrives at the end does it meet the load — and the meeting goes one of three ways.

  • The load equals Z₀ (matched). The load takes exactly the power the wave is carrying, in exactly the form the wave offers it. Nothing is left over, so nothing reflects. The line goes quiet at one clean level.
  • The load is an open circuit. Current cannot leave the line, so the arriving wave has nowhere to put its energy. It reflects whole, in phase: the voltage at the open end momentarily doubles, and a wave of the same sign runs back toward the source.
  • The load is a short circuit. Voltage at a perfect short must be zero, so the reflection comes back inverted and cancels the arriving wave at the end. Same size, opposite sign.

Anything in between — a resistor that is not quite Z₀ — splits the difference: part absorbed, part reflected, with the sign and size set by one small formula below. This is not a curiosity. Reflections are why antenna feedlines want matching, why old Ethernet had terminators on both ends of the coax, why a badly crimped cable can pass a continuity test and still mangle data, and why "the cable is fine" and "the link is fine" are different sentences.

The telegraph model

The working picture dates to the telegraph era and is still the picture engineers use: a transmission line is a ladder. Each short section contributes a little series inductance L (the magnetic field the current builds around itself) and a little shunt capacitance C (the electric field between the conductors). A wave on the line is those two fields handing energy back and forth, section to section, at a speed the ladder itself sets. Write the voltage and current on the ladder as functions of position and time and you get the telegrapher's equations; solve them and two numbers fall out — the speed and the impedance:

Z₀ = √(L / C) The characteristic impedance: the ratio of voltage to current in a single travelling wave. This exhibit's sections carry L = 2.5 µH and C = 1.0 nF, so Z₀ = √(2.5×10⁻⁶ / 1.0×10⁻⁹) = √2500 = 50 Ω — stated, fixed, and not adjustable in the exhibit.
delay per section = √(L·C)  ·  speed = 1 / √(L·C) per section √(2.5×10⁻⁶ × 1.0×10⁻⁹) = 50 ns per section. The simulation's time step is exactly one section-delay, so the wave advances exactly one cell per step, and a 200-cell line has a one-way delay of 200 × 50 ns = 10.00 µs.
Γ = (ZL − Z₀) / (ZL + Z₀) The reflection coefficient: reflected step height ÷ incident step height. Open: (+∞ limit) → +1. Short: (0 − 50)/(0 + 50) = −1. Matched 50 Ω: 0. 100 Ω: 50/150 = +1/3. 25 Ω: −25/75 = −1/3. 200 Ω: 150/250 = +0.6.
Vlaunched = Vsource × Z₀ / (Rs + Z₀) = Vsource / 2 The source drives the line through its own 50 Ω source resistance — stated here and in the game, and matched to Z₀ on purpose, so a returning reflection is absorbed at the source instead of bouncing again. The source amplitude is 2.0 V, so the step that actually launches into the line is 1.0 V, and in the matched case the whole line settles uniformly at that same 1.0 V = Vsource/2.

How Γ is measured in the exhibit (not typed in)

The simulation's probe sits at cell 50, a quarter of the way along the 200-cell line. A step is fired; the probe reads the flat top of the step as it first passes — the incident height, 1.0000 V. The reflection needs 300 steps to go from the probe to the load and back, so there is a long, clean window before the probe reading changes to incident + reflected. Γmeasured is that second reading divided by the first, minus 1. A step is used for the measurement rather than a pulse because the flat top is a steady place to read; a pulse's edges are exactly where a discrete lattice is least honest (see the bends, below).

Bends named honestly

Lossless line. The ladder has L and C only — no series resistance, no dielectric loss. A real cable attenuates, and attenuates high frequencies more, so real reflections come back smaller and rounder than these. Here, an open or short reflection returns at full size forever (until the matched source absorbs it). That is the textbook line, not a field-worn one.

Discrete lattice dispersion — measured, not waved at. The line is 200 lumps, not a continuum, and lumps disperse: the shortest wavelengths the lattice can carry travel at the wrong speed and are not perfectly absorbed even by a matched load. We measured what that does. Driven by a mathematically perfect instantaneous step, the matched line at 900 steps still carried a ripple packet between 0.8125 V and 1.1875 V — an 18.75% deviation that decays only slowly. So the shipped source is not a perfect step: it rises smoothly over 10 steps (500 ns), the way a real pulse generator does. With that rise, the matched settle is uniform to 0.20% and every Γ above lands on theory to six places. The bend is the rise time; it is stated in the game, and Γ is read on the flat top of the step, away from the edge, for this reason.

The source is matched to Z₀ — stated, not hidden. The source resistance is 50 Ω, equal to Z₀. That is a choice with a visible consequence: a reflection returning from the load is absorbed at the source and the story ends. With a low-impedance source the reflection would re-reflect and the line would ring through several round trips, settling only gradually to the load's DC value. The exhibit chose the clean case and says so.

Boundary currents are solved implicitly. At the two end nodes, the current through the source resistance (or the load) depends on the very voltage being updated, so the update solves for them together instead of using last step's voltage. An explicit version is unstable for loads below Z₀ — the 25 Ω case blows up — which is a fact about the naive scheme, not about transmission lines. The implicit form is also why Γ lands on theory so closely; a cruder boundary would show its error in exactly these rows.

One speed, everywhere. The model's wave advances exactly one cell per step at every frequency that matters here, because the time step equals the section delay. Real lines are close to this; the lattice only misbehaves near its Nyquist edge, which is the ripple above.
