# Collection origin

Original Everyday Cool workshop, added 18 November 2026 at Steven Powell's request. In the Everyday Cool ideas side chat that day he named "Everyday Electronics" as a future room and seeded its spec in his own words: "electronics can also show how simple radio works (with the 3penny or a crystal radio plan) and a link to from radio room :)" — then, while out, told Muse to start on it and that he would look at it when he got home. Built by Muse (AI assistant) and added directly to the collection at his direction; this folder is the maintained original, and there is no upstream copy.

The room follows the Games Room / Radio Room shape (a verified shelf of legitimate places, things that work right on the page, a short workshop underneath) and the Find It / Radio Room pattern (Help ▾ menu with full documentation, banner verified on the page and inside the desktop app). The updated Everyday Cool desktop app ships as app v1.0.13 through the app repo's existing build workflow.

Steven's cross-link request runs both ways, as he asked: this room links to The Radio Room ("hear the stations this radio would catch"), and The Radio Room's Keep Going list gained the reciprocal link to this room the same day (recorded in its COLLECTION.md). While the build was underway Steven added one more spec item — simple chemical batteries, "like the one that uses an orange to run an LED" — which is the fruit battery card: an orange front and centre, copper and galvanized-nail electrodes, an honest ≈0.9 V per cell, and the series lesson (one fruit will not light a normal LED; three or four in series will light a red one).

Everything interactive is computed in the visitor's browser from the rules in `elec.js` — the page sends nothing anywhere. The crystal radio explanation stays physically honest: tuning is coil-and-capacitor resonance, detection is keeping half the wave, and there is no battery because the transmitter supplies the energy. The build cards' safety lines are part of the lessons, not boilerplate: antenna wires never near power lines, no antenna work or long-wire listening during thunderstorms, coin cells and small magnets away from small children, and mains electricity never a material.

The rules live in `elec.js` (no DOM), checked by `test.cjs`.

Local change (19 November 2026): two benches added and the room finished out. **The breadboard** (section 03): a virtual solderless breadboard following the real tie rules — rows tied in fives on each side of the centre groove, continuous power rails (the common mid-rail split is not modelled, and the page says so) — with a parts pile (batteries 3/5/9 V, an AC source 1–12 V peak at 1–120 Hz, resistors 100 Ω–10 kΩ, capacitors 10/100/1000 µF modelled polarity-free, red/yellow/green LEDs with polarity enforced, a toggle switch, a pushbutton, jumper wires), a time-stepped simulation (fixed 1/4800 s step, nodal analysis over the tie-point nets, capacitors as charge state), an oscilloscope probe for any hole, per-part readouts in the room's voice, and three presets (LED starter, RC charge/discharge, amplifier). LEDs pop past ≈30 mA sustained, shorts warn, and the page prints its simplifications. **The crystal radio bench** (section 05): a labeled schematic agreeing with the build plan, an interactive tuner (40–365 pF against a stated 250 µH coil, f = 1/(2π√(LC)) live), five clearly fictional AM stations over a static floor, a loudness meter and a "what you'd hear" line. **The transistor shipped in this same build at Steven's direction** — first planned as a later add, then called in early the same morning ("a small amplifying circuit as well"): an NPN part labelled 2N2222 on the breadboard (deliberately simple model: Vbe ≈ 0.7 V, β ≈ 100, saturation floor 0.2 V; no saturation curves or temperature effects, stated on the page), a one-transistor amplifier bench beside the crystal bench with a labeled schematic and walk-through, a "Load the amplifier" breadboard preset, and a with-amplifier toggle on the tuner. The room tests (`node test.cjs`) measure the amplifier preset's gain at ×7.3 under the stated conditions and cover every breadboard behaviour the page promises. Game (21 November 2026): at Steven Powell's direction, the room gained its first game — `dont-cook-the-led.html`, **Don't Cook the LED**: seven circuits where the player picks an E12 resistor (and, on the wattage level, a power rating) to light an LED at its target current without cooking it or burning the resistor. The physics core (`currentMA = (Vs - Vf)/R`, `resistorPowerW = I^2R`) is a pure function fenced in the page and shared with its proof, which verifies a winning pick on every level, that the level-1 wire-only trap cooks, and that the wattage level's in-band 820 ohm pick burns at a quarter watt. `dont-cook-the-led-lesson.html` is its back door — Ohm's law, the worked levels, the coding choices, the simplifications named — opened from inside the game by a Lesson pop-up. `index.html` gained a game card after the LED resistor bench.
