RLC Resonance — the lesson

An inductor and a capacitor trade energy back and forth — magnetic field to electric field and back — and left alone they would do it forever at one natural frequency. Add a resistor and a driving source and that private oscillation becomes the sharpest tool in analogue electronics: a circuit that answers one frequency loudly and the rest quietly. That is resonance. It is how a radio picks one station out of the whole sky, how a crossover keeps bass out of a tweeter, and how a notch filter removes one hum and leaves the music. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the physics, the math, the worked answers, and an audit of the exhibit itself, measured by driving its own shipped code.

⚠ FALSE CLAIM — read this first. The exhibit's shaded “−3 dB band” on the Bode plot is drawn in the wrong place whenever Q is low, and it is not the −3 dB band of the displayed curve at all for the Low-Pass, High-Pass and Notch taps. The code shades f₀ ± Δf/2 — symmetric on a linear axis — but true half-power frequencies are symmetric on a log axis (their geometric mean is f₀, their arithmetic mean is not). Measured on the exhibit's own band-pass curve at Q = 0.707: the true −3 dB edges are 514.95 Hz and 1,921.48 Hz; the shading paints 291 Hz to 1,698 Hz — the low edge 43% too low, the high edge 12% too low. At Q = 10 the same drawing is only ~0.1% off, which is why the error hides in the default view. The width Δf it reports is exactly right; the painted position is wrong at low Q. Details and the second mislabel (parallel “gain”) are in the bends, below. The core engine itself is exact — see the proofs.

What this is

Drive a series R–L–C loop with a sine wave. At low frequency the capacitor dominates: its reactance 1/(ωC) is huge, little current flows, and the current leads. At high frequency the inductor dominates: ωL is huge, little current flows, and the current lags. Somewhere between, the two reactances are exactly equal and cancel. The source then sees only the resistor. That frequency is resonance, and at it three things happen at once, all of which the exhibit shows:

  • Current peaks. Series impedance is at its minimum, |Z| = R, so I = V₀/R — the largest current the source will ever deliver to this loop.
  • Phase is zero. With the reactances cancelled, voltage and current are in step. Below f₀ the circuit is capacitive (phase negative in the exhibit's convention, current leading); above, inductive.
  • The reactive voltages are huge. VL and VC are each Q × V₀, in exact 180° opposition, so they cancel in the loop sum while each individually can dwarf the source. In the default preset, a 10 V source puts 100 V across the inductor and 100 V across the capacitor. This is not a parlour trick — it is why resonant circuits stress component voltage ratings.

Wire the same three parts in parallel instead and everything inverts: at resonance the tank's impedance is at its maximum (= R), the tank voltage peaks, and it is the branch currents through L and C that magnify to Q times the source current, circulating around the tank in opposition. Series resonance is a current peak through a minimum; parallel antiresonance is a voltage peak across a maximum. The exhibit offers both, and the audit drove both.

The working formulas

f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC)) The resonant frequency. Note what is not in it: R. Resistance sets how sharp the resonance is, never where it sits. Default parts L = 16.0 mH, C = 1.60 µF give f₀ = 994.72 Hz — the exhibit's “995 Hz” readout is that number rounded.
Z₀ = √(L/C)  ·  Qseries = Z₀/R  ·  Qparallel = R/Z₀ Z₀ is the characteristic (surge) impedance of the L–C pair — 100 Ω for the default parts. Q is the quality factor: how many radians' worth of energy the circuit stores versus loses per cycle, and the voltage (series) or current (parallel) magnification at f₀. Default series: Q = 100/10 = 10.0. Parallel preset (R = 800 Ω): Q = 800/100 = 8.0.
Δf = f₀ / Q  ·  ζ = 1 / (2Q) Δf is the half-power (−3 dB) bandwidth: the width between the two frequencies where the band-pass response falls to 1/√2 of its peak. Default: 994.72/10 = 99.47 Hz. ζ (zeta) is the damping ratio of the step response — the same Q wearing its transient hat; ζ < 1 rings, ζ = 1 is critically damped, ζ > 1 creeps.
Zseries = R + j(ωL − 1/ωC)  ·  Yparallel = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/ωL) The two complex impedances the exhibit evaluates. Every number in the proofs below — impedance, phase, current, and all four filter taps in both topologies — comes out of these two expressions, which is exactly why the audit could check the exhibit against independent phasor arithmetic and expect agreement digit for digit.

All four filter taps, both topologies

Every tap transfer function — Band-Pass (VR), Low-Pass (VC), High-Pass (VL) and Notch (VL+VC) in series, and their parallel counterparts — was checked at f₀/2, f₀ and 2f₀ against independent complex division: 24 of 24 values agree with the reference to 0.000% at the precision printed. Spot values worth remembering: the series low-pass and high-pass taps peak at exactly |H| = Q = 10 (+20.0 dB) at f₀ — a series “low-pass” taken across C is a peaking filter, not a gentle one, unless Q is tamed; and both notch taps fall to exactly 0 at f₀, displayed as the code's −100 dB floor (see bends). The presets also check out as labelled: “Audio Low-Pass” is a genuine Butterworth alignment (Q = 0.7072 ≈ 1/√2, f₀ = 994.7 Hz ≈ its “1 kHz”), “HP Crossover” sits at f₀ = 2,516 Hz ≈ its “2.5 kHz” with Q = 0.706, and “Notch Rejector” sits at 600.58 Hz ≈ its “600 Hz”.

The step response (RK4) — checked against the closed form

The exhibit's transient solver was run at 1 µs steps on the default parts with a 10 V step and compared with the analytic underdamped response. At t = 0.5, 1, 2 and 5 ms its current and capacitor voltage matched the closed form to all five decimals printed (e.g. at 0.5 ms: vC = 18.54288 V in both — yes, the capacitor voltage overshoots to nearly twice the step on its first swing at Q = 10; that is the same Q magnification, in the time domain). Its damped ringing frequency is 993.47 Hz — f₀ pulled down by damping, exactly as fd = f₀√(1 − ζ²) predicts. Pass.

Bends named honestly

FALSE — the painted −3 dB band (see the box at the top). The Bode shading is drawn from f₀ − Δf/2 to f₀ + Δf/2 on a log-frequency plot, and is drawn identically for every tap. Two errors follow. (1) Placement: half-power frequencies are geometrically symmetric about f₀, so at low Q the painted band sits too low — measured above, 291–1,698 Hz painted vs 515–1,921 Hz true at Q = 0.707; at Q = 10 the error shrinks to ~0.1% and at Q = 8 (parallel) the true centre is 1,594.7 Hz arithmetic vs 1,591.5 Hz painted. (2) Meaning: for the Low-Pass, High-Pass and Notch taps the displayed curve's peak is not 0 dB (it is +20 dB at f₀ for the Q = 10 series LP/HP taps, and the notch's feature is a null, not a peak), so no band drawn around f₀ from Δf alone is that curve's −3 dB band. The numeric Δf readout is exact; only the paint lies, and only where Q is small or the tap is not band-pass.

MISLABELLED — parallel Low-Pass / High-Pass “Filter Gain.” In parallel mode those two taps are not voltage gains at all: they are branch-current ratios, IL/Is and IC/Is. The maths is exact (at f₀ both equal Q = 8), but the top-left telemetry still calls the result “Filter Gain … +18.1 dB,” which reads as an output voltage 8× the input. There is no such voltage — the tank voltage at f₀ is exactly V₀. The drawer's small tap label does disclose “Tap: IL / Is”; the headline telemetry does not. Current magnification is the real and interesting phenomenon here; it deserves its own label.

MISLABELLED, smaller — parallel “Ipk.” In parallel mode the telemetry's Ipk is the Norton source current, V₀/R = 12.50 mA, constant at every frequency. That is a true current, but at resonance the currents a visitor cares about — the tank's circulating IL and IC — are 100 mA each, and they appear only in the circuit drawing's “circulating” caption near resonance. In series mode Ipk is the loop current and means what it appears to mean; in parallel mode it does not.

Ideal parts, Norton source. The parallel tank is driven by a perfect current source (Is = V₀/R) and the series loop by a perfect voltage source; R, L and C are ideal and frequency-independent. A real inductor brings winding resistance that moves with frequency, a real capacitor brings ESR, and a real source brings its own impedance into the tank — all of which lower the working Q below the component Q computed here. The exhibit's Q is the textbook Q of the parts you dialled, honestly computed.

−100 dB is a floor, not a measurement. The notch's true gain at exactly f₀ is zero (−∞ dB). The code clamps |H| at 10⁻⁵ before taking the logarithm, so the readout and the Bode curve bottom out at −100 dB. A real notch bottoms out wherever its parts' imperfections put it — usually well above −100 dB — so read the floor as “very deep,” not as a depth.

One R, two jobs. In series mode R is the loop's loss; in parallel mode the same slider is both the tank's parallel loss and the resistance that defines the Norton source current. Switching topology with R left at 10 Ω therefore gives a parallel Q of 0.1 — a tank so damped it barely resonates — which is correct arithmetic for that circuit, but the visitor has to raise R (the parallel preset uses 800 Ω) to see antiresonance sing. The presets handle this; free play does not warn about it.
