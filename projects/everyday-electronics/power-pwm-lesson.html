Power & PWM — the lesson

The exhibit — titled Power Lab in the page itself — is five power circuits sharing one bench: a half-wave rectifier, a bridge rectifier, a buck converter, a boost converter, and an H-bridge that drives a DC motor or synthesises AC. Underneath all five is one idea: you do not control power by wasting the excess as heat; you control it by switching — fully on, fully off, and letting an inductor and a capacitor average the result. The fraction of each cycle the switch spends on is the duty cycle D, and D is the knob this whole page turns on. This lesson is the other half of the exhibit: the physics, the math, the worked answers, and an audit of the exhibit's own numbers, measured by driving its shipped code.

One scope note before anything else, because it decides what can even be asked: the exhibit has no bare PWM mode. There is no switch chopping a voltage straight into a lone resistor or an LED, so there is no LED-brightness model to audit — honest or otherwise. Every PWM path in the page is filtered (buck, boost) or drives a motor (H-bridge). The famous raw-PWM laws — Vavg = D·Vin, VRMS = Vin·√D — are still covered below, as the reference the filtered circuits are built on.

What this is

Pulse-width modulation is a bargain with time. A switch that is fully on drops almost no voltage; a switch that is fully off passes no current; either way it dissipates almost nothing. Chop between the two fast enough and the load — helped by an inductor that resists current changes and a capacitor that resists voltage changes — sees the average. Three different loads cash that bargain out three different ways, and confusing them is the classic beginner's error this lesson exists to prevent:

  • A filtered converter (buck/boost) responds to the average: the LC filter strips the chop away and delivers Vavg = D·Vin (buck). Power into its resistive load is then Vavg²/R, because after filtering there is barely any ripple left for RMS to disagree about.
  • A bare resistor on raw PWM responds to RMS: it heats during the on pulse at full Vin and not at all between pulses, so P = Vin²·D/R = VRMS²/R with VRMS = Vin·√D. Using the average here — Vavg²/R — undercounts the heating by a factor of 1/D: at D = 0.25, four times too little. Same waveform, different question, different answer.
  • A DC motor responds mostly to the average voltage (its own inductance and inertia do the filtering), settling where back-EMF balances the applied average minus the armature-resistance drop — roughly linear in D, with a stall dead-zone near zero that no amount of averaging removes.

The math

Vavg = D · Vin Raw PWM average, and the buck converter's ideal output in continuous conduction (CCM). At Vin = 24 V: D = 0.25 → 6.00 V, D = 0.50 → 12.00 V, D = 0.90 → 21.60 V.
VRMS = Vin · √D  ·  PR = Vin² · D / R Raw PWM into a resistor. At 24 V into 10 Ω: D = 0.25 gives VRMS = 12.00 V and 14.40 W — while Vavg²/R would claim only 3.60 W. Heating follows RMS, never the average. The exhibit computes load power the correct general way — it integrates v·i instantaneously, every sub-step — so it lands on the right answer in both the raw and the filtered case without ever naming this formula.
Vout,boost = Vin / (1 − D) The boost converter's ideal CCM gain. It has a cliff built in: as D → 1 the gain → ∞ on paper and the inductor current explodes in fact. At Vin = 12 V: D = 0.55 → 26.67 V, D = 0.67 → 36.36 V, D = 0.82 → 66.67 V.
ΔIL = Vin · D(1 − D) / (L · f)  ·  ΔVpp ≈ ΔIL / (8 · f · C)  +  ΔIL · ESR Buck ripple. The first term is the capacitor drinking the triangular inductor ripple; the second is the same ripple current dropping voltage across the capacitor's own series resistance. The first falls as 1/f², the second only as 1/f — so at high frequency the ESR term takes over and ripple stops obeying the simple formula. The audit below catches exactly that hand-over in the exhibit's numbers.
ΔVpp ≈ Iload / (f · C)  (half-wave)  ·  Iload / (2f · C)  (bridge) Rectifier ripple: between charging peaks the capacitor alone feeds the load, so it sags by roughly the charge the load drinks in one period. The bridge recharges twice per cycle, halving the sag. Both formulas assume the sag is small next to the output voltage.

The proofs

The exhibit's circuit solver — a real sub-stepped ODE integration with CCM/DCM handling, not a formula lookup — was extracted from the shipped page byte-for-byte, run headlessly in Node with the display stubbed out, and driven through its own controls. Measured means measured: read off the simulation's own cycle-averaged telemetry after settling, not computed from the formula being checked. Conditions for the buck sweeps: Vin = 24 V, L = 100 µH, C = 22 µF, R = 10 Ω, f = 50 kHz unless stated.

From 20 kHz to 150 kHz the average output is frequency-independent to within 0.02 V, exactly as ideal theory demands. Pass. Ripple tracks the capacitive formula at 20–50 kHz, then falls progressively slower — +27% over the formula at 100 kHz, +74% at 150 kHz — because the ESR term (ΔI·ESR, only 1/f) overtakes the capacitive term (1/f²). That is the bend the formula box above names, measured rather than asserted. The 5 kHz row is the other honest failure: ΔIL grows past twice the load current, conduction goes discontinuous, and the output climbs toward Vin — real DCM physics, and the page's badge flips to DCM to say so. Efficiency sags gently at high f (95.7% → 95.1%) because the model charges a switching loss of ½·V·I·45 ns on every edge, and edges per second is what frequency is.

In the big-C regime where the textbook formulas apply, they overestimate the sag by roughly 20–30% (they assume a constant load current and ignore that the diode recharges the cap before the trough); treat them as upper bounds. The shipped presets use small caps on purpose, deep in the sawtooth regime where the formulas do not apply at all — good theatre, labelled as ripple exhibits, but do not check the formula there.

Power bookkeeping: the load power is right

For the filtered buck, the page's integrated load power matches Vavg²/R to the third decimal (D = 0.50: 13.354 W measured vs 13.354 W from the average) — as it must, since post-filter the RMS and the average are the same number to within the ripple. The method — integrate v·i, never square an average — is the one that would also get raw PWM right. Pass. The input side of the ledger is another story; see the flagged box below.

⚠ Claims that failed the audit

FALSE AS PRESENTED — the efficiency, input power, and loss numbers cannot all be true at once. The displayed η is computed as Pout/(Pout + Ploss), from the loss side of the ledger — and the displayed Pin is silently floored at Pout + Ploss in the code (Math.max), so the panel can never contradict itself on screen. Driven off screen, the buck at D = 0.50 actually integrates to Pin = 14.14 W against Pout + Ploss = 13.96 W: there is 0.19 W (1.3%) of input power the loss model never accounts for, the true Pout/Pin efficiency is 94.4%, and the panel says 95.7%. At D = 0.25 the gap is worse in proportion: 91.5% displayed vs 87.4% by input power. In the boost and rectifier modes the unaccounted remainder flips sign — the floor hides it the other way. The individual loss terms are real (next section); the headline η is the rosiest of the defensible definitions, presented as the only one.

FAILED — the SPWM peak claim. The schematic header states "SPWM AC Peak ≈ 21.3 V (15.0 Vrms)" for the inverter preset (D = 0.78, 24 V bus). Driven, the output actually peaks at +23.28 V / −23.65 V — about 10% high, because the LC filter rings up near its resonance and the claim's m·Vin formula assumes it doesn't. Related mislabel: in SPWM mode the telemetry card titled "Ripple ΔVpp" reads 46.9 V — that is the AC waveform's own peak-to-peak swing, not ripple on a DC output.

OVERSTATED — the rectifier peak formulas. The header text claims "Peak DC ≈ Vpk − Vf = 17.30 V" (half-wave) and "Vpk − 2Vf = 16.60 V" (bridge). Driven with the exhibit's own component values, the peaks are 16.08 V and 15.62 V. The missing ~1 V is dropped across the 0.55 Ω transformer source resistance during the short, heavy charging pulses — a real effect the printed formula omits while presenting two decimal places.

Bends named honestly

The loss model is real — five separate mechanisms, each verifiable. MOSFET conduction (I²·RDS(on)), switching loss (½·V·I·45 ns per edge, so it grows with frequency — measured: the MOSFET loss bucket rises from 0.12 W at 50 kHz to 0.23 W at 150 kHz), diode forward drop (Vf·I plus I²·rD), inductor DCR, and capacitor ESR. Isolation tests behave: raising RDS(on) from 10 mΩ to 500 mΩ drops buck η from 96.1% to 93.5% and Vout from 11.60 V to 11.33 V; raising Vf from 0.15 V to 1.2 V drops η from 96.9% to 92.7%. Nothing here is idealised away — except in the efficiency headline above.

"Losses OFF" is not ideal. The toggle substitutes small residual values (RDS(on) 2 mΩ, Vf 0.02 V, DCR 4 mΩ, ESR 4 mΩ) rather than zero, and the displayed efficiency is hard-capped at 99.8% in code. Hence losses-off Vout still sits 0.1–0.8% under the ideal formulas. Close enough to see the textbook through it; not a perfect-ideal mode.

The motor is a cartoon, knowingly built. Beyond the linear-by-construction speed: its mechanical time constant is set to 3.5 switching cycles, so in physical time the rotor would settle in microseconds — it only looks right because the exhibit runs in slow motion, and changing fsw changes how sluggish the rotor feels. Its "power out" counts mechanical power plus an arbitrary 25% of armature heating, with the other 75% filed under "other losses" — constants chosen in code, not physics. Motor η (60–75% across the sweep) should not be quoted.

The MOSFET temperature is theatre. T = 25 + 18·PMOS + 10·Pdiode with fixed coefficients — no heatsink, no ambient model. It moves the right way when losses rise; the number itself is illustrative.

Duty is fenced to 5–90%. The slider never reaches 0% or 100%, so the two endpoints where PWM degenerates to plain DC cannot be explored, and the boost's worst cliff country (D > 0.90) is fenced off — probably wisely.

Hard clamps guard the solver. Inductor current is clamped at 45 A (boost), capacitor voltage at 140–180 V, motor current at ±25 A. In normal play you never meet them; at the fences they, not physics, decide the reading.

Slow motion is the price of seeing. Real switching at 50 kHz is invisible, so the exhibit runs thousands of times slow and integrates the true microsecond physics underneath. The waveforms are honest; the pace is not — and the motor bend above is what that bargain costs.
