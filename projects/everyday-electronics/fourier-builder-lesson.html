Fourier Builder — the lesson

The exhibit stacks plain sine waves — a fundamental and its harmonics — until they add up to a square wave, a triangle wave, or a sawtooth. It works almost everywhere. The one place it refuses to behave is the most interesting thing on the page: at a jump, the stack overshoots by about 8.95% of the jump, forever, no matter how many harmonics you pile on. That is the Gibbs phenomenon, and this page is the other half of the exhibit — the maths, the measured proof that the code does what it claims, and the places where the picture bends, named honestly.

What this is

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed something that sounded outrageous: any repeating waveform — however jagged — is a sum of smooth sine waves. Not approximately a sum. A sum. The jaggedness doesn't live in any one sine; it emerges from adding many of them, each one a pure tone at a multiple of the repetition rate. Those multiples are the harmonics: the fundamental (m = 1) plus m = 2, 3, 4, …

The builder lets you feel that claim instead of just reading it. Choose a target, drag the harmonic count N, or press ▶ Animate stacking and watch the terms arrive one at a time. Each new sine is small and polite. The sum is not. Corners sharpen, flats flatten — and at a discontinuity, a little ear of overshoot appears and then refuses every invitation to leave.

Three readouts keep the exhibit honest while you play: the max error away from jumps (samples within 0.35 rad of a discontinuity are excluded, because there the error is ringing, not failure to converge), the overshoot % past a jump, and the RMS error over the whole period. Every one of those numbers is computed live from the same core code the proofs below run against — there is no second, prettier set of maths behind the curtain.

The maths

All three targets have amplitude ±1 and period 2π. Because each target is an odd function, its Fourier series contains only sine terms — no cosines, no constant term. The three series the core evaluates, exactly as written in the code:

Square: SN(x) = (4/π) · Σk=1..N sin((2k−1)x) / (2k−1) Odd harmonics only, coefficients shrinking as 1/m: 1, 1/3, 1/5, … That slow 1/m decay is the whole story of this wave — it is why the corners take so many terms, and why the ringing at the jump never dies.
Triangle: SN(x) = (8/π²) · Σk=1..N (−1)k−1 sin((2k−1)x) / (2k−1)² Odd harmonics only, but shrinking as 1/m²: 1, 1/9, 1/25, … Squared denominators converge fast and — because the triangle has no jump — they converge uniformly: everywhere at once, peaks included, with no overshoot anywhere.
Sawtooth: SN(x) = (2/π) · Σk=1..N (−1)k+1 sin(kx) / k Every harmonic this time, shrinking as 1/k with alternating signs. The ramp is rebuilt well; the wrap at ±π is a genuine jump, so Gibbs ringing lives at the edges.

Why the overshoot is 8.95%, and why it won't shrink

Near a jump, the partial sum's first peak settles at a fixed multiple of the jump height. For a jump of 2 (from −1 to +1), the peak tends to ≈ 1.179 — an overshoot of ≈ 0.179, which is ≈ 8.95% of the jump. The constant behind it is (2/π)·Si(π) − 1 ≈ 0.0895, where Si is the sine integral. Adding harmonics does not lower the peak; it only pushes it closer to the jump, squeezing the ringing into a narrower band. The energy of the error falls, the height does not. First described by Henry Wilbraham in 1848 and explained by J. Willard Gibbs in 1899, it is not a bug in this exhibit or in anyone's code — it is what sine sums do at a discontinuity.

Reading the three error numbers

  • Max error away from jumps answers "has it converged where convergence is possible?" It deliberately excludes a ±0.35 rad neighbourhood of each jump.
  • Overshoot % answers "how tall is the Gibbs ear?" — (peak − 1) ÷ 2 × 100 for the ±1 waves. For the triangle it reports the peak of the partial sum instead, which stays under 1: no jump, no ear.
  • RMS error averages the squared error over the whole period, jumps included, so it keeps falling with N even for the square and sawtooth — the ringing gets narrower even though it never gets shorter.

Proof — measured against the shipped core

The exhibit's maths lives in a pure core fenced between /* === CORE START === */ and /* === CORE END === */ markers in fourier-builder.html — no DOM, nothing display-side. The page's UI and the proof harness call the same functions. The harness extracts that fenced core, runs its runProofs() under Node, and prints the measurements. Where it bends — named honestly

The triangle threshold. It is tempting to claim the triangle's max error is under 0.005 by N = 25. The exact series says otherwise: measured at N = 25 it is 0.008105, because at the very peak every term adds with the same sign and the tail behaves like the full 1/m² tail, not an alternating one. The error crosses 0.005 at N = 41 (measured 0.004942) and reaches 0.003973 at the slider's maximum, N = 51. The proofs above state the thresholds the series actually meets — 0.01 at N = 25, 0.005 at N = 51 — rather than the tidier claim it doesn't.

Ideal jumps. The targets jump instantly. No real signal does — finite rise time softens Gibbs ringing in hardware (and anti-aliasing filters in audio are, in part, a negotiation with it). The exhibit shows the mathematics in its pure form; the 8.95% figure is the ceiling real systems approach, not a lab measurement.

Finite grids. Peaks and max errors are found by sampling (4,096–8,000 points), so a reported peak can undershoot the true peak by a hair — bounded by one grid step. The Gibbs numbers land within 0.0004 of the theoretical 1.1790 at N = 49, so the grid is not hiding anything material, but it is a grid, and it is named as one.

Display trims. The stacked-lane chart draws at most the first 15 harmonics even when N is larger (it says so on the chart), and the residual plot clips at its ±0.35 scale. The readouts and the proofs are never trimmed — only the pictures are.
