Filter Bench — the lesson

One resistor, one capacitor, wired two ways. That is the whole circuit — and it is enough to build the most-used filter in electronics. The bench draws its Bode plot live: how much a sine wave of each frequency gets scaled (in decibels) and shifted in time (phase), from the exact transfer functions, while a second scope actually simulates the circuit in the time domain so you can watch the sag and the lag happen. This page is the other half: the physics, the math, how the code works, and the proof table — measured numbers, not promises.

What this is

A capacitor is a frequency-dependent resistor. Its reactance, Xc = 1 / (2πfC), is enormous at low frequencies and shrinks toward zero as frequency rises. Put it in series with an ordinary resistor and you have a voltage divider whose split ratio depends on frequency — which is exactly what a filter is.

Take the output across the capacitor and you get a low-pass: slow signals see a big Xc and most of the voltage lands on the capacitor; fast signals see a small Xc and most of the voltage is dropped across the resistor instead. Take the output across the resistor and the same divider reads the other way — a high-pass. Same two parts, two opposite personalities, one knob-pair (R and C) that sets where the changeover happens.

The cutoff frequency, and why −3 dB matters

The changeover centres on the frequency where the resistor and the capacitor's reactance are equal: R = 1 / (2πfC). Solve for f and you get the cutoff:

fc = 1 / (2πRC) The bench's default setting — R = 1 kΩ, C = 1 µF — gives fc = 159.1549 Hz.

At fc the divider splits evenly in magnitude, so the output amplitude is exactly 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 of the input. In decibels that is 20·log₁₀(0.707) = −3.01 dB, and because power goes as amplitude squared, it is also the half-power point — which is why engineers define a filter's bandwidth by its −3 dB point. The phase shift at fc is exactly halfway through its swing: −45° for the low-pass, +45° for the high-pass. Drag the bench's probe to fc and check: the readouts say so, because they are computed from the same formulas printed here.

The rolloff: 20 dB per decade

Far above fc, the low-pass output falls in direct proportion to 1/f: double the frequency, halve the output; ten times the frequency, one tenth the output — −20 dB per decade. That is the signature of a single-pole filter, and its real limitation: one RC stage is a gentle slope, not a brick wall. Serious filters stack poles (or use inductors, or go active with op-amps) to get 40, 60, 80 dB per decade. The high-pass is the mirror: +20 dB per decade rising toward fc from below.

How the core works

The exhibit follows the house pattern: a pure simulation core, fenced in the page between /* === CORE START === */ and /* === CORE END === */, with no DOM access inside it. The user interface and the proof function call the same core, so every number on screen and every number in the table below comes out of one piece of math.

The core has three functions:

  • cutoffFreq(R, C) — returns 1 / (2πRC).
  • transfer(type, f, R, C) — evaluates the exact complex transfer function with x = ωRC = f / fc. Low-pass: H = 1 / (1 + jx), i.e. real part 1 / (1 + x²), imaginary part −x / (1 + x²). High-pass: H = jx / (1 + jx), real part x² / (1 + x²), imaginary part x / (1 + x²). Magnitude, dB, and phase come from that complex number directly — the Bode curves are 240 of these evaluations per redraw.
  • simulate(type, f, R, C) — the honest part. The scope inset does not draw a sine scaled by |H|. It integrates the circuit's own differential equation, RC · dv/dt = vin − v, on an input sine, using the exact exponential update v += (vin − v)(1 − e^(−dt/τ)) at each of 720 steps per period, runs past the settling transient, then measures the output's amplitude and phase by correlating the last five periods against sine and cosine. Low-pass output is the capacitor voltage v; high-pass output is vin − v, the resistor's share.

Two different roads through the same physics, arriving at the same place: that is what a proof is for.

Bends & simplifications, named honestly

The rolloff decade. The −20 dB/decade figure is the asymptotic slope. If you measure the literal secant from fc to 10·fc on the exact curve you get −17.0329 dB (and +17.0329 dB for the HP secant from fc/10 to fc) — not because the filter underperforms, but because at fc the curve is still bending away from the flat passband; the −3.01 dB already "spent" at the corner is part of that decade's drop. So the proof measures the slope over the decade where the asymptote actually holds (10·fc → 100·fc), and this paragraph exists so nobody has to take that choice on faith.

Simulation vs phasor. The scope's numbers are close to the transfer function's, not identical: at the proof resolution the simulated phase at fc is −44.7504° against the exact −45°, and at the coarser resolution the live UI uses it reads about −44.5°. That gap is real numerical-integration behaviour (the input is held constant across each step), it shrinks with smaller steps, and the bench shows you the simulated numbers as measured rather than quietly substituting the exact ones.

Ideal parts. The core's resistor and capacitor are perfect: no capacitor leakage or equivalent series resistance, no stray inductance, a source with zero output impedance and an output loaded by nothing. A real RC filter built on a breadboard bends all of these a little, and a real load bends the cutoff itself. The math here is the ideal the real parts approximate.

One pole. Everything on this bench is a single RC stage. The gentle 20 dB/decade slope is the honest behaviour of that circuit — steeper filters are different circuits, not this one trying harder.
