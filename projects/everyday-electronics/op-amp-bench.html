What this is

An ideal op-amp on a bench: pick a circuit, drive it with a signal, and watch input and output on the scope. Every number on this page is computed by the same pure core that the built-in proofs test.

The two golden rules (ideal op-amp, negative feedback)

1. No current flows into either input. 2. The op-amp drives its output so the two inputs sit at the same voltage (V+ = V−, the “virtual short” — and when V+ is grounded, V− is a “virtual ground”). Those two rules, plus Ohm's law, give every gain formula on this page.

Modes

Inverting amplifier: gain = −Rf/Rin. The minus sign is real — the output flips. Non-inverting amplifier: gain = 1 + Rf/Rin, always ≥ 1, no flip. Integrator: the feedback part is a capacitor; Vout = −(1/RC)∫Vin dt with R = Rin. A steady DC input makes a straight ramp; a sine makes a cosine-shaped output whose amplitude is Vin_peak/(2πfRC).

Every control

Rin / Rf sliders set the resistors (kΩ). C slider (integrator only) sets the feedback capacitor in µF; RC is shown in the readouts. Signal picks sine, square or DC. Amplitude is the input peak in volts (the DC level for DC). Frequency is in Hz (ignored for DC). Supply rails ±V set the saturation limits from 5 V to 15 V.

The scope and schematic

Cyan is Vin, amber is Vout. Dashed rose lines are the ±rails. The circuit drawing redraws for each mode and its component labels update with the sliders. Measured gain is read back off the simulated trace itself (peak out ÷ peak in for amplifiers), not copied from the formula — if the output is clipping, measured gain drops below theory, and the page says so.

Simplifications, named

This is an ideal op-amp: infinite open-loop gain and bandwidth, no slew-rate limit, no input bias current, no offset voltage, inputs draw nothing. The one non-ideal behaviour modelled is saturation: a hard clip at the rails — the output stops exactly at ±V and stays flat there. A real part rounds off near the rails, often can't reach them at all, and a real integrator drifts and needs a reset or a large feedback resistor; this one integrates forever and, while saturated, holds at the rail (its internal state is clamped, so it comes back off the rail as soon as the input reverses).

Proofs

The panel under the scope runs the shipped core against the textbook claims — inverting and non-inverting gains, exact rail saturation, and the integrator's DC slope and sine amplitude — and prints measured numbers, live, in your browser.

Op-Amp Bench

Two golden rules run the whole bench: no current into the inputs, and the inputs held equal. Set the resistors, pick a signal — then push the gain until the output hits the rails and watch a clean sine turn into a clipped one.
