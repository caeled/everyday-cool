Sampling Lab — the lesson

Every digital recording you have ever heard is a list of numbers taken at regular instants — samples — pretending to be a smooth wave. Most of the time the pretence is perfect. This lab is about the one condition that makes it perfect, and what happens, exactly and measurably, when you break it: sample slower than twice per cycle and the samples stop describing your signal. They describe a different one — and no amount of cleverness afterwards can tell. This page is the other half of the exhibit: the rule in plain words, the math the page runs, the proofs with their real measured numbers, and the bends, named.

What this is

Two views of one act of sampling. In the first, a sine wave is measured fs times a second, and a machine tries to rebuild the wave from the measurements alone. In the second, a spinning wheel is “measured” by a strobe light that flashes at a fixed rate — your eye under a strobe, or a camera filming a wheel, is a sampler too, and it falls for the same trick. That second view is the wagon-wheel effect: the wheel in a film that looks frozen at speed, creeps forward, or rolls backwards while the car drives on.

Nothing in either view is faked for effect. The alias on screen is not drawn from a formula typed into the drawing code: the page's core generates the samples, measures where their energy actually sits, and rebuilds a wave from the samples alone. The formula only gets to predict. The samples get the last word.

Nyquist in plain words

A wave repeats. To know which wave you are looking at, you have to catch it at least twice per repeat — once somewhere on the way up, once somewhere on the way down, roughly speaking. Catch it less often and a slower wave can be drawn through exactly the same catch-points. Not approximately the same points. The same points.

That is the whole theorem wearing work clothes. The formal version — the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem — says a signal containing no frequencies above B Hz can be perfectly rebuilt from samples taken faster than 2B per second. The rate 2B (here, fs/2 for a signal of frequency f) is the Nyquist rate, and fs/2 itself is the Nyquist frequency: the highest frequency a given sampler can honestly represent.

Go under it and the too-fast wave doesn't come back noisy or distorted. It comes back as a clean, confident, wrong wave — its alias. A 7 Hz wave sampled at 10 Hz does not look like a damaged 7 Hz wave. It looks like a perfect 3 Hz wave. Every sample agrees. There is no residue, no clue, nothing left to appeal to. That is why engineers put an anti-aliasing filter before the sampler in real equipment: once the fold has happened, it has happened for good.

The fold, as arithmetic

alias: k = round(f / fs),  signed = f − k·fs,  folded = |signed| The true frequency is reflected back and forth into the honest band [0, fs/2] like a ball bouncing between mirrors spaced fs apart. f = 7, fs = 10: k = 1, signed = −3, folded = 3 Hz. f = 12, fs = 10: k = 1, signed = +2, folded = 2 Hz. The sign is not bookkeeping: a negative signed alias is a wave whose phase runs backwards — which is precisely a wheel turning in reverse.
samples: s[n] = sin(2πf·n / fs + φ) The only thing a digital system ever owns. Everything on the exhibit page — the predicted alias, the measured alias, the reconstruction, the wheel — is computed from sequences like this one and the wheel's version of it, θ[n] = (2π·frot·n / fstrobe) mod 2π.

How the page measures, instead of just predicting

The exhibit shows two alias numbers side by side, and they come from different places. Predicted is the fold formula above. Measured is earned: the core takes 200 samples of the signal (at a fixed phase φ = 0.4 rad — a generic phase, not a flattering one), runs a DFT (discrete Fourier transform) over that sequence alone, searching [0, fs/2] in 0.05 Hz steps, and reports where the peak actually is. The measuring routine is never told the true frequency. When prediction and measurement agree — and in the proofs below they agree to the last displayed digit — that agreement is the experiment working, not an echo.

The reconstruction is the other honest machine on the page. It is ideal sinc interpolation computed from the samples: each sample contributes a sinc bump, sin(πx)/(πx), centred on its instant, and the bumps are summed — the textbook band-limited rebuild the sampling theorem promises. Below Nyquist it lands on the true wave. Above Nyquist it lands, just as faithfully, on the alias — because the alias is the band-limited wave those samples actually describe. Proof P6 below puts numbers on both halves of that sentence.

The wheel runs the same core in angle form. Between two strobe flashes the wheel turns by 2π·frot/fstrobe; fold that step into (−180°, +180°] and multiply by the strobe rate and you get the apparent rate your eye will report. 9 rev/s caught at 10 flashes a second advances 324° per flash — which your visual system reads, correctly given what it was shown, as −36° per flash: −1 rev/s, backwards. At exactly the strobe rate the step is a full 360°, folds to 0, and the wheel is frozen.

Bends named honestly

The reconstruction is ideal sinc interpolation over a finite window — and finite is doing real work in that sentence. The theorem's perfect rebuild sums sinc bumps from infinitely many samples. The page sums a window: 200 samples in the proofs, and in the live view the samples in the window plus a margin on each side. Truncation leaves a small error that is worst near the window's edges, which is why the proofs grade the reconstruction only in the interior — t from 5 s to 15 s of the 20 s proof window, 401 evaluation points — and why the live amber trace can wobble slightly where it enters and leaves the screen. The stated bound, RMS < 0.02 in the interior, is what the finite machine actually achieves (0.00218 and 0.00411 measured); no infinite-window claim is made.

The DFT measurement has a resolution, and it is stated: peak search in 0.05 Hz steps over [0, fs/2] on a 200-sample window. Frequencies that fall between steps, or two tones closer together than the window can separate, would measure less cleanly than the single-tone proofs here. The ±0.3 Hz tolerances in P1–P4 are sized to that honesty, and the measured values landed on 3.00, 3.00, 2.00 and 1.00 anyway.

One spoke of the wheel is painted amber, on purpose. Six identical spokes have a 60° symmetry, so an unmarked wheel adds its own symmetry aliasing on top of the strobe effect — real, but a second phenomenon. The painted spoke lets your eye follow one point of the rim, which is what the core's angle arithmetic tracks. The faint cyan “true wheel” is likewise a teaching overlay: no strobe-lit room shows you the true rotation alongside the sampled one.

The strobe wheel is sample-and-hold. Between flashes the bright wheel sits exactly on its last sampled angle. A real eye has persistence of vision and a real camera has a shutter interval and motion blur; both soften the steps. The arithmetic of which angles get sampled — the part that decides forward, frozen, or reversed — is not softened at all.

Exactly at Nyquist is a knife edge, and the page says so. At f = fs/2 the samples alternate between two values and the phase decides whether they carry the wave or nearly miss it. The exhibit flags that case in words rather than letting a lucky phase pose as a clean capture.

Things to try in the exhibit

  1. Start at f = 7 Hz, fs = 10 Hz. The amber reconstruction ignores the cyan 7 Hz wave and draws the dashed rose alias instead — then drag f down through 5 Hz and watch it snap onto the truth.
  2. Set f = 9 Hz, fs = 10 Hz and read the signed alias: −1 Hz. A wave running backwards, and the wheel below is doing the same thing in a circle.
  3. Wheel: rotation 9 rev/s, strobe 10/s — reversed at −1 rev/s. Raise the rotation to exactly 10 rev/s: frozen. Raise it to 11: it creeps forward at +1 rev/s, having passed through frozen.
  4. Drop fs toward its minimum with f high and count how many times the fold bounces — k = round(f/fs) in the formula is the number of mirrors it hit on the way down.
