Don't Cook the LED — the lesson

The game is seven small jobs: a supply, an LED, and a drawer of resistors. Land the current inside the band and the LED lights; overshoot and it cooks. Underneath every level is one line of Ohm's law and one fact about LEDs that surprises almost everyone the first time: the LED does not decide how much current it takes. The resistor does. This page is the other half of the game — the physics, the math, the worked answers, and how it is built — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

The physics

An LED is not a little light bulb

A filament bulb is a resistor that glows: feed it more voltage and it takes proportionally more current, politely, by Ohm's law. An LED is a diode — a one-way door for current — and it behaves almost the opposite way. Once the voltage across it reaches its forward voltage (Vf), the door opens and the LED holds that voltage roughly constant while passing whatever current the rest of the circuit allows. A red LED holds about 2.0 V whether it is sipping 2 mA or being force-fed 200 mA. It does not throttle itself. It cannot. There is nothing in it that says "enough."

Why a bare LED on a battery dies

Connect an LED straight across a 5 V supply — the wire-only chip in Level 1 — and the arithmetic has nowhere to land. The LED takes its 2.0 V; the remaining 3.0 V has to fall somewhere, and with no resistor the only resistance left is the LED's own tiny internal slope and the battery's insides. Tiny resistance under 3 V means enormous current, and current is heat in a very small crystal. The junction overheats faster than the package can shed it, and the LED dies — in real life in a blink, sometimes with a smell you remember. The game makes it instant and calls it what it is: cooked. Nothing about the supply was "too strong." The circuit was simply missing the part whose entire job is to be the limit.

The resistor's job: drop the leftover, set the flow

Put a resistor in series and the picture becomes civilised. Voltages around a loop must add up to the supply, so the resistor takes whatever the LED doesn't: the leftover (supply − Vf). And because the resistor does obey Ohm's law, that leftover across a known resistance sets one definite current — the same current everywhere in the series loop, through resistor and LED alike. Choosing the resistor is choosing the current. Too small a resistor and the current overshoots the LED's comfort (in the game, the top of the band); too large and the LED starves into a dim glow. The LED's brightness is, to a good working approximation, just its current wearing a visible form.

The resistor pays for its work in heat

Dropping voltage at a current is power, and power has to go somewhere: the resistor turns the leftover × current into heat. On a 5 V job that is a whisper (Level 1's winner sheds 0.06 W). On Level 6's 24 V job it is 0.43 W — real warmth — and now the part's wattage rating matters as much as its value. A resistor's ohms say how much current it will allow; its watts say how much punishment its body can shed. The right value in too small a body cooks itself, which is why Level 6 grades the body as well as the number.

Series LEDs add their tolls

String two LEDs in series (Level 4) and each still keeps its own forward voltage — 2.0 V + 2.0 V = 4.0 V before the resistor sees a volt — while the single current flows through both. One resistor still runs the whole string; you just do the subtraction with a bigger Vf.

The math the game runs

Four short formulas, exactly as the game's code states them. Everything else is substitution.

Vleftover = Vsupply − Vf What the resistor has to drop. Level 1: 5 − 2.0 = 3.0 V. Level 6: 24 − 3.2 = 20.8 V — which is why that level gets hot.
I = (Vsupply − Vf) / R Ohm's law, aimed at the resistor: the leftover voltage across R sets the current for the whole loop. This is the game's currentMA(), in amps before it is scaled to mA.
Rwanted = (Vsupply − Vf) / Itarget The same law read backwards — how you shop. Decide the current first (the game's jobs all ask for about 20 mA), then divide. If the answer isn't a value in the drawer, the nearest E12 neighbour usually lands inside the band.
Presistor = I² × R The resistor's heat bill — the game's resistorPowerW(). It equals Vleftover × I as well (the two are the same fact wearing different algebra), and it must come in under the part's wattage rating or the part becomes the fuse.

Forward voltage is the colour's fingerprint

Vf is not arbitrary: it tracks the energy of the photons the LED makes. Red light is the cheapest to produce, blue and white cost more per photon, and the voltage follows the photon bill. Typical values — your LED's packet is the authority for your LED:

Why E12 values are those odd numbers

The drawer holds 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82 — and then the same numbers times ten, forever. Nobody chose them to be awkward. Each step multiplies by the twelfth root of ten (≈ 1.21), so the values climb by ratio rather than by difference: twelve equal percentage steps per decade. The spacing is matched to how resistors are actually made and graded — E12 parts carry ±10% tolerance, so a value's real resistance can wander a tenth either way, and steps of about a fifth mean the wanderings just about meet: some E12 value is always within reach of the resistance you calculated, at any size, from ohms to megohms. That is also why the game can be fair: your ideal 350 Ω isn't in the drawer, but 330 Ω and 390 Ω bracket it, and both land in the band.

Worked examples — the game's own levels, solved

Before the game shipped, a script played it: the proof test lifts the game's physics core out of the shipped file and checks that every level offers a win, that a "nearest E12 to ideal" strategy wins all seven, and that the wire really does cook. These are its numbers.

Example 1: Level 1, end to end (5 V, red LED)

Supply 5 V, red LED Vf 2.0 V, target about 20 mA, band 14–26 mA.

  • Leftover: 5 − 2.0 = 3.0 V.
  • Wanted R: 3.0 V ÷ 0.020 A = 150 Ω — an E12 value exactly. Fit it and the current is 3.0 ÷ 150 = 20.0 mA, dead centre of the band.
  • Its heat: P = 0.020² × 150 = 0.06 W — any small resistor loafs through this job.
  • The near misses teach the edges: 100 Ω gives 30.0 mA — cooked, over the 26 mA top. 220 Ω gives 13.6 mA — dim, just under the 14 mA floor. One E12 step either way of perfect is the difference between cooked, lit, and dim; that sensitivity is the subject.
  • And the wire: R = 0 divides by zero — in the game, unbounded current and an instant cook; in life, "merely" every milliamp the supply and the LED's insides can conspire to pass, which is more than enough.

Example 2: Level 6, the wattage level (24 V, white LED)

Supply 24 V, white LED Vf 3.2 V, target about 20 mA — and this time you pick a wattage too.

  • Leftover: 24 − 3.2 = 20.8 V. Wanted R: 20.8 ÷ 0.020 = 1040 Ω → nearest E12 is 1000 Ω.
  • Current: 20.8 ÷ 1000 = 20.8 mA — in the band. Half the job done.
  • Heat: P = 0.0208² × 1000 ≈ 0.43 W. Fit that resistor as a ¼ W part and the value is right while the part cooks itself — the game's resistor-burned outcome. Fit it as ½ W and it wins, with the rating honestly earned.
  • The trap inside the level: 820 Ω is also in the band (25.4 mA), but it sheds ≈ 0.53 W — even the ½ W body burns under it. Only the 1 W part survives that pick. Resistance is only half of a resistor's name.

How it's built — the coding choices

Don't Cook the LED is one HTML file with no libraries, and its code is arranged the way the collection's games are: the physics lives apart from the page, so the game can be proven, not just played.

A pure-function core, fenced between markers

Everything electrical sits in one block of code between the comments /* === CORE === */ and /* === END CORE === */: two small functions — currentMA(vs, vf, r) and resistorPowerW(vs, vf, r) — the E12 series as data, the seven levels as a data array, and a judge(level, choiceR, choiceW) function that takes a choice and returns the outcome with the numbers behind it, so the page can show its working ("30.0 mA — over the 26 mA top of the band") instead of just saying wrong. The core knows nothing about buttons or SVG; the page is a shell around it.

The proof is a script that plays the game

Because the core is separable, the test that ships with the collection lifts that exact block out of the shipped file and plays headlessly. It asserts three things: every chip in every drawer is a genuine E12 value (or Level 1's deliberate wire); every level offers at least one winning choice, wattage included; a scripted "best pick" — the offered value nearest the ideal resistance, in the smallest body that survives it — wins all seven levels; and the wire-only trap on Level 1 judges cooked with unbounded current. If a future edit retunes a level into impossibility, the proof fails before a player can. The table above is its output, not an illustration.

The lesson is a page, not a printout

The 📖 Lesson button in the game opens this page in an overlay, loaded lazily the first time it is asked for, closable with Esc, a click outside, or its Close button — the same back-door pattern as the collection's other games. There is no simulation running behind it here, so nothing needs pausing: reading mid-choice costs the player nothing, which is when the reading is most useful.

Simplifications, named

Stated plainly: the game's LEDs are modelled at a fixed forward voltage — a real LED's Vf creeps up a little as current rises and drifts with temperature, and its current-versus-voltage curve is a steep slope, not a cliff; the game keeps the room's own bench model (fixed Vf, typical datasheet values) because the lesson is the resistor's job, not the diode's curve. For the same reason the game gives the LED no internal resistance, which is why the wire-only current is "infinite" in the game and merely enormous in life — in life the battery's internal resistance and the LED's slope are the only limits, and they are not enough. The supply is ideal: a real 9 V battery sags under load and as it ages, and a coin cell's high internal resistance is exactly why the room's paper-circuit and throwie builds can run an LED with no resistor at all — a small, weak source is its own limit, and nothing bigger gets that excuse. The win band (target ±30%) is the game's chosen comfort zone, not a datasheet limit; real LEDs carry a rated maximum current (often 20–30 mA) and live happily dimmer for far longer. A resistor in the game burns the moment its dissipation passes its rating; a real part gets hot first, drifts, and wants headroom — working builders routinely fit twice the wattage the arithmetic demands, and Level 6's ½ W winner at 0.43 W would get exactly that raised eyebrow on a real bench. Brightness is treated as proportional to current, which is close enough to teach with and not exact. And three cooks refilling the LED is a kindness of the parts bin; the real bin charges you money.
