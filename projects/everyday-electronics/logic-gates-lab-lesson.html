Logic Gates Lab — the lesson

The exhibit — it calls itself Logic Bench on its own canvas — is a working digital workbench: every basic gate, a transistor-level CMOS X-ray, latches and flip-flops, a 4-bit ripple-carry ALU that adds and subtracts, a counter, a shift register, a ring oscillator, a decoder and multiplexer, and a blank sandbox where you wire your own. Underneath all of it is one idea, the idea every computer is built from: a gate is a rule about voltages — 1 is high, 0 is low, and the rule never negotiates. Chain enough rules together and the chains start doing arithmetic, remembering, and counting. This page is the other half of the exhibit — the truth tables, the worked answers, and the audit: every circuit in the lab, driven exhaustively through its own shipped code and checked against the standard tables.

⚠ False claims found — read these first

1. The HEX display's “DEC” caption lies in Subtract mode (ALU preset). The 7-segment glyph itself is always right, but the caption under it is computed as DEC = digit + (decimal-point pin ? 16 : 0), and in the ALU the decimal-point pin is wired to COUT. That is correct for addition, where COUT really is the 16s place. In Subtract mode COUT = 1 means “no borrow” — it is not a 16s place — yet the caption still adds 16. That is wrong on 136 of the 256 subtract rows (every row with A ≥ B): 5 − 3 displays the correct glyph 2 with the caption “DEC 18.” The telemetry readout at top-left computes subtraction correctly — trust it, not the caption.

2. The same caption lies in the Decoder preset. There the decimal-point pin is wired to ENABLE, so whenever the decoder is enabled the caption reads DEC = index + 16: selecting line 1 shows the correct glyph 1 captioned “DEC 17.” The index is 1. The caption is adding a pin that was never a digit.

3. “SR & D Flip-Flop” is a mislabel. The preset button and its notes call the second memory element a flip-flop. It is not one: the audit drove it and it is a level-sensitive gated D latch — while CLK = 1 the output follows D continuously (verified: D flipped 1→0 with CLK held high and Q followed), and it only holds when CLK falls. A flip-flop changes only on a clock edge. The exhibit's own on-canvas group title gets it right (“GATED D-LATCH — TRANSPARENT WHEN CLK=1”); the drawer button and the architecture notes overclaim. The real edge-triggered D and T flip-flops live in the counter, shift register, and sandbox palette, and those passed every edge test below.

Everything else on the page — every truth table, every readout formula, the ring-oscillator period, the CMOS current claim — checked out against driven output, as the proofs below record. Two things the lab simply does not offer, so there was nothing to test: there is no XNOR gate and no buffer anywhere (not in the palette, not in the evaluation engine), and the 3-input AND / 4-input OR used inside the decoder and MUX presets cannot be placed in the sandbox — the palette stocks only the 2-input versions.

What this is

A logic gate takes one or two (sometimes more) binary inputs and produces one binary output, according to a rule you can write down completely in a truth table — every possible input combination, and the output for each. That completeness is what makes digital logic auditable in a way analogue circuits are not: a 2-input gate has exactly four rows, and you can test all four. This audit did exactly that, for everything, with no sampling.

  • NOT flips its input. AND is 1 only when all inputs are 1. OR is 1 when any input is 1. XOR is 1 when its inputs differ.
  • NAND and NOR are AND and OR with the output flipped — and each is universal: either one alone can build every other gate, which is why real chips are so often seas of NANDs.
  • CMOS is how the gates are actually built: a pull-up network of PMOS transistors connects the output to the supply, a complementary pull-down network of NMOS transistors connects it to ground, and in any steady state exactly one network is on — so a settled gate draws (ideally) no static current at all.
  • Feedback turns gates into memory. Cross-couple two NORs and the pair holds whichever state it was last pushed into: that is the SR latch, one bit of storage made of nothing but gates and a loop. Add clock gating and you get latches; make the storage change only on a clock edge and you get flip-flops — the atoms of counters, registers, and every state machine.
  • Adders are gates arranged to do sums. A half adder (XOR + AND) adds two bits. A full adder adds two bits plus a carry-in. Cascade four full adders, letting each carry-out feed the next carry-in, and you have a ripple-carry adder; invert B and inject a carry-in of 1 and the same hardware subtracts, by two's complement: A − B = A + ~B + 1.

Timing, delay, and hazards — what the engine actually does

The lab's central timing claim is the t_pd slider: a per-gate propagation delay of 5–120 ns. The engine does model delay — really, not decoratively — but it splits each delay between the wire and the gate, and it is worth knowing the split because everything else follows from it:

delay per stage = wire travel (0.64 · tpd) + gate delay (0.36 · tpd) = 1.00 · tpd Measured, not read off the constants: a step into a lone NOT gate changed its output exactly 25.0 ns of sim time after the switch flipped at tpd = 25 ns, and exactly 100.0 ns at tpd = 100 ns. The signal then needed one more wire time — 0.64 · tpd — to reach the next pin (41.0 ns and 164.1 ns total to the LED). So the honest statement is: one gate plus its output wire costs exactly one tpd, which is how the presets are tuned. Sim time itself is slowed-down time: 1 ns of sim time takes 6.8 ms of wall-clock, which is the whole point — you can watch a nanosecond-scale event with your eyes.
  • Carry ripple — measured. Set A = 15, B = 0, then flip B0 (the “15 + 1 Ripple!” button does exactly this). At tpd = 25 ns the sum bits fell at 91, 116, 166, and 216 ns and COUT rose at 241 ns ≈ 9.6 · tpd; at tpd = 100 ns every number scaled by exactly four (COUT at 964 ns). The carry really does ripple stage by stage, at uneven per-bit cost (the carry path is two gates deep in some slices), and the total is honestly ~10 gate delays for a 4-bit add — the true weakness of ripple-carry, visible.
  • Ring oscillator period — exact. The notes claim T = 2 · N · tpd = 6 · tpd for the 3-inverter ring. Measured periods: 150 ns at tpd = 25, 360 ns at 60, 720 ns at 120 — on the formula to the nanosecond at all three settings, and the telemetry frequency readout (1000 / (6 · tpd) MHz) agrees. With ENABLE = 0 the ring stops and parks, as it should: the first “inverter” is really a NAND gated by ENABLE.
  • Hazards — real, and reproducible. The engine's delays are genuine enough to produce the textbook static hazard. Wire Y = A · ¬A in the sandbox (ideal output: always 0) and flip A from 0 to 1: the direct path reaches the AND a full stage before the inverted path does, and the output pulses high for 25 ns of sim time — exactly 1 · tpd — before settling back to 0. The 1 → 0 transition glitches not at all, which is also correct for this topology. The lab never claims hazards, but it has them, the way real hardware does; that is a point in its favour, and a fine thing to go and see.
  • Short pulses get swallowed — the delay is inertial, not transport. A pulse narrower than the gate delay never appears at the output: measured, a 0.2 · tpd pulse into a NOT gate vanished entirely, while a 0.5 · tpd pulse came through. Real gates behave this way too (it is part of why hazard glitches have a minimum effective width), but know that the model is “a change must persist to be believed,” not a perfect delay line.
  • CMOS switching — an RC picture, honestly drawn. In the X-Ray the output does not snap; it charges/discharges exponentially toward the rail with a time constant scaled from tpd, and the supply-current spike lives only during that swing — in steady state the measured spike decays to exactly zero and the meter reads 0.00 µA, as the page claims. The size of the spike (up to 420 µA on the meter) is an invented display scale, not a device calculation — see bends.

Bends named honestly

The delay is split between wire and gate. A gate element alone accounts for only 0.36 · tpd; the wire feeding it carries the other 0.64. Per stage the total is exactly tpd, so every preset-level claim holds — but if you count gates in a hand-built chain and multiply by tpd, add a wire time for the final hop to your LED, or your total will read ~0.64 · tpd short of the last leg.

Inertial delay swallows narrow pulses. If an input changes back before a gate's delay expires, the pending output change is cancelled and its timer restarted — pulses under roughly a third of tpd simply never happened, as far as downstream logic is concerned. Measured above. Real silicon is similar in spirit, different in detail.

The SR latch's forbidden release never resolves. S = R = 1 correctly forces Q = Q̄ = 0. But release both inputs in the same instant and the two NORs, having identical delays, chase each other forever: the audit watched Q/Q̄ oscillate 00 ↔ 11 indefinitely, never settling. A real latch resolves — unpredictably, via tiny mismatches and metastability — to one side or the other. This engine has no mismatch to break the tie, so the race is a permanent oscillation. It is an honest picture of why the state is forbidden; just don't wait for it to pick a winner.

The CMOS current meter's scale is invented. The shape is right — zero at rest, a spike while the load capacitance charges — but the 420 µA full-scale is a display choice (iSpike × 420), not computed from any transistor size, supply, or capacitance. The X-Ray's pull-up/pull-down logic, by contrast, is exact: complementary in all 10 rows.

Voltages are normalised, then labelled 5 V. Inside, signals are 0/1 and the CMOS output is a 0…1 ramp; the readouts multiply by 5 and call it volts, and the legend calls HIGH “+5V.” Nothing in the engine models actual voltage thresholds, noise margins, or a gate's behaviour between the rails (except the X-Ray's 0.5 decision point). It is a logic simulator wearing a voltmeter's badge — fine, as long as you know which one you're holding.

The counter's skew is real, and the notes admit it. Each flip-flop is clocked by the previous stage's Q̄, so bits change in a staggered ripple, not together — the architecture notes say so, the logic analyzer shows it, and the count sequence is still exactly right once settled. Likewise the first ring-oscillator “inverter” is an ENABLE-gated NAND; the canvas labels it “INV 1,” which is what it acts as while ENABLE = 1.

Small display omissions. The MUX preset's logic-analyzer channels omit D3 (D0, D1, D2, and OUT Y are shown), and the sandbox palette omits the AND3/OR4 multi-input gates the presets use internally. Neither affects any logic; both are places a careful reader's count will come up one short.
