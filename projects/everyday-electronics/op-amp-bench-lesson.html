Op-Amp Bench — the lesson

The bench is three circuits built from one strange little triangle: an amplifier so eager that, left alone, it would slam its output to a supply rail at the slightest provocation. Tame it with negative feedback and two rules fall out — and those two rules predict every trace the scope draws. The op-amp does not set the gain. The resistors do. This page is the other half of the bench — the rules, the formulas, the proof numbers, and an honest list of what the model leaves out — readable while you play, from the 📖 Lesson button in the game.

What this is

An operational amplifier is a differential amplifier with enormous open-loop gain: it outputs (roughly) its gain multiplied by the difference between its two inputs, Vout ≈ A·(V+ − V−), with A in the hundreds of thousands. A difference of a few microvolts would be enough to drive the output all the way to a supply rail. That sounds useless, and open-loop it nearly is — a comparator, not an amplifier. The trick of every circuit on the bench is negative feedback: feed some of the output back to the − input, and the op-amp is forced to behave, because any error at its inputs gets amplified into a correction. The bench lets you build the three textbook feedback circuits — inverting amplifier, non-inverting amplifier, and integrator — drive them with a sine, square or DC input, set the supply rails from ±5 V to ±15 V, and read the gain back off the simulated trace.

The golden rules

For an ideal op-amp in a stable negative-feedback circuit, two rules hold. They are not laws of physics; they are what infinite gain forces to be true whenever the output is not saturated.

  1. No current flows into either input. The inputs are pure voltage sensors. Whatever current arrives at a node through a resistor must leave through another resistor — it cannot disappear into the op-amp.
  2. V+ = V−. If the inputs differed by more than microvolts, infinite gain would rail the output; feedback settles exactly where the difference is driven to (almost) zero. This is the “virtual short.” When V+ is grounded, V− sits at 0 V without being connected to ground — a virtual ground.

Inverting amplifier: gain = −Rf/Rin

The input drives Rin into the − node, and + is grounded, so the − node is a virtual ground. The current through Rin is Vin/Rin; by rule 1 none of it enters the op-amp, so all of it flows through Rf, dropping Vin·(Rf/Rin) across it — on the far side of a 0 V node, which puts the output at the negative of that drop.

Vout = −(Rf/Rin) · Vin Bench default: Rin 10 kΩ, Rf 47 kΩ → gain −4.70. A 1 V peak sine comes out at 4.70 V peak, upside down.

Non-inverting amplifier: gain = 1 + Rf/Rin

Now the signal drives + directly. Rule 2 says − follows it, and Rin/Rf form a divider from the output down to that node — so the output must be the input scaled up by the divider's inverse. The gain can never be less than 1, and there is no sign flip.

Vout = (1 + Rf/Rin) · Vin Same resistors as above → gain +5.70. With Rf = Rin the gain is exactly 2; with Rf = 0 it is the unity-gain buffer, 1.

Integrator: the resistor's partner becomes a capacitor

Swap Rf for a capacitor C and the feedback element stops dropping a fixed voltage and starts accumulating one. The current through Rin (still Vin/R, still all of it, by rule 1) charges C, and the output is whatever voltage that charge requires:

Vout = −(1/RC) ∫ Vin dt,   R = Rin Constant Vin = 1 V with RC = 0.01 s gives a ramp of slope −100 V/s. A sine of peak A at frequency f comes out as a cosine shape of amplitude A/(2πfRC) — double the frequency, halve the output, and shift it a quarter-cycle.

The bench's integrator core really does integrate, sample by sample (trapezoidal steps at a fixed dt), rather than drawing the closed-form answer — which is why the proofs below can measure a slope and an amplitude off the same samples the scope draws.

The amplifier gains land at exactly 0.0000% because, inside the linear region, the model is the formula — the honest reading of that is “the trace and the formula are the same code path,” not “real op-amps are this exact.” The integrator's sine amplitude is the interesting one: it comes out of thousands of accumulated integration steps, and it still lands within 0.0001% of the closed-form answer.

Where it bends — named honestly

Ideal op-amp. The model assumes infinite open-loop gain and infinite bandwidth, so the golden rules hold exactly and the gain never rolls off with frequency. A real part (a 741, say) has finite gain-bandwidth: push the frequency or the gain high enough and the real output shrinks and phase-shifts. This one never does.

No slew-rate limit. Real op-amps can only move their output so many volts per microsecond; a fast, large signal comes out triangular. Here the output follows the formula instantly, however steep that is.

No input bias current, no offset voltage. Real inputs sip a little current and are never perfectly matched, so real circuits show small output offsets — worst in a bare integrator, where bias current charges the capacitor and the output drifts to a rail on its own. This integrator only moves when the input tells it to.

Saturation is a hard clip at the rails. That is stated plainly because it is a modelling choice, not a fact: the output stops exactly at ±V and sits flat there. Real parts round off approaching the rails, many cannot swing within a volt or two of them, and recovery from saturation takes time. The one behaviour chosen deliberately: while saturated, the integrator holds at the rail — its internal state is clamped rather than winding up beyond the rail — so it leaves the rail as soon as the input reverses. A real integrator's capacitor keeps the true accumulated charge instead.

No feedback resistor on the integrator, no reset switch, perfect components. Resistors and the capacitor are exact, noiseless, and temperature-proof. Real ones are not, which is why real gains are a percent or two off the printed ratio before anything else goes wrong.
