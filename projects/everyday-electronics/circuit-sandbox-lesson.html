Circuit Sandbox — the lesson

The exhibit — it calls itself Breadboard — is not a toy with a few canned circuits. It is a general circuit solver: you place parts on a grid, and a real Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) engine computes every node voltage and branch current, timestep by timestep. That makes it the riskiest kind of exhibit to ship, because a solver can be quietly wrong in ways a picture never is. So this lesson does not take its word for anything. The shipped solver was extracted from the page byte-for-byte, driven headlessly in Node through circuits built with its own component format, and every number below was checked against an independent computation — a separate nodal analysis and a separate Shockley diode solve written for this audit, sharing no code with the page.

Verdict — and the flags, up top

The solver is right. On every ordinary circuit tested — divider, parallel branches, an unbalanced bridge, RC and RL transients, a diode, an LED, an AC low-pass in phasor steady state, a ringing RLC, a free-running LC tank — the sandbox's own reported voltages and currents agree with independent computation to between 0.00003% and 0.4%, and the largest errors are the known, honest cost of its Backward-Euler timestep, shrinking exactly as the timestep shrinks. Kirchhoff's current law holds at its nodes to ~10⁻¹⁰ A in its own reported numbers. No preset note was found making a false claim about a normal operating point; the checks of those claims are in the tables.

Flag 1 — a short across a source is silent. A plain wire across a 9 V source makes the solver report 8,911 A through the wire, flag itself “Converged,” and raise no warning of any kind. Two conflicting voltage sources in parallel (5 V ∥ 9 V) likewise settle quietly at ±1,980 A. The only over-current warning in the whole exhibit is for LEDs above 40 mA. On a real bench the first circuit is a fire; here it is a number in small type. Treat huge currents as the smoke they represent.

Flag 2 — the diode model bends hard above ~10 A. The diode's exponential is replaced by a straight-line extension once Vd passes 30·nVT ≈ 0.89 V. At ≈99 A forward current the sandbox reports Vd = 1.137 V where the plain Shockley model it otherwise implements says 0.958 V — an 18.6% overstatement of the drop (the current, set mostly by the series resistor, stays within 0.4%). Nobody sane runs 99 A through this breadboard's diode, but the bend is real and it is named here, not hidden.

Flag 3 — floating nodes never complain. A disconnected island of parts reads a clean 0.000 V and “Converged,” because the solver quietly ties every node to ground through a 10⁻¹¹ S leakage (GMIN). That is good numerical hygiene and a quiet lie at the same time: a floating node on a real board reads whatever it pleases.

Small correction — the LED preset note. The LED lab note says the red LED runs at “21 mA.” The solver's own operating point, confirmed independently, is 21.50 mA — the note understates it by about 2.4%. Everything else in the preset notes checked out as written, including the bridge's “exact 0.00 mA” at balance, which really is exact.

What this is

Every circuit, from a flashlight to a power grid, obeys two bookkeeping rules stated by Kirchhoff in 1845. KCL: the currents entering any node sum to zero — charge does not pile up. KVL: the voltages around any closed loop sum to zero — energy does not appear from nowhere. Add each part's own law (a resistor: V = IR; a capacitor: current is C·dV/dt; a diode: the Shockley exponential) and you have a system of equations. Solving that system is all any circuit simulator has ever done, from SPICE on a 1970s mainframe to this page.

The page's method is Modified Nodal Analysis: ordinary nodal analysis (write KCL at every node, solve for the node voltages) extended with one extra unknown per voltage source — the current through that source — because an ideal source's current is whatever it has to be. Nonlinear parts (the diode and LEDs) are solved the SPICE way: guess the diode voltage, replace the diode by its tangent line at that guess, solve, re-guess, repeat (Newton–Raphson), with SPICE's own pnjlim step-damping to keep the guesses from exploding. Reactive parts are stepped in time with Backward Euler: each capacitor becomes, for one timestep, a resistor of value dt/C in parallel with a current source remembering its last voltage; each inductor likewise with dt/L. The frame loop runs 16 substeps per animation frame. None of this is decoration — every piece of it is exercised, and measured, below.

KCL at node k:  Σ Ileaving k = 0 The audit's sharpest test: take the sandbox's own reported branch currents, add up what leaves each node of a multi-branch circuit, and see whether the books balance. They do — to about 10⁻¹⁰ A (see the bridge table).
Shockley diode:  I = Is·( eVd/(n·VT) − 1 ),  VT = 25.85 mV The model the page states in code: silicon diode n = 1.15, Is = 10⁻¹² A; LEDs use the same law with larger n per colour (red n = 3.10, cyan n = 4.90). The independent check solves this equation by bisection — no Newton, no leakage, no caps — so any trick the page's solver plays shows up as a difference.
Backward Euler:  C → Req = dt/C  ·  L → Req = dt/L Unconditionally stable, first-order accurate: the error is proportional to dt. That is a promise the audit can test — halve the step and the transient error should roughly halve. It does better than that here (see the RC table).

The proofs

Method, once, plainly: the solver class was lifted out of the shipped page unchanged and run in Node. Circuits were assembled as component lists in the page's own format (the divider, bridge, LED, peak-detector, RLC and RC circuits use the exact topologies and values of the shipped presets). The independent column comes from a from-scratch nodal solver and bisection diode solve written for this audit. “Sandbox value” always means a number read out of the solver's own reported node voltages or component currents — never a number computed from the formula being checked.

Bends named honestly

Backward-Euler damping. The transient method trades accuracy for unconditional stability: charging curves read up to ~0.3% low at a 1 ms step (table 4), and a lossless LC tank slowly bleeds energy it should keep forever — 8.00 V became 7.27 V after ten cycles at a 10 µs step, pure numerical damping with no resistor anywhere in the circuit. Shrink the step and both effects shrink proportionally. A trapezoidal (SPICE-style) integrator would keep the tank's energy but can ring numerically on stiff circuits; the exhibit chose the stable side of that trade.

Wires are 1 mΩ, switches are 1 mΩ / 1 GΩ, sources carry a 10 µΩ stamp. Every wire you draw is a real, tiny resistor. That is why the divider's top node reads 11.999994 V and the parallel bank reads 0.005% low — honest physics, and the reason a short across a source stops at 8,911 A instead of dividing by zero.

GMIN everywhere. Each node carries a 10⁻¹¹ S conductance to ground so floating islands can't make the matrix singular. Consequence: floating nodes read a serene 0 V (Flag 3), and a reverse-biased diode leaks 5 nA at −5 V through the diode model's own added 10⁻⁹ S leakage instead of Shockley's picoamp — five thousand times the textbook value, and still only five nanoamps. Named here because at nanoamp-scale work it would matter.

The diode's two safety rails. Above Vd = 30·nVT the exponential becomes a straight line (slope capped at 1000 S), which keeps Newton from overflowing and costs 18.6% on Vd at ~99 A (Flag 2). Below −40·nVT the current floors at −Is. Both are standard simulator self-defence; both are invisible at any current this breadboard will plausibly see.

The potentiometer is a rheostat, not a divider. The “pot” has two terminals, not three: the solver stamps a single resistance of value × wiper between its two ends. You cannot build a true three-terminal divider with it — the divider preset uses it as a variable load, which is the honest use. And the wiper is clamped to [2%, 100%]: at “0%” a 2 kΩ pot is still 40 Ω, never a short. The canvas label reports value × wiper, matching the stamp.

Source current reads negative while supplying. A source's reported current is defined from its + terminal to its − terminal through the source, so a source delivering 8 mA displays −8.00 mA where every load displays a positive number. Magnitudes are right everywhere; the sign is a convention a first-time reader will trip on.

The lamp is a fixed resistor. The incandescent lamp is stamped at its set resistance (120 Ω default) regardless of temperature — no cold-filament inrush, no resistance rising with brightness. Its glow is computed from the power it dissipates; the filament's physics is not simulated.

No transistor, no op-amp, no dependent sources. The palette ends at the diode and LED. Everything the solver does, it does correctly — but amplification is outside this sandbox's world, and circuits needing gain belong in the transistor and op-amp exhibits.
