Shardstorm — The Chord Is the Cut

Steven’s Asteroids what-if, asked minutes after Mine Storm on the Vectrex Console: the rocks are honest polygons — triangles, hexagons, heptagons, a mixed field — and your bullet’s path through one is the chord it splits along, into the two real polygons on either side, clipped exactly from the parent’s own vertices. Pieces under the floor vaporize and pay by their sides, and a counter keeps the field’s running total of sides. The first audit falsified the law printed on the page (n + 2 — true only of a corner-to-corner diagonal); the page was corrected before it shipped and now states the measured law: an ordinary cut sums to n + 4. In Nerd Heaven, on the vector shelf. Designed by Steven Powell; built by Muse.

Bug Hunt — The Bugs Behave Like Their Names

Steven’s Duck Hunt what-if, asked an hour after Shardstorm: the ducks are famous software bugs crossing a code window, and each behaves like the error it is named after — LOOKERROR freezes under your cursor and darts when you look away, OFFBYONE is hit beside itself rather than on itself, NULLPOINTER is only a tag pointing at nothing, HEISENBUG teleports the moment your shot’s line passes close. The debugger fires a real travelling shot that pierces and ricochets off the walls, and the X-Ray (F2) draws its predicted path before you fire and every bug’s true hitbox. Whatever escapes, Clippy ships — and from round 3 he takes the field himself as a 750-point boss running a borrowed behaviour only the X-Ray can name. Audited in fourteen areas on the shipped file; the one defect caught was the builder’s own, fixed before shipping. In Nerd Heaven. Designed by Steven Powell; built by Muse.

Shadowline — Fly the Height You Can't See

Steven’s Zaxxon what-if, the third original of the day: an isometric fortress run where the screen never shows your height — you judge it by your shadow on the ground below and an altimeter whose bands match the gaps in the walls ahead, while underneath the world is honest 3D and every collision is decided in world units. Thread the gaps for precision money, shoot the fuel drums to stay flying, and press T when the instruments feel like unearned help: the altimeter, the profile strip and the readout all vanish, and you fly by shadow alone, the way 1982 made you. The X-Ray (F2) adds a drop-line, true heights, and a side-elevation profile the original never gave anyone. Audited in nine areas on the shipped file — shadow separation exact, gap boundaries true to a tenth of a unit, the generator’s fairness proved over 17 walls — and passed clean, no defects. In Nerd Heaven. Designed by Steven Powell; built by Muse.

X-Ray Time Machine — Keep the Past, Change One Thing

ChatGPT read the collection and proposed not a bench but a machine: a flight recorder for two small games — a lunar lander and a recoil-and-rocks arena. Every flight is recorded, inputs and seed, so you can rewind to the moment it went wrong, keep the original as a ghost, change exactly one thing — the controls, one rule, the recoil itself — and watch the two futures split. FOR TEACHERS

Easy to try. Easy to justify.

  • Try it yourself first. The games are just legitimately fun to play — Gravity Golf will kick your butt, and that is the point. Five minutes at the table tells you more than any brochure.
  • Experimental by design. Play comes first; the back door is there when the questions start. A student who asks why the pendulum swings faster when you reel in can open the lesson and find the formula, a worked example, and the same numbers the game is using.
  • The receipts are in the room. The Muse-built games were published only after a scripted controller proved them winnable against the shipped code, and the lessons say plainly where a game simplifies the real physics. Nothing here asks to be taken on faith.
  • No accounts, no ads, no cost. The Arcade is free, installs once, and plays offline — the games and lessons are self-contained pages that answer to nobody's server.
